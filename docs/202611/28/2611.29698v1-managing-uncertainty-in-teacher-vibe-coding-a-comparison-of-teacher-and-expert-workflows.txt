Managing Uncertainty in Teacher Vibe 
Coding: A Comparison of Teacher and 
Expert Workflows
Jiaju Lin and Ellen Wenting Zou
Pennsylvania State University
Accepted for presentation at the 2027 American Educational Research Association Annual Meeting
Abstract
Vibe coding, or natural language software development, enables teachers to become creators 
of digital teaching artifacts. However, novice educators oftentimes face different barriers. This 
qualitative study examined the workflows of pre-service teachers, in-service teachers, and 
expert developers through think-aloud protocols. Findings reveal vibe coding operates through 
a loop of problem framing, development, and verification governed by uncertainty management. 
Teachers reduced uncertainty by grounding designs in authentic needs: pre-service teachers 
drew on pedagogical principles, whereas in-service teachers relied on everyday teaching 
challenges. Conversely, Experts reduced ambiguity by constraining requirements, 
decomposing artifacts into testable modules, and automating tests. These differences reveal 
complementary forms of pedagogical, contextual, and technical expertise. Future work may 
be about supporting nontechnical teachers in developing reliable and educational applications.
Introduction
Teachers are increasingly understood not only as implementers of instructional materials but 
also as designers of learning experiences and technology-rich activities (Warr & Mishra, 2021). 
Research on teachers as designers indicates that active participation in educational technology 
design can support the development of technological pedagogical knowledge, strengthen 
ownership, and improve the practicality of technology-enhanced activities in classroom contexts 
(Koehler & Mishra, 2005; Cviko et al., 2014).


Large language model–based code generation (or "vibe coding") may extend this design role 
by allowing teachers to create functional applications through natural-language interaction 
rather than conventional programming. However, removing the need to write code syntax does 
not eliminate the expertise required to develop reliable software. Research with novice and 
end-user programmers has identified an “abstraction gap” between users’ intended outcomes 
and the forms of requests that code-generating models can interpret and implement effectively 
(Liu et al., 2023). Novices also experience difficulties evaluating generated code, diagnosing 
errors, regulating their reliance on AI, and modifying initially successful solutions when 
requirements change (Prather et al., 2023; Kazemitabaar et al., 2023).
Existing research establishes important challenges in AI-assisted programming but lacking in 
explaining how teachers use emerging “vibe-coding” tools to design educational applications. 
Addressing this gap requires comparative evidence that distinguishes barriers associated with 
limited software-development expertise from design practices grounded in teachers’ 
pedagogical expertise, thereby clarifying how educational application development differs from 
general-purpose AI-assisted programming.
This qualitative comparative study therefore examines the vibe-coding workflows of pre-service 
teachers, in-service teachers, and experienced AI-assisted developers. By identifying where 
these workflows converge and diverge, the study examines how pedagogical, contextual, and 
software-development expertise shape vibe-coding practice. Specifically, it addresses the 
following research question:
How do vibe-coding workflows converge and diverge across pre-service teachers, in-
service teachers, and experienced AI-assisted developers?
The study contributes an empirically grounded account of teacher vibe coding and identifies 
practices that may inform future instructional support for nontechnical educators.
Conceptual Perspective
This study adopts a teacher-as-designer perspective, which conceptualizes teachers as active 
designers of learning experiences and technology-rich activities rather than merely as 
implementers of instructional technologies (Warr & Mishra, 2021; Cviko et al., 2014). Teacher 
design is situated in pedagogical intentions, classroom routines, learner needs, and anticipated 
conditions of implementation. Drawing additionally on Schön’s (1983) account of reflective 
practice, design is understood as an iterative conversation with an evolving situation: 


practitioners frame a problem, make provisional moves, interpret the consequences, and 
revise either the artifact or their understanding of the problem. This perspective directs attention 
to how participants frame educational needs, respond to AI-generated prototypes, and evaluate 
both technical performance and contextual appropriateness. The Replacement–Amplification
–Transformation framework (Hughes et al., 2006) is used separately as an artifact-level lens 
to characterize the educational functions represented in teacher-designed applications.
Methodology
This qualitative comparative study examined vibe-coding workflows among 15 pre-service 
teachers, 13 in-service teachers, and 10 expert developers. Both teacher groups had no 
programming background, whereas experts regularly used AI coding assistants and served as 
a benchmark for mature uncertainty-management practices.
Participants completed remote Zoom sessions including background, interviews, a 30-minute 
development task, and post-task debriefing. Teachers developed self-defined applications, 
while experts implemented clear-defined requirements generated by teachers. This design 
ensured that the experts worked on authentic educational problems and facilitated comparison 
across participant groups. Teachers used a common AI coding platform, whereas experts used 
familiar tools. All participants shared their screens and thought aloud.
Data included questionnaires, interviews, screen recordings, verbalizations, and coding traces. 
We used inductive thematic analysis to discover the patterns in participants workflows.
The teacher-built artifacts were coded using the Replacement-Amplification-Transformation 
(RAT) framework to characterize the integration of technology into the designed learning 
activities. Expert-produced artifacts were not coded separately because they were developed 
from teacher-generated requirements and were treated as matched counterparts to the 
corresponding teacher-produced artifacts. Two trained annotators independently coded each 
artifact. Inter-rater agreement exceeded 90%, and disagreements were resolved through 
discussion and consensus.
Findings
We observed pre-service teachers (PST01–PST15), in-service teachers (IST01–IST13), and 


coding experts (EXP01–EXP10) as vibe–coded educational web apps. The work tended to 
follow a loop of Problem framing, Development, Verification, under the governance of 
uncertainty management. Across participant groups, we observed different focus on the loop 
stages and uncertainty management methods. 
5.1 Problem framing stage: from teacher struggles 
to AI-negotiated requirements
Participants initiated their artifact design by framing an ambiguous problem into a concrete 
requirement. Our observation showed that it involved reflecting daily teaching struggles and 
constraints, then turning them into artifact design. Participants modified their specific 
requirements to a clearer and concrete design representation with decreased uncertainty. 
Each group differed in tasks they wanted to solve, contexts they cared about, and their 
perceptions of the capability and constraints of vibe-coding.
Teachers started from a teaching struggle, while participant groups’ understanding of 
struggle varies. RAT analysis further revealed different uses of technology. In-service 
teachers primarily produced replacement artifacts that automated familiar routines, such as 
weekly reading checks with automated assessment, tablet-based worksheets, and tools 
fitting “the lesson I already teach” (IST03; Figure 2). Pre-service teachers more often 
pursued amplification or transformation, drawing on pedagogical principles to design 
mission-based games, immediate reading assistant (PST04; Figure 3), and a collaborative 
historical modeling game (PST10; Figure 4). 
Teachers’ initial ideas of addressing teaching struggles were not always directly translated into 
the requirement towards AI. It may be shaped by beliefs about their vibe-coding abilities. PST01 
envisioned an AI tutor for every student but produced a complex multi-module course, while 
IST03’s textbook-translation idea became a multilingual course-selection page without textbook 
integration. In post-experience interview, they admitted that ‘I think that (initial design) may be 
too complex and out of the scope of vibe-coding ', ‘Since I have no idea how to build an AI 


tutor and test that.’ In other words, participants discarded the design they were uncertain about. 
As part of the vibe-coding loop, problem framing did not always precede the first round of 
prompting in a linear sequence. Instead, problem framing and artifact design were refined 
through prototype-mediated reflection towards higher certainty: prototypes materialized 
tentative solutions while eliciting more tacit knowledge about the relevant contexts and 
constraints. Many teachers reported that their requirements became concrete only after they 
saw a functioning webpage. Some (PST07, IST04) even said AI’s suggestions were beyond 
expectation.  
There were also differences in elicited tactic contexts and constraints. In-service teachers 
expanded problems toward classroom workflows: IST11 extended a student quiz to support 
before-, during-, and after-class decisions, including identifying struggling students, error 
patterns, and next instructional steps. Pre-service teachers focused more on existing feature 
improvements, such as voice and badge colors.
Experts instead translated teacher-generated ideas into clearer specifications to reduce 
uncertainty associated with black-box AI, using ambiguity checks, success criteria, and 
feasibility assessments. This process involved iterative negotiation with the AI. As EXP02 
noted, asking the AI to pose clarifying questions also helped experts refine their own 
understanding of the problem.
5.2 Development stage: micro-experimentation and 
the limits of supervision
In the development stage, participants confronted two related uncertainties: whether the AI 
was capable of implementing a particular requirement and whether the growing artifact 
remained aligned with the intended design. To address them, participants mainly used two 
strategies: testing AI’s ability by micro-experiment and supervising the artifact as it grew. 
Teachers and experts participated in both, but the balance between them differed.
Many participants, including several experts, were uncertain about AI’s capabilities in relatively 


specialized tasks, such as implementing a particular technology stack or applying curriculum 
standards. To manage this uncertainty, they often engaged in a micro-experimentation loop, 
testing the AI on a narrowly defined subtask before full-scale implementation. For example, 
PST06 conducted a focused probe of the AI’s domain knowledge: “Let’s test whether it knows 
the Grade 5 mathematics curriculum and can generate the instructional content by itself.” 
Experts used a more rigorous version . As EXP03 explained, “I will ask the AI to complete task 
one first to see whether it can build Swift apps.” This approach enabled experts to identify 
errors at an early stage and update their understanding of the AI’s limitations. 
The ability to supervise artifact construction was largely limited to experts. Although some 
teachers attempted to understand the underlying code by examining its architecture, they were 
often overwhelmed by its complexity. Experts, similarly, did not read code line by line. They 
decomposed the artifact into functional modules and tested separately. Some experts (EXP01, 
EXP03, and EXP06) defined explicit success criteria for each stage of construction during the 
problem-framing phase and oversaw them when developing. As EXP06 said, “Detailed success 
criteria make the planning phase complex but make me better understand the project.’ 
Through micro-experimentation and structured process supervision, participants developed 
an understanding of what the AI could build and what it had produced, thereby reducing 
uncertainty during the development stage.
5.3 Verification stage: functional checks versus 
pedagogical judgment
During verification, participants evaluated the quality of the completed artifact to mitigate 
potential risks in real-world use. By checking functionality, applying professional judgment, and 
using prototype-driven reflection, participants reduced uncertainty about the artifact’s future 
implementation and returned to the problem-framing stage when necessary.
Teachers checked functions lightly and judged situated quality carefully.  Teachers’ test for 
their artifacts was almost manually, which limited the coverage.  PST04: "I didn’t try all the 
wrong answers. I just checked that the first question can be moved to the next one."  By 
contrast, teachers drew sharp judgment on the pedagogical quality. IST06 rejected red 
feedback as too error-like for children, and PST12 requested slower animations and more 
encouraging responses.. In-service teachers were more sensitive about classroom 
feasibility. IST07 redefined the artifact from an in-classroom tool to an after-school work, 


driven by the concern on the instability of technologies. “ My children are young, they will 
lose patience if the latency is high“. Some participants even preferred “boring but reliable” 
traditional tools because established classroom routines with minimum uncertainty in 
implementation.
Experts used more systematic verification, like AI-generated test cases covering common 
interaction paths and code-quality review. These tests reduced implementation uncertainty by 
covering everyday user interaction paths. However, polished AI-generated code made hidden 
errors harder to detect. EXP08 complained that “Previously, I can allocate my attention to 
review poor-quality code. But now, all codes are so clean and formal... “.  Experts therefore 
focused on defining appropriate quality gates and revised these criteria when failures emerged, 
linking verification back to problem framing, which was the key method used to improve 
certainty in code quality.
Conclusion
The growing accessibility of AI code generation creates new opportunities for teachers to 
design customized educational applications without conventional programming expertise. 
However, the expertise required for successful educational vibe-coding remains poorly 
understood in existing literature. We address this gap through a qualitative comparative 
study of three participant groups, examining how technical and pedagogical expertise shape 
vibe-coding workflows. From these comparisons, we derive an initial conceptualization of the 
vibe-coding paradigm for educational application development and identify implications for 
preparing teachers to become AI-assisted educational designers.
Across groups, vibe coding followed an uncertainty-managed loop of problem framing, 
development, and verification. Pre-service teachers contributed pedagogical improvement 
and creativity, in-service teachers focused on contextual classroom knowledge, and 
developers emphasized technical strategies for requirement clarification, modularization, and 
verification. Effective educational vibe coding therefore integrates pedagogical, contextual, 
and technical expertise.
Future research should translate these workflow findings into instructional sequences and 
scaffolding strategies and evaluate whether such supports improve both the technical 
reliability and pedagogical value of teacher-built applications.
