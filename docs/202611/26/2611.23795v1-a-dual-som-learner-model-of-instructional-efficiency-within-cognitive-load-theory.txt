# A Dual-SOM Learner Model of Instructional Efficiency within Cognitive Load Theory †Spyridon Revithis 

> School of Computer Science and Engineering University of New South Wales, NSW Sydney, Australia revithiss@cse.unsw.edu.au

Abstract 

This paper presents a novel methodological framework, integrating a dual self-organising map (SOM) neural network architecture with Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), for assessing instructional efficiency within learning environments. By representing curriculum content as a directed acyclic graph of topic dependencies, the proposed model captures collective learning behaviour across two core dimensions: academic performance and subjective/introspective mental effort. During SOM training, representative data sets of successful learner trajectories are compressed into two topologically ordered, identically structured neural networks: PSOM (performance space) and ESOM (mental effort space). By pairing these behavioural maps, the model serves as a decision-support tool that enables instructors to simultaneously pinpoint structural instructional design defects, such as hidden topic prerequisites, and evaluate collective cognitive load profiles. The implementation and performance of the PSOM component (and, by extension, its symmetrical twin, ESOM) is linked to an earlier modelling study employing a SOM with identical structure and encoding semantics situated in the same domain (i.e., a learning environment). This paper combines the study of behavioural phenomena with theoretical work on human cognitive architecture via SOM neurocomputational cognitive behavioural modelling. 

Keywords : Self-Organising Maps; Neural Network Modelling; Cognitive Load; Working Memory; Instructional Design.

## 1 Introduction 

Studying how people learn and acquire knowledge has long been a focal point in education, driven by an enduring demand for the efficient dissemination and assimilation of knowledge (Sweller, 1999; Sweller, van Merriënboer, & Paas, 2019). Despite the pervasive application of ever-expanding digital technologies and continuously developing educational methods, bridging the distance between learning behaviour and instructional design remains aconstant challenge. There are numerous studies on intelligent adaptive computational systems in education based on AI methodologies, web engineering, and cloud-based platforms (e.g., Wenger, 1987; Brusilovsky, 1999; Shang, Shi, and Chen, 2001; Ben-Naim, Marcus, and Bain, 2007; Kabudi, Pappas, and Olsen, 2021; du Boulay, Mitrovic, and Yacef, 2023), demonstrating the ubiquitous need for measuring instructional efficiency. The work presented here is an extension of early research on behavioural modelling of human learners (Revithis, 2001) and its e-learning applicability prospects (Shi, Revithis, and Chen, 2002). It introduces a novel approach to assessing instructional design, based on collective (rather than individual) working memory load, within the framework of Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) (Sweller, 1988; Sweller, 1994; Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 1998; Sweller, Ayres, and Kalyuga, 2011; Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 2019; Sweller, 2025). In the next section, the characteristics of learning environments are examined in relation to human cognitive architecture, and the role of working memory load is analysed. In Section 3, a behavioural learner model based on a dual-SOM neural network is introduced and described in terms of its use as a collective cognitive load indicator. Section 4 presents key implementation details as well as evaluation aspects of the dual-SOM component, and the paper concludes by discussing operational limitations and future research directions in Section 5. ## 2 Human Learning 

Learning behaviour can be viewed as being constrained and shaped by human cognitive architecture. Because these cognitive structures dictate how information is processed and stored, instructional design largely determines the functional specifications for the acquisition of knowledge within learning environments. 

2.1 Learning Environments 

A generic learning environment consists of an instructor and a varying number of learners ,with the central goal of enabling learners to acquire predetermined domain knowledge. Traditionally, this process is facilitated through structured interactions between the learners and the instructor. By convention, a learning environment corresponds to a unit of study (such as introductory algebra), the learning content of which is delivered across a sequence of instructional sessions. Each session introduces a thematically distinct topic (for example, equation preliminaries, mathematical operations and their properties, or solving quadratic equations). The structural organisation of this learning content is formalised as a dependency graph of topics, where each topic contains a subset of the domain content and requires zero or more prerequisite topics. This prerequisite structure reflects the prior knowledge necessary to engage with subsequent material; attempting a topic before completing its prerequisites violates the dependency constraints and results in a deficit of the foundational knowledge required to assimilate new information. A learner is deemed to have successfully completed a topic upon achieving adequate performance in the corresponding topic assessment. Figure 1 illustrates an example dependency graph for hypothetical five-topic learning content. In this structure, Topic Z serves as a prerequisite for both Topic S and Topic J, while Topic Q and Topic B act as additional prerequisites for Topic J. Conversely, Topics Z, Q, and B have no prerequisite dependencies and represent valid entry points into the learning environment.

By design, each topic has a high level of content self-sufficiency, enabling learners to focus their effort on a single topic without engaging in multiple topics simultaneously. Although conceptually self-contained, topics are often not independent; many require prerequisite knowledge distributed across the curriculum. This network of content dependencies, formalised by the dependency graph, dictates a valid ordering within the learning environment and establishes the baseline structure for instructional design. Learners entering an instructional environment possess varying degrees of prior 

background knowledge . According to schema theory (Bartlett, 1932; Minsky, 1975; Bobrow and Winograd, 1977; Larkin et al., 1980; Chi, Glaser, and Rees, 1982; Mayer, 1992) knowledge is organized within long-term memory into categorical, retrieval-oriented units termed schemas, which perform lossy compression on complex information. These schematic structures play a critical role in the acquisition and application of new knowledge (Kalyuga, 2010; Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 1998), enabling working memory to interpret novel inputs against existing representations. Whether a learner's prior knowledge is adequate for a given instructional environment is typically assessed through diagnostic screening upon entry. However, diagnostic imperfections may allow individuals with an insufficient knowledge base to proceed undetected. This knowledge mismatch results either in the complete absence of required schemas or in a structural incompatibility between the learner's existing schemas and the new domain content. Typically, the learners go through timely and systematic evaluation so that the instructor can obtain qualitative information on their acquired knowledge. The overall learner’s feedback provides particularly valuable information on the effectiveness and efficiency of the instructional design of the learning environment. 

2.2 Working Memory and Cognitive Load 

The set of all valid sequences for successfully completing the topics of a learning environment E, bounded by the constraints of the dependency graph, constitutes the environment space S(E) . As the number of topics increases, the cardinality | S(E)| grows factorially; for n unconstrained topics, | S(E) | = n!. Graph-theoretically (Cormen et al., 2009), the dependency structure is formalised as a directed acyclic graph (DAG), where each topic corresponds to a node and each prerequisite dependency represents a directed edge. A valid sequence to complete all topics thus corresponds to a topological ordering of the DAG, where a directed edge from node j to node i requires node j to precede node i in the traversal. If the graph is sparse ( m ≪ n, where m is the number of edges), | S(E)| approximates (n - m)!. Conversely, in a strictly linear sequence ( A1 → A2 → A3 → A4 → ... → An ), the DAG edges completely constrain the path, yielding | S(E)| = 1. constrained space to identify an optimal traversal path, one whose topic sequence maximises knowledge acquisition. Knowledge acquisition can be impaired in any educational setting. This study adopts an empirically supported framework explaining how and why such impairment occurs, grounded in working memory capacity and cognitive load limitations (Baddeley, 2012; Miyake and Shah, 1999; Kalyuga, 2006; Baddeley and Hitch, 1974), and will be discussed in the remainder of this section. Learners receive information primarily via their audio-visual sensory system into their sensory memory and then process it while it is temporarily retained in working memory; such processing employs schema-encoded knowledge residing permanently in long-term memory (Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 1998). These fundamental elements of human cognitive architecture point to working memory as a focus of conscious mental functioning. In this architecture, the impact of cognitive load, or the associated amount and impact of mental effort on working memory (Sweller, 1994) on knowledge acquisition is substantial, as will be discussed next. Working memory capacity is subject to an intrinsic constraint, permitting only a few chunks of information to be processed simultaneously (Miller, 1956; Cowan, 2001, 2010). In this context, a ‘chunk’ denotes a discrete operational unit handled by working memory as a single element rather than an unorganized set of disparate inputs. This limitation aligns with recent empirical findings from the Time-Based Resource Sharing (TBRS) framework, which show that heavy processing demands consume the attentional resources needed to refresh working memory traces and encode item order into long-term memory (Baggini and Ricciardelli, 2025). From a CLT perspective, when continuous processing monopolises central attention, too few temporal resources remain for germane processing and schema acquisition. In addition to intrinsic capacity constraints, the schemas residing in long-term memory significantly influence working memory processing. Cognitive strain arises from the interplay between finite working memory capacity and a lack of compatible or automated long-term memory schemas (Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 1998). This excessive burden on working memory, defined as high cognitive load, severely impairs knowledge acquisition and serves as a foundational construct in CLT (Sweller, 1994). Schema-deficient incoming information disrupts processing between working memory and long-term memory due to the absence of relevant long-term memory schemas required to interpret new knowledge (schema referencing failure). Similarly, incoming information with high structural complexity can cause working memory overload. Information complexity is defined here in terms of element interactivity: the degree of semantic interdependence among constituent information elements (Sweller, 1994). In both instances, the functional consequence manifests as a virtual reduction in effective working memory capacity (Figure 2). This element-interactivity perspective aligns with relational complexity metrics, which provide a rigorous neurocomputational approach to information complexity and working memory processing capacity (Halford, Wilson, and Phillips, 1998, 2010). While absolute physical working memory capacity remains constant, effective processing capacity is virtually reduced when processing schema-deficient or highly complex information. Within the conceptual framework of CLT, three core dimensions determine the working memory demand during knowledge acquisition: intrinsic load, extraneous load, and germane processing (or germane load). Intrinsic cognitive load relates to the inherent structural complexity of the learning content, characterised by element interactivity, relative to the learner's existing schema knowledge base in long-term memory. Suboptimal instructional presentation or redundant task processing generates extraneous cognitive load and consumes working memory capacity without facilitating learning. Historically, CLT framed germane cognitive load as an independent, additive third category of load devoted directly to schema construction and automation. In more recent formulations (e.g., Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 2019; Sweller, 2025), germane load does not impose an additive load in its own right; instead, it functions by redirecting working memory resources away from extraneous activities and toward processing the intrinsic demands of the learning task. Under both perspectives, higher germane processing signifies productive mental effort that fosters schema acquisition, making the distinction between unproductive (extraneous) and productive (germane/intrinsic) effort central to evaluating instructional efficiency. ## 3 The Dual-SOM Behavioural Learner Model 

Despite the vast variability of human behaviour, domain-specific context and constraints naturally reduce this variability significantly. As will be discussed in Section 3.1, a natural reduction of the behavioural space, which occurs in domain-specific learning, is an initial working assumption in modelling learning behaviour in this study. In a typical learning environment the instructor reviews the learners’ feedback in order to determine the degree of knowledge acquisition and instructional effectiveness. Situated behaviour within a learning environment (that is, domain-specific learning behaviour) can provide the necessary empirical data to construct not only a model of learner performance but also a behavioural learner model , allowing for the assessment of instructional design and working memory load as a function of cognitive strain and performance during knowledge acquisition. The model introduced in this work is based on performance and introspective metrics in order to quantitatively characterise the learners’ behaviour and help estimate their cognitive load. The behavioural learner model, which fits the characterisation of abehavioural outcome model (Sun and Ling, 1998), can be implemented as a computational dual self-organising map (SOM) neural network, the characteristics and architecture of which will be presented next. 

3.1 Architecture and Function of the Model 

The behavioural learner model, which is described here and linked to CLT in Section 3.2, is based on the novel use of two Kohonen SOM neural networks (Kohonen, 1982, 2001) that map the learners’ behaviour within the learning environment. The first neural network, PSOM , represents the learners’ cognitive performance per topic, whereas the second one, ESOM , represents the learners’ cognitive effort per topic during the same period. Both behavioural maps are formed after PSOM and ESOM undergo training that exposes each neural network to a sizeable data set of representative past cases from learners who have successfully completed all topics. In the case of PSOM, the training set consists of numerical data on performance in topic evaluation tests, whereas, in the case of ESOM, it consists of numerically scaled introspective data on the mental effort required to successfully complete each topic (Figure 3). During the training session, each neural network is sequentially presented with performance and mental effort vectors, respectively, in order for the network to converge to a stable state according to the machine learning and termination characteristics of the SOM algorithm; a comprehensive layout of standard SOM analysis is provided by Haykin (2009). The significance and efficiency of the learners’ behavioural mapping depends on how statistically representative of the learner population the training data set is. Assuming that a form of behavioural-space reduction takes place naturally in domain-specific learning, both the performance and mental effort behavioural spaces for any learning environment are generally much smaller than the vast theoretical space of human behaviour, albeit they remain prohibitively large. However, the explosively large space of potential domain-specific learning behaviour can be compressed into a manageable set of behavioural patterns in the form of two topologically ordered behavioural maps, PSOM and ESOM, representing statistically dominant cognitive behavioural patterns for performance and mental effort, respectively. Due to the well-established properties of SOM neural networks (Haykin, 2009), it is expected that each map would be a close approximation of the domain behavioural space it compresses, provided that each map has been trained using astatistically representative subset of that behavioural space (Figure 4).

Significant space reduction of the original human behavioural space can be achieved via training a SOM network using a statistically representative data set drawn from the domain behavioural space. Upon completion of training, PSOM constitutes a topological cognitive behavioural map of the learners’ performance in each topic of the learning environment. The structure of PSOM is a two-dimensional lattice where each node (neuron) retains a vector pattern of performance. Similarly, the trained ESOM network maps the learners’ mental effort in each topic of the environment, with each node in its lattice retaining a vector pattern of mental effort. The size of each lattice is primarily a function of the number of performance (or mental effort) patterns the model designer deems appropriate for the SOM network to represent; for the proposed design, it is in the order of 100 nodes per map. Implementation of the model is discussed in Section 4. Each node in either map contains not the performance (or mental effort) of a single learner in the environment, but a performance (or mental effort) pattern; that is, it represents the centre of a cluster of similar performance (or mental effort) values for a statistically dominant group of learners. Therefore, each node represents a distinct family of characteristic learning behaviours drawn from the learners’ domain behavioural space. Due to the SOM topological property, neighbouring nodes in the map represent similar behaviours.

## 4 Implementation and Evaluation Aspects 

4.1 SOM Vector Structure 

The model consists of two artificial neural networks, PSOM and ESOM, which possess different semantic content (performance and mental effort, respectively) but an identical structure. Specifically, each neuron weight vector in either neural network: (a) encodes scores within the real-number interval [0,1], corresponding to topic performance or mental effort scores, and (b) contains a number of elements equal to the total number of topics in the learning environment (Figure 8).   

> Figure 8 : Neuron weight structure in PSOM and ESOM neural networks for a learning environment of utopics.

In terms of algorithmic analysis, if m is the cardinality (size) of the training set, n is the size of the network’s neuron lattice, and k denotes the number of iterations of the self-organising and convergence part of the SOM formation algorithm, the complexity of the algorithm (for either PSOM or ESOM) at vector-level processing is generally Θ(m·n·k). 

4.2 Validity of PSOM and ESOM 

A real-life evaluation of the proposed dual SOM model would require sufficient training data gathered from prolonged experimentation. This would involve monitoring human learners and an instructor on a predesigned learning environment, running for a significant period of time until completion, as well as adequate class repetition, each time with a cohort of new students. Beyond the obvious practical challenges (e.g., the duration of the evaluation process) involved in this approach, there is also a significant methodological challenge regarding the model’s evaluation against the behavioural trends of learners (i.e., their inclinations to follow certain sequences when completing topics in the given learning environment).

In other words, the underlying behavioural trends must be known in order to measure how accurately the model depicts them in a trained SOM. In a real learning environment, it is particularly difficult to determine such behavioural inclinations with certainty; however, this is not the case in a simulated environment, where they can be imposed by design (and thus known prior to the evaluation process). Accordingly, PSOM semantic encoding raises the question of whether the SOM neural network can feasibly detect and represent behavioural inclinations linked to hidden topic dependencies. Strong evidence indicates that this question can be answered in the affirmative based on the conclusions of an earlier investigation (Revithis, 2001), in which a SOM neural network with an identical structure to PSOM demonstrated the capacity to form statistically appropriate representations of both the training set (i.e., the domain behavioural space) and hidden topic dependencies within a five-topic learning environment. In that study, controlled computer SOM training simulations were performed using synthetic training data sets (i.e., artificial learners) containing predesigned embedded behavioural norms, reflected in distinct learner performance, followed by a systematic evaluation process. The SOM successfully depicted the predesigned behavioural norms of the learners. Revealing these norms is precisely what the PSOM component of the model is expected to accomplish, while the remaining part of the proposed model addresses the interpretation of performance (combined with mental effort depicted in ESOM) informed by CLT, as discussed in the preceding sections. The implementation and evaluation of the ESOM neural network can be grounded entirely in the identically structured PSOM. Consequently, no new challenges or unforeseen technical issues are expected to arise when modelling mental effort using a representative training set drawn from the domain behavioural space. This is because ESOM possesses, by design, an identical neural network structure and semantic encoding format to PSOM: for each topic, PSOM retains performance scores while ESOM retains mental effort introspective scores. For the same reasons, the computational feasibility of ESOM is directly analogous to that of PSOM. A trained ESOM depicts a map of introspective mental effort patterns; due to the SOM topological ordering property, similar patterns are represented by neighbouring neuron nodes in the resulting two-dimensional ESOM map. Taken together, this structural and encoding symmetry between PSOM and ESOM substantiates both computational and representational validity across the two maps. ## 5 Current and Future Considerations 

The novel model presented in this paper integrates a diverse set of cognitive and computational theories: a dual-SOM neural network is structured around an input-space encoding that enables the representation and analysis of learning behaviour in terms of working memory load within the framework of CLT. Such a synthesis entails several operational constraints and design considerations: 

§ Beyond the initial training of each SOM (PSOM, ESOM), the model requires continuous learning via SOM training sessions scheduled at regular intervals to remain responsive to behavioural and cognitive shifts in future cohorts. Applying a performance metric is both transparent to learners and well established, as topic assessment is a standard component of typical educational processes. On the other hand, supplying the model with introspective data is more challenging, as it requires learners to systematically complete self-evaluating tasks; from a learner's perspective, these tasks may appear unnecessary and relatively distant from the primary learning goal, potentially affecting the reliability of the metric. Consequently, future implementations should consider embedding introspective assessment as an integral part of the overall learning experience. 

§ As discussed in Section 3.2, the model does not rely on specific CLT assumptions to operate successfully: although designed to generate data that facilitates educational evaluations under CLT, it functions as a stand-alone learner model. However, translating the model's outputs into pedagogical action requires either human interpretation by an instructor or the development of an artificial agent designed for that purpose. 

§ The model is explicitly designed to provide the instructor of a learning environment with diagnostic insights regarding collective cognitive load and its implications for instructional design. This implies that a) there is no built-in mechanism to automatically recommend structural modifications to the learning environment, and b) any structural adjustments necessarily build upon the existing curriculum architecture; that is, the model cannot be meaningfully employed to redesign the instructional environment entirely from scratch without reference to its existing structure. The following points address a number of important and anticipated challenges, and provide additional insights as well as further support in pursuing future work: 

§ Minimisation of selection bias in training data: By design, the training data set is restricted to successful trajectories from learners who clear an initial background-knowledge screening. This isolates true pedagogical friction within the curriculum from extraneous non-academic variables (e.g., personal or external dropout reasons) that could otherwise pollute the behavioural space.
