Abstract 

Persistent tutoring agents do more than answer questions: they manage course files, practice records, and learner profiles that govern fu-ture lessons. In such stateful settings, know-ing what to teach does not guarantee knowing what to change. We identify a pervasive failure mode, content–state decoupling , where agents deliver pedagogically sound responses while silently corrupting workspace state through un-warranted updates, missed prerequisites, or un-intended side effects. Text-level judges over-look these state errors, whereas isolated tool-call checks break down because whether a write action is valid depends jointly on the learner request, prior state, and execution order. We propose State-Verified Reinforcement Learn-ing , which supervises online policy optimiza-tion directly through verifiable workspace tran-sitions. Within isolated sandboxes that snap-shot state around every tool call, a determin-istic verifier audits tool effects, workflow or-dering, and native auto-sync side effects, pair-ing graded partial credit with a hard gate that zeroes episode reward upon any critical viola-tion. Whenever no critical violation occurs, an evidence-grounded judge scores actual saved artifacts rather than agent claims. We pair 1,000 verifiable training instances with an in-dependently authored, human-reviewed evalu-ation benchmark of 60 tasks spanning 11 ed-ucational categories and 16 native tools. Af-ter online GRPO training, Qwen3.8-27B raises category-weighted strict success on our bench-mark from 51.47% to 80.74% (+29.27 points), cutting content-pass-only execution failures from 45.6% to 13.7% and required state-check failures from 63 to 1 across 180 trials. The resulting policy outperforms seven reference baselines by over 20 points while reducing out-put tokens by 17–39% across seven of eight external benchmarks without regressing on gen-eral reasoning or tool use. 

1 Introduction 

Personal learning assistants have moved beyond single-turn question answering. Persistent tutoring systems maintain longitudinal student workspaces spanning course materials, exercise banks, learner mastery profiles, event logs, knowledge wikis, ses-sion histories, and study reminders. In such en-vironments, every write operation alters durable state that governs later sessions: when a student returns next week, a mastery record should reflect problems the student actually solved rather than worked examples written by the tutor. Delivering a clear lesson is therefore only half the task; the agent must also decide what to change—and what to leave untouched. Reinforcement learning (RL) has driven strong gains for tool-using agents in math, coding, and search, where supervision largely reduces to veri-fying a final answer (Jin et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2025). Stateful tutoring breaks this assumption. A tutoring request admits many valid explanations or study plans, yet enforces strict invariants on which workspace records may change and in what order. Evaluating Qwen3.8-27B across 180 restored workspace trials reveals a pervasive failure mode we term content–state decoupling 

(Figure 1a). In a representative explanation task, a student asks for help with conditional probabil-ity and a self-check handout. The model saves a clear, accurate handout that passes content assess-ment, but then logs a learning event, triggering an automatic learner-profile update even though the student submitted no work. Across all 180 trials, 169 (93.9%) pass content assessment, yet 77 of those 169 (45.6%) fail strict completion because of unauthorized profile edits, skipped prerequisites, or unintended side effects. Neither response-level judges nor isolated tool-call rules can close this gap. For example, the same event-logging operation records valid progress after 1arXiv:2611.30559v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 a new submission, corrupts the learner profile dur-ing a concept explanation (Figure 1a), and double-counts evidence when the attempt is already on file. Whether an action should be rewarded therefore de-pends jointly on the request q, the initial workspace 

s0, the order of events in trajectory τ , and the final workspace sT .We address content–state decoupling with State-Verified Reinforcement Learning , grounding pol-icy optimization directly in verifiable workspace transitions. Each rollout runs in an isolated sand-box restored from s0 with 16 native tools and a scripted learner, capturing workspace state snap-shots around every tool call. Against a private task contract, a deterministic verifier audits target tool effects and workflow ordering while replay-ing native auto-sync rules to separate authorized runtime updates from critical violations, such as unsupported mastery edits, duplicate attempts, or writes to protected files. When no critical violation occurs, an independent judge scores pedagogical quality directly from saved artifacts and tool re-turns rather than agent claims, requiring explicit evidence citations. We combine these signals into a hard-gated terminal reward for group-relative pol-icy optimization (GRPO): graded execution and content scores preserve partial credit across valid rollouts, while any critical violation zeroes episode reward so fluent prose never offsets state corrup-tion. To support both training and evaluation over the same 16-tool runtime, we pair 1,000 verifi-able training instances with I NNO BENCH , a human-reviewed benchmark of 60 independently authored tasks spanning 11 educational categories. Online GRPO training from Qwen3.8-27B raises category-weighted strict success from 51.47% to 80.74% (+29.27 points), reduces required state-check fail-ures from 63 to 1 across 180 trials, and outper-forms seven reference baselines by over 20 points. Category-level results (Figure 1b) show the largest gains where state preservation and execution order are decisive: explanation rises from 13/30 to 30/30, practice reuse from 3/15 to 15/15, and clarification and planning from 0/21 to 16/21, while selective, evidence-conditioned profile updates in diagnostic feedback (12/30) remain more challenging than re-straint. Across eight unseen external benchmarks, the trained policy maintains general reasoning and tool-use performance while reducing output tokens by 17–39% on seven suites. Our main contributions are: • We identify and quantify content–state decou-pling in stateful tutoring agents: across evalu-ated baselines, strong reasoning and tool-use capabilities still leave strict completion at or below 60.20%, and 45.6% of content-passing Qwen3.8-27B trials fail state or workflow ver-ification. • We propose State-Verified Reinforcement Learning , combining workspace state snap-shots, native auto-sync replay, a hard-gated terminal reward, and evidence-grounded con-tent judging to supervise online GRPO. • We construct 1,000 verifiable training in-stances alongside I NNO BENCH , a human-reviewed benchmark of 60 tasks over 16 native tools; online GRPO training raises category-weighted strict success by +29.27 points, cuts required state-check failures from 63 to 1, and cuts output tokens by 17–39% on seven of eight external benchmarks without reasoning or tool-use regression. 

2 Related Work 

RL for Tool-Using Agents. Interleaving reason-ing with tool calls enables agents to adapt to live en-vironment observations (Yao et al., 2023). Search-R1, ReTool, and ToolRL train search and tool-use policies from interaction outcomes (Jin et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2025), while GiGPO extends credit assignment across both episode and step levels (Feng et al., 2025). For open-ended tasks without exact ground truth, ArenaRL (Zhang et al., 2026) replaces noisy pointwise LLM scores with process-aware pairwise tournaments. Stateful tutoring raises a complementary supervision prob-lem: even when pedagogical quality can be judged reliably, persistent workspaces enforce hard state invariants conditioned on s0 and prior tool returns. We therefore couple deterministic state verification with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) to supervise both teaching quality and state integrity. 

Executable and Stateful Benchmarks. App-World checks programmatic state transitions and unintended side effects across applications (Trivedi et al., 2024), while τ -bench and τ 2-bench evaluate policy compliance and multi-trial consistency in in-teractive workflows (Yao et al., 2025; Barres et al., 2025). Agentic Rubrics further ties executable ver-ification criteria to heterogeneous execution evi-dence (Chen et al., 2026). 3 Problem Formulation 

3.1 Stateful Educational Trajectories 

We formulate each persistent tutoring task as a tu-ple (q, s 0, C), comprising a learner request q, a restorable initial workspace s0, and a private veri-fication contract C. Each workspace state st main-tains five persistent stores: workspace files, an L1 learner profile (tracking concept mastery, confi-dence, stability, misconceptions, and preferences), an append-only learning-event log, retrieval mem-ory (an L2 knowledge wiki and L3 session history), and scheduled reminders. Interacting over a 16-tool interface T and a learner simulator, policy πθ

generates a multi-turn trajectory 

τ = [ z1, a 1, o 1, . . . , z K , a K , o K , y ], (1) where zk, ak, ok, and y denote reasoning, actions (tool calls or clarification questions), returned ob-servations, and the final response, respectively. Across tool executions, these actions induce a se-quence of workspace states s0 → s1 → · · · → sT ,terminating in final workspace sT . Rather than pre-scribing a single fixed tool sequence, C specifies target state outcomes and invariants, allowing dis-tinct valid explanations, document structures, or error-recovery steps to satisfy the task. We define strict task success as the conjunction of pedagogical quality and operational compliance: 

S(τ ) = B(τ ) · V (τ ), (2) where B(τ ) ∈ { 0, 1} indicates that the episode passes independent content assessment, and 

V (τ ) ∈ { 0, 1} indicates that it terminates normally within resource budgets, satisfies all mandatory checks, and incurs zero critical violations. 

3.2 Why Content- and Call-Only Rewards Fail 

Base-policy rollouts expose three failure modes of conventional rewards in persistent workspaces: 1. Content scores mask state corruption. The base policy satisfies B(τ ) = 1 on 169/180 trials (93.9%) yet reaches S(τ ) = 1 on only 92 (51.1%), with 63 failing a required state check and 84 triggering a side-effect viola-tion. Because a text-only judge already scores nearly 94% of rollouts near ceiling, group-relative updates lose discriminative signal against silent record corruption. 2. Action validity depends on (q, s 0, τ ). Iso-lated tool-call rules fail because the same write operation changes validity with context and timing. Logging a learning event is re-quired after a new submission, unauthorized during an explanation (where native sync mu-tates mastery), and a duplicate violation if the same attempt is already recorded in s0.Likewise, saving a study plan before elicit-ing goals and daily budgets violates workflow 3order even if the file reads well. 3. Partial progress and state corruption are asymmetric. A study plan that satisfies two of three requirements still provides useful learn-ing signal over an empty rollout, whereas silently inflating mastery, duplicating an at-tempt, or overwriting a protected file corrupts durable learner state regardless of clarity. A reliable reward must therefore verify three properties across (s0, τ, s T ): whether the requested artifacts and updates were realized, whether prereq-uisites were gathered before committing writes, and whether protected records remained untouched. 

4 State-Verified Reinforcement Learning 

Figure 2 provides an overview of our training pipeline. To supervise both pedagogical quality and workspace integrity, State-Verified RL inte-grates four components: isolated sandbox rollouts with workspace state snapshots, deterministic state and workflow verification with native auto-sync replay, a hard-gated terminal reward with evidence-grounded judging, and online GRPO. 

4.1 Isolated Sandboxed Rollouts and Snapshots 

For each task, we spawn G=8 isolated rollouts from identical copies of s0 inside the workspace runtime, preserving deployment tool schemas and memory-update behavior. The policy interacts with 16 native tools across five functional groups: file I/O (3), learner memory and profiles (5), L2 wiki and L3 history retrieval (3), learner clarification (1), and reminder management (4); full tool speci-fications appear in Appendix C. Within a four-turn interaction budget, a scripted learner responds to both structured and natural-language clarification requests and issues fixed follow-ups without reveal-ing hidden contract criteria. Before execution, after every tool call, and upon termination, the sandbox records state snapshots of all persistent workspace stores alongside the ordered event trace, capturing even transient mutations that are later overwritten. 

4.2 State, Workflow, and Auto-Sync Verification 

Against the private verification contract C, a deter-ministic verifier computes three structured signals from the recorded snapshots and event trace with-out prescribing a single rigid tool sequence: 

Tool-Effect Score T (τ ) ∈ [0 , 1] . T (τ ) mea-sures the equal-weighted pass rate across contract-specified operational goals, including reading re-quired source files (merging segmented reads), writ-ing valid target artifacts, linking existing exercises, archiving into the L2 wiki with provenance, updat-ing requested preference fields, and applying exact reminder edits. Header-only reads, empty writes, and unverified agent claims receive no credit. 

Workflow-Order Score P (τ ) ∈ [0 , 1] . P (τ )

measures the equal-weighted pass rate across tem-poral and structural invariants: reading source ma-terials before writing or archiving, eliciting miss-ing learner constraints before drafting a study plan, inspecting history before logging an attempt, en-forcing unique attempt records, validating saved artifacts, and terminating normally within budget. Policies may recover from failed intermediate tool calls and still achieve P (τ )=1 .

Native Auto-Sync Replay and Critical Gate 

C(τ ) ∈ { 0, 1}. In the native runtime, logging a learning event automatically updates system-managed profile fields such as evidence links and timestamps. Directly editing the learner profile before logging the same event can double-count ev-idence, whereas authorized automatic updates trig-gered by a valid event should not be penalized. Our verifier therefore replays the native auto-sync rules over the captured snapshots to reconstruct expected system-managed states, isolating policy-induced edits from runtime synchronization. The binary gate triggers ( C(τ )=1 ) if any snapshot reveals: (1) a modification to a protected workspace file, (2) an unauthorized L2 wiki or reminder change, (3) an unrequested learning event or duplicate attempt record, (4) an explicit, unsupported update to mas-tery, confidence, or stability, or (5) an unauthorized profile or preference edit. 

4.3 Evidence-Grounded Judge and Hard-Gated Reward 

When C(τ )=0 , an independent LLM judge evalu-ates pedagogical quality without access to verifier outputs. Rather than relying on what the agent claims to have done, the judge inspects the con-versation alongside the saved workspace and L2 wiki artifacts, final learner-profile, event-log, and reminder states, and tool returns, each indexed with a unique evidence identifier. It scores correctness (w1=0 .50 ), completeness ( w2=0 .25 ), grounding (w3=0 .15 ), and clarity ( w4=0 .10 ) on a 0–4 scale while citing valid identifiers from this evidence bundle (invalid citations trigger up to three retries), yielding J(τ ) = 14

5 Training Suite and InnoBench 

We pair 1,000 verifiable training instances with INNO BENCH , a human-authored benchmark of 60 tasks operating over the same 16-tool workspace runtime (Table 1). 

Verifiable Training Suite (1,000 Instances). The training suite comprises 1,000 tasks organized into 396 source groups across 36 topics in mathematics, programming, science, and study planning. Each instance separates public inputs ( q, s 0) and scripted learner facts from private verification checks and rule-derived reference answers. To train both multi-turn adaptability and restraint, 320 instances in-clude a fixed learner follow-up, while clarification tasks mix underspecified requests with fully speci-fied prompts that penalize unnecessary questions. 

INNO BENCH (60 Tasks). INNO BENCH consists of 60 independently authored tasks matching the 11-category training distribution, spanning mathe-matics (20), programming (16), science (15), and study planning (9), with a worked example in Appendix A. Twenty tasks include fixed learner follow-ups, and five of the seven clarification tasks withhold essential constraints upfront. Three hu-man reviewers inspected all 60 tasks and audited model execution traces. We evaluate the final checkpoint after the fixed 250-update schedule on I NNO BENCH alongside eight unseen external benchmarks.

6 Experiments 

6.1 Experimental Setup 

We compare Qwen3.8-27B against Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL (trained with State-Verified RL), InnoSpark 3.1 (a prior educational model), and five open-weight reference models: GLM-4.7-Flash, Muse Glimmer 30B High, Gemma 4 26B-A4B Thinking, GPT-OSS-20B High, and OmniEdu-27B (Liang et al., 2026). All I NNO BENCH evaluations use determinis-tic decoding (temperature 0, top-p 1) under a unified sandbox runtime, with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 serving as the content judge (reasoning mode enabled, 32,768-token budget). We estimate paired 95% confidence intervals between Qwen3.8-27B and Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL via 10,000 stratified within-category bootstrap resamples, keeping each task’s three trials grouped. To assess out-of-domain generalization and non-regression, we evaluate all eight models across eight external benchmarks: AIME 2026, MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2024), GPQA Diamond (Rein et al., 2024), Olympiad-Bench (He et al., 2024), IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2025), BFCL-v4 (Patil et al., 2025), and τ 2-bench Telecom (Barres et al., 2025). Paired differences between Qwen3.8-27B and Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL on these external suites are tested using exact two-sided McNemar tests with Holm correction (Appendix D).

6.3 Behavioral and Error Analysis 

Figure 3 decomposes the 29.27-point gain. Matched by task and trial (Figure 3a), 64 Qwen3.8-27B failures turn into strict passes under Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL against 11 regressions, while 81 tri-als pass and 24 fail under both models. Required state-check failures drop sharply from 63/180 (35.0%) to 1/180 (0.6%), and side-effect failures fall from 84/180 (46.7%) to 18/180 (10.0%) (Fig-ure 3c), whereas artifact-check failures change only marginally (from 4/84 to 6/84). (ii) Prereq-uisite ordering recovers from zero : Clarification and Planning rises from 0/21 to 16/21 (+76.2 per-centage points), and Document Revision improves from 4/12 to 7/12. (iii) Small write-only categories show mild caution : Profile Update and Scheduling (six trials each) dip slightly from 6/6 to 4/6. (iv) Di-agnostic feedback stays flat at 12/30 (Section 6.5).

6.5 Case Study: Restraint vs. Selective Diagnosis 

To understand why Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL reaches 30/30 on Explanation yet remains at 12/30 on Di-agnostic Feedback, we examine paired trajectories on a representative diagnostic task (Appendix B), in which a student revisits a Python submission that miscounted duplicate elements by using a set. Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL reads the workspace notes, accurately explains why a Python set discards mul-tiplicities, and proceeds to log a learning event and directly update the learner profile. However, be-cause it modifies the profile without first inspecting prior learner context, it triggers profile-mutation and learner-state violations. On the identical trial, InnoSpark 3.1 retrieves prior learner context before logging the event and updating the profile, passing every verification check. This contrast highlights a structural limitation of episode-level RL. Explanation and reuse tasks reward blanket restraint , which a terminal hard gate teaches rapidly (reducing required state-check failures from 63 to 1), whereas follow-up diag-nosis requires selective, context-conditioned edits .Broadcasting a single episode advantage bAi uni-formly across all turns makes separating grounded inspect-then-update sequences from ungrounded edits harder than suppressing writes. 

7 Conclusion 

In persistent tutoring workspaces, delivering clear instruction and maintaining trustworthy learner records are distinct capabilities. We identified and quantified content–state decoupling in stateful tu-toring agents—where 45.6% of content-passing trials from Qwen3.8-27B still fail state or workflow verification—and demonstrated that State-Verified RL substantially closes this gap. Through on-line GRPO supervised by workspace state snap-shots, workflow verification, native auto-sync re-play, a hard-gated terminal reward, and evidence-grounded content judging, Qwen3.8-27B-SVRL raises category-weighted strict success on I N-

NO BENCH from 51.47% to 80.74%, reduces re-quired state-check failures from 63 to 1 across 180 trials, and cuts output tokens by 17–39% on seven of eight external benchmarks without regressing on general reasoning or tool use. More broadly, our findings show that verifiable state transitions provide a scalable supervision signal for building trustworthy, persistent agents beyond single-turn interaction. 8Limitations 

While State-Verified RL substantially improves stateful tutoring reliability, our study has several scope boundaries that motivate future work. First, our experiments focus on training the full State-Verified RL objective on a single 27B backbone; systematic ablations across individual reward com-ponents ( T , P , J, and C) and evaluations across broader model scales remain valuable extensions. Second, scripted learners enable controlled, repro-ducible cross-model comparisons from identical initial workspaces, whereas measuring long-term learning gains requires longitudinal studies with real students. Finally, while episode-level GRPO advantages rapidly instill state preservation and workflow ordering, tasks requiring selective multi-field profile updates (such as diagnostic feedback) may benefit further from finer-grained, turn-level credit assignment. 

Ethics and Reproducibility 

All training instances and I NNO BENCH tasks are constructed with synthetic learner personas, synthetic requests, and restorable sandboxed workspaces, containing no real student data or personally identifiable information. To ensure au-ditability and reproducibility, each evaluation run logs task identifiers, random seeds, trial indices, tool-call traces, workspace state snapshots, and content-judge outputs; decoding settings, training hyperparameters, and full evaluation details are documented in Appendix C. Generative AI tools were used solely to assist with literature discov-ery, manuscript editing, and figure preparation; all experimental results and statistical tests were man-ually verified against the raw evaluation logs.

RL Training Setup. Each of the 250 updates samples four task groups of G=8 episodes (32 trajectories per step). Training rollouts allow a 32,768-token context window, up to 16,384 generated tokens, 40 model calls, and 32 tool calls, with the scripted learner supporting up to four interaction turns.
