# ArEssays: Towards Robust Arabic Handwritten Text Understanding in Educational Settings 

Hamdy Mubarak, Anas Madkoor, Ehsaneddin Asgari, Majd Hawasly 

Qatar Computing Research Institute, Hamad Bin Khalifa University, Doha, Qatar 

{hmubarak,amadkoor,easgari,mhawasly}@hbku.edu.qa 

Abstract 

Automated understanding of handwritten Arabic student essays remains challeng-ing due to script complexity and high vari-ability in children’s writing. We intro-duce ArEssays , a large-scale dataset of au-thentic essays from primary to secondary students across Egypt, with both verba-tim transcriptions and linguistically cor-rected versions. We benchmark 34 vision-language and OCR models, including fine-tuned systems, and find large performance gaps on real student handwriting. We re-lease the dataset, training pipelines, and results to support research in Arabic OCR, document understanding, and educational NLP. 

1 Introduction 

Robust Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR) is essential for educational AI systems for grading, learning analytics, and language analysis. While Vision–Language Models (VLMs) perform well on printed and adult written English text, Arabic handwriting especially children’s evolving script remains challenging (Nacar et al., 2025; Hennara et al., 2025; Ayadi et al., 2025). This limits scal-able educational technology in Arabic speaking contexts. Arabic student handwriting combines intrinsic script complexity with developmental noise. Ara-bic is cursive and position-dependent through lig-atures and dotting ( I’jam ); children further intro-duce unstable baselines, inconsistent sizing, erratic diacritics, and irregular spacing. Classroom data also includes mobile-phone capture artifacts, varied writing tools, different paper types, and erasures. Consequently, OCR systems trained on synthetic, printed, or adult handwriting (Heakl et al., 2025b; Mahmoud et al., 2014) often fail on authentic stu-dent prose through attention drift, character substi-tutions, and linguistic hallucinations. Existing children’s Arabic HTR efforts (Alher-aki et al., 2022; Durayhim et al., 2023; Alwagdani and Jaha, 2023; Alshehri, 2025) are restricted to 

isolated character recognition under largely con-trolled acquisition conditions, with a single tran-scription target per sample. They therefore cannot characterize how modern VLMs behave on multi-paragraph essays, where ligature distortions, base-line drift, orthographic variability, and developmen-tal spelling errors interact. ArEssays introduced in this paper fills this gap. The shift to multi-paragraph, multi-grade essays exposes a methodological subtlety: when ground truth contains orthographic noise that pretrained models tend to smooth away, standard Word/Character Error Rate becomes ambiguous. A model may lower CER by correcting the student rather than transcribing faithfully. For educational applications, this distinction should be explicit, which we address through paired verbatim and 

linguistically corrected ground truth. 

Contributions. Our contributions in this work are: (I) One of the largest known Arabic children’s writ-ing collections, with grade-stratified essays from Grades 4 through 12 written under naturally oc-curring classroom conditions. (II) A dual ground-truth annotation protocol provid-ing both verbatim and linguistically corrected 

targets, enabling a clean separation of visual transcription from grammatical correction. (III) A detailed analysis of morphological and syntac-tic development, as well as error patterns, across students at different educational stages. (IV) A comprehensive benchmark of thirty-four 

models: general-purpose VLMs, document-specialized OCR engines, Arabic-specialized systems, and our own fine-tuned baselines. 1arXiv:2611.32211v1 [cs.CL] 24 Nov 2026 (V) A two-stage Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) training recipe that decouples visual specializa-tion from linguistic regularization, with public adapters at three parameter scales. (VI) A detailed error analysis correlating Charac-ter/Word Error Rates with educational stage, handwriting clarity, and visual-noise strata, sur-facing actionable findings for Arabic educa-tional AI. Code, data, and trained recipes will be released for academic use. 

2 Related Work 

Arabic Handwritten Character and Text Recog-nition. Arabic HTR has evolved from Ga-bor/SIFT/HMM pipelines (El-Hajj et al., 2005; Mohamad et al., 2009; Elzobi et al., 2012; Rabi et al., 2018) to CNN classifiers for isolated char-acters (Younis and abdullah khateeb, 2017; Alt-waijry and Al-Turaiki, 2020; Wagaa et al., 2022; Kamal et al., 2022; Attar, 2025), CNN, BiL-STM/ConvLSTM, and CTC sequence models (Eltay et al., 2020; Gader and Echi, 2022; Dahbali et al., 2024), and Transformer recognizers (Kassem et al., 2021, 2022; Momeni and BabaAli, 2023; Chan et al., 2024). End-to-end Arabic document OCR pipelines (Mosbah et al., 2024; Waly et al., 2024, 2025) and broad surveys (Lorigo and Govin-daraju, 2006; Balaha et al., 2020; Alrobah and Al-bahli, 2022; Khayati et al., 2024; Kasem et al., 2023) document this trajectory. All of these sys-tems are trained predominantly on printed, histori-cal, or adult sources, leaving authentic classroom data largely uncovered. 

Arabic Handwriting Corpora and the Children Gap. KHATT (Mahmoud et al., 2014) remains the canonical paragraph-level adult benchmark; Muharaf (Saeed et al., 2024) and HICMA (Is-mail et al., 2023) target cursive and calligraphic manuscripts; ADBase/MADBase (El-Sherif and Abdelazeem, 2007) support digit recognition. The few studies addressing children’s Arabic handwrit-ing (Alheraki et al., 2022; Durayhim et al., 2023; Alwagdani and Jaha, 2023; Alshehri, 2025; Qedear et al., 2024) are confined to isolated characters 

under controlled conditions, with a single transcrip-tion target. CER-HV (Al-Azzawi et al., 2026) fur-ther motivates human-in-the-loop curation for Ara-bic HTR. To our knowledge, no prior public bench-mark targets multi-paragraph essay-level handwrit-ing from Arabic school children across grade bands with paired verbatim and corrected ground truth, the gap that ArEssays fills. 

Vision-Language Models for Arabic Document Intelligence. End-to-end Arabic document under-standing is now dominated by multimodal LLMs and VLMs. Qalam (Bhatia et al., 2024) intro-duced an Arabic-tailored multimodal LLM; KITAB (Heakl et al., 2025b), Baseer (Hennara et al., 2025), and SARD (Nacar et al., 2025) provide benchmarks and Arabic-adapted backbones; community efforts such as Qari-OCR (Wasfy et al., 2025) and AIN (Heakl et al., 2025a) extend the Qwen-VL fam-ily with synthetic Arabic OCR data. While these systems achieve strong results on printed and adult-produced material, their robustness on authentic in-the-wild educational handwriting is largely un-characterized, the evaluation gap that ArEssays directly addresses. 

Educational NLP and Learner-Language Anal-ysis. Beyond pure recognition, our work relates to Arabic educational NLP, including the ZAE-BUC writer corpus (Habash and Palfreyman, 2022), Arabic Automated Essay Scoring systems using synthetic data and CEFR-aligned error modeling (Qwaider et al., 2025), and Arabic Grammatical Er-ror Correction methods based on noisy learner text (Alhafni and Habash, 2025). However, these ap-proaches assume clean typed transcriptions, which is unrealistic for handwritten input. Our dual-annotation framework explicitly separates verbatim transcription from linguistic correction, bridging OCR and downstream educational NLP and en-abling end-to-end handwritten Arabic AES, GEC, and learner-language analysis. 

3 Corpus Construction 

3.1 Data Collection 

To construct the corpus, we collaborated with two school teachers in Egypt who coordinated the data collection process. These teachers subsequently re-cruited eight additional teachers from five Egyptian governorates from differnt geographical regions to ensure diversity in handwriting styles, educational backgrounds, and regional writing variations. Prior to participation, parental consent was ob-tained for all participating students (Details in 8). The collection protocol was designed to preserve natural handwriting conditions while maintaining consistency across contributors. Students from grades 4 (G4) through 12 (G12) were asked to handwrite an essay on one of 15 general-domain topics. The topics were intention-2ally selected to be broad, age-appropriate, and fa-miliar to students from different educational levels in order to encourage free-form writing and natural language production. Most students contributed a single essay, typically consisting of approximately one handwritten page. To ensure authentic writing conditions, students wrote their essays in the pres-ence of their teachers without using digital devices, spelling correction systems, or AI-based writing assistants. After completion, teachers captured the essays using mobile phone cameras under naturally occurring classroom and home conditions, result-ing in realistic variation in lighting, resolution, ori-entation, and image quality. In the current release, we publicly provide 800 essays along with their associated annotations and metadata. Details of the corpus collection and post processing steps can be found in the Appendix C. 

3.2 Annotation and Text Processing 

Each essay was manually processed by four expert Arabic linguists through multiple annotation stages. • Line-Level Transcription : The handwritten es-says were first transcribed line-by-line while pre-serving the original line boundaries appearing in the image. This alignment enables research on handwritten OCR, line segmentation, and multi-modal text-image alignment. • Text Correction : A corrected version of each essay was subsequently produced by expert lin-guists. The correction process focused on fixing spelling, orthographic, and punctuation errors while preserving the student’s intended meaning and writing style as much as possible. • Sentence-Level Segmentation : In addition to the line-level transcription, linguists created a sentence-segmented version of each essay in which text was reorganized into complete se-mantic sentences rather than image-based line boundaries. This representation supports down-stream NLP tasks such as language modeling, ed-ucational assessment, OCR post-correction, and syntactic analysis. 

3.3 Metadata 

Each essay is accompanied by a set of metadata fields, including: student grade level (Grades 4–12), gender, essay title, governorate, and anonymized student and teacher information to protect privacy. 

3.4 Image Quality Assessment 

Because handwriting legibility and image quality substantially affect OCR performance, all essays were independently evaluated by two linguists us-ing three quality dimensions. The average inter-annotator agreement across the quality dimensions was approximately 80%. Disagreements were re-solved through consolidation discussions. The qual-ity measures are defined as follows: 

I. Legibility : This criterion evaluates the readabil-ity of the handwriting itself and serves as the most important quality factor. Ratings: 3-Very clear handwriting, 2-Legible with moderate effort, and 1-Difficult to read. 

II. Visual Noise : This criterion captures the presence of artifacts such as corrections, strike-throughs, decorations, smudges, background clut-ter, and additional markings. Ratings: 3-Clean image, 2-Some visual noise, and 1-Heavy visual noise. 

III. Image Quality : This criterion evaluates tech-nical image characteristics including sharpness, brightness, contrast, and resolution. Ratings: 3-High-quality image, 2-Medium quality, and 1-Low or blurry image.

4.2 Linguistic Correction (Correction OCR) 

The Correction OCR task couples visual text extrac-tion with grammatical error correction (GEC) and orthographic normalization. Given a handwritten image X, the model generates a corrected sequence 

ˆYc aligned with expert-annotated ground truth Yc,which preserves meaning while fixing grammar and orthography (Heakl et al., 2025b). The task evaluates whether models can resolve visual ambi-guities, restore missing diacritics, and normalize dialectal Egyptian Arabic into standard MSA. 

4.3 Layout Segmentation 

Document layout analysis and line level segmen-tation remain critical bottlenecks for continuous paragraph processing. In this benchmark, the ob-jective is to localize and isolate individual text lines and word bounding structures within full page unconstrained essay images. Given an im-age X ∈ RH×W ×C , models must predict bound-ing polygons or horizontal segmentation masks 

S = {s1, s 2, . . . , s m} mapping to consecutive tex-tual lines. This task is quantified using Intersection over Union (IoU) metrics. It explicitly benchmarks a model’s structural resilience against non standard-ized ruled paper lines, irregular vertical line drift, inter line word bleeding, and heavy erasures or crossings out characteristic of school age writers (Ayadi et al., 2025). 

4.4 End-to-End Paragraph Recognition 

The End-to-End Paragraph Recognition task is a full-page benchmark for educational document un-derstanding. Unlike line- or word-level settings (e.g., KHATT (Mahmoud et al., 2014)), models process entire multi-paragraph pages, requiring reading-order inference, layout understanding, and robustness to noise. Outputs are evaluated as full-page transcripts in markdown or plain text, with CER/WER computed over 100–400 words/page.

5.2 Domain-Adapted Fine-Tuned Models 

To map performance headroom, we apply a two-stage LoRA recipe to Baseer (3B) , Qwen3-VL-4B ,and Gemma-4 (27B MoE) . Projection layers of all Transformer blocks are adapted in bfloat16 under a cosine schedule. Dev-set validated checkpoints and hyperparameters are detailed in §I. 

Stage 1: Target-Domain Specialization (v1). 

Models are fine-tuned on ArEssays training split with high-capacity LoRA weights and aggressive augmentations, adapting the vision encoder to the irregular strokes, erratic spacing, and varying align-ments of child handwriting (Table 7). 

Stage 2: Curricular Regularization (v2). To mitigate catastrophic forgetting and stabilize vi-sual layouts, Stage-1 weights are merged into the base. New lower-rank adapters ( r=32 , scaled-down learning rate) are trained on a heterogeneous corpus blending adult script from KHATT (Mah-moud et al., 2014) and printed text from Books-OCR (Seemorg, 2024), reducing augmentation (0.50 → 0.20 ) to prevent unconstrained text hallu-cinations (Table 8).

8 Conclusion 

We introduced ArEssays , the first multi-paragraph essay-level corpus of authentic Arabic handwriting produced by school children, with paired verba-tim and linguistically corrected ground truth. Our benchmark of thirty-four state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models, Arabic-specialized systems, and fine-tuned baselines establishes a strong, repro-ducible foundation for Arabic educational docu-ment intelligence and exposes a clear gap between current models and the demands of authentic de-velopmental handwriting. We further introduced a two-stage LoRA recipe that explicitly decouples visual specialization from linguistic regularization, yielding the strongest results on ArEssays at three distinct parameter scales. Beyond the immediate benchmark, our findings highlight a methodological lesson for the wider community: when ground-truth annotation con-tains the very kind of noise that pretrained mod-els implicitly correct, a single error metric is in-sufficient. The dual-annotation framework and 

∆CER analysis introduced here provide a concrete blueprint that we expect to be useful in any educa-tional, dialectal, or historical-text setting where the distinction between what was written and what was meant carries real-world consequence. We release the corpus, training pipelines, evaluation harness, and full benchmark results to support follow-up work on Arabic OCR, educational NLP, and multi-modal language understanding. An extended dis-cussion of the implications of our findings for Ara-bic NLP, educational AI, and multimodal learning is provided in §J. 8Limitations 

Although the proposed corpus provides a large-scale and realistic benchmark for Arabic handwrit-ten student essays, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the dataset was collected exclusively from students in Egypt and therefore primarily reflects Egyptian educational practices, handwriting conventions, linguistic usage, and so-ciocultural writing patterns. While the corpus in-cludes students from five governorates and covers both male and female students across grades 4–12, the findings may not generalize to other Arabic-speaking countries, dialects, educational systems, demographic groups, or socioeconomic settings. Moreover, the corpus has grade imbalance (Figure 2) and gender imbalance (Figure 3). Consequently, broader conclusions regarding Arabic handwriting, OCR robustness, or educational language develop-ment should be validated through additional studies conducted under different geographical and educa-tional conditions. Second, although the essays were collected un-der naturally occurring school and home conditions using mobile phone cameras, the corpus may still not capture the full range of real-world document variability. Extremely degraded images, severe blur, truncated pages, and highly illegible hand-writing were partially filtered during corpus con-struction to ensure annotation quality and usability. Future releases may include more challenging sam-ples to support robustness evaluation under harsher visual conditions. Third, the corrected versions of the essays were manually produced by expert linguists, and while annotators were instructed to preserve students’ in-tended meaning and writing style, certain normal-ization decisions may introduce subjective bias, particularly in cases involving ambiguous spelling, punctuation, or sentence segmentation. Similarly, sentence-level segmentation in Arabic handwritten text can be inherently ambiguous due to inconsis-tent punctuation and spacing behavior in student writing. Fourth, we acknowledge potential sources of error in the corpus and analysis pipeline. Auto-matic linguistic analysis tools, including CAMeL Tools and Stanza, may introduce morphological and syntactic analysis errors, particularly for noisy handwritten student text. Furthermore, manual tran-scription, correction, and metadata annotation may contain occasional human annotation errors, es-pecially in subjective tasks such as image-quality assessment and handwriting readability evaluation. Fifth, the OCR and vision-language benchmark results reported in this study are dependent on the selected evaluation models, prompts, preprocess-ing decisions, and decoding settings. As OCR and multimodal language models continue to evolve rapidly, benchmark performance may change sub-stantially with future systems. Finally, although the corpus enables analysis of linguistic development across educational stages, the dataset is cross-sectional rather than longitudi-nal. The essays therefore represent different stu-dents at different grade levels rather than tracking the same students over time. As a result, the ob-served developmental trends should be interpreted cautiously. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work involves handwritten essays produced by school students and therefore raises important ethical and privacy considerations. Prior to par-ticipation, parental consent was obtained for all participating students (sample in Figure 13). The data collection process was coordinated through school teachers, and participation was voluntary. To protect participant privacy, all released data were anonymized. The corpus does not contain per-sonally identifying information about students, and anonymized identifiers were used for both students and teachers. Metadata associated with the released corpus is limited to non-identifying attributes such as educational grade, gender, and governorate. The essays were collected under natural educa-tional conditions in which students wrote by hand without the use of digital devices, spell checkers, or AI-based writing assistants. Teachers captured the essays using mobile phone cameras to simulate realistic classroom and home acquisition settings commonly encountered in Arabic OCR applica-tions. The handwritten essays were manually tran-scribed and corrected by expert linguists. The cor-rection process focused on fixing spelling, ortho-graphic, and punctuation errors while preserving the students’ intended meaning and writing style as much as possible. Because educational writing may contain developmental language patterns and non-standard forms, annotators were instructed to avoid excessive rewriting or stylistic normalization. J Extended Discussion and Implications 

Our benchmark surfaces three findings whose im-plications extend beyond Arabic Handwritten Text Recognition. This appendix expands on each of them, referenced from the Conclusion in the main body. 

Implications for Arabic NLP. On authentic chil-dren’s handwriting, broad multimodal scaling alone is not sufficient: the largest non-adapted Vision-Language Models still trail dedicated fine-tuned 3B-scale models by a substantial margin. Targeted, in-domain data dominates parameter count in this regime, echoing observations in low-resource Ara-bic NLP more broadly (Nacar et al., 2025; Hennara et al., 2025). The negative ∆CER exhibited by large general-purpose VLMs further reveals that they conflate visual transcription with implicit lin-guistic correction, silently smoothing developmen-tal orthographic errors towards Modern Standard Arabic. While this behaviour is valuable for down-stream language understanding, it is a hazard for any system whose ground truth is meant to be the 

actually written script, the precise setting our dual-annotation protocol is designed to disentangle. 

Implications for educational AI. Automated ed-ucational technology in Arabic-speaking contexts cannot be built on transcripts that silently correct what students wrote, since these are exactly the sig-nals teachers and learning-analytics systems need to track developmental progress. Our results show that a separation between verbatim and linguis-tically corrected targets is essential for building trustworthy formative-assessment pipelines: ver-batim transcription should drive error analysis and feedback, while corrected transcription supports downstream NLU. The clarity-×-grade stratifica-tion in §6 further indicates that handwriting clarity, not grade level alone, is the principal bottleneck, so investments in image capture, paper standardiza-tion, and lightweight pre-processing in classrooms are likely to yield larger gains than further raw model scaling. 

Implications for multimodal learning. The two-stage LoRA curriculum, Stage 1 visual specializa-tion followed by Stage 2 curricular regularization on KHATT and printed text, yields the strongest models on ArEssays and recovers performance across grade and clarity strata. The explicit de-coupling of visual specialization from linguistic regularization provides a transferable recipe for any multimodal task that combines a narrow visual distribution with a broad linguistic prior. We hy-pothesize the same recipe will be effective in adja-cent settings such as historical Arabic manuscripts, low-resource cursive scripts (Persian, Urdu), and child-produced texts in other languages, and we release our training code, prompts, and adapters to facilitate this line of work. 16 Model Model-specific setting
