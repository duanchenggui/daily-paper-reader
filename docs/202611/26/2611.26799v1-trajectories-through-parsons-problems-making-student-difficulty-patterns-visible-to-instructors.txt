Background and Context: Existing Parsons problem tools primarily focus on correctness, indicating whether a student solved a problem, but providing limited visibility into the underlying problem-solving process. Without that visibility, instructors cannot see which blocks caused repeated confusion or which distractors were consistently selected. 

Objective: We introduce Pulla, a Parsons problem tool that instruments programming assignments to capture fine-grained interaction data. We examine what these behavioral traces make visible to instructors, namely how long students take on a problem and how many moves they make, which recurring errors the distractors expose and how the class arrives at them, whether those errors follow a student from one problem to the next, and which students complete the whole assignment without a single error. 

Method: Pulla records every block arrangement a student submits together with its per-block feedback, and presents per-student attempt histories, activity logs, and AI-assisted difficulty reports to instructors. After a pilot in an upper-division software design course at the University of Houston (United States), we deployed it in an online, self-paced introductory Python course at Aalto University (Finland), where 176 students worked on three exception-handling Parsons problems, and analyzed the resulting traces. 

Findings: The distractors exposed four recurring errors: misidentifying exception types, returning a value instead of raising an exception, guarding the wrong condition, and placing correct blocks in the wrong order. On the hardest problem 89% of the students who submitted a wrong block chose the same incorrect guard, and the most common trajectory passed through a failed submission before reaching the correct arrangement. Errors carried over between problems, with a student who made one on the first problem 1.8 to 2.5 times as likely to make it again later, depending on the error. Students who failed before solving a problem spent two to three times longer on it than those who solved it immediately, while those who never solved it spent as little time as the latter. Twenty-seven of the 171 students who submitted to all three problems solved every one at the first attempt, and doing so on the first problem was associated with doing so on the second but not on the third. 

Implications: Behavioral traces expose conceptual difficulties that correctness scores hide, including cases in which much of a cohort fails in the same way. We discuss what this means for designing Parsons problems and distractors.

1 Introduction 

Parsons problems are exercises in which students reconstruct a program by arranging shuffled code blocks into the correct order [Parsons and Haden 2006]. They can optionally include distractors that must be excluded [Ericson et al. 2022; Parsons and Haden 2006]. They naturally generate behavioral traces as students solve them, since each block placement, each removal and retry, and each intermediate solution state encodes conceptual decisions that a student makes during problem solving. Parsons problems are relatively small exercises designed to be completed in short bursts for logical reasoning, structural comprehension, and problem-solving without syntax friction. They can expose common mistakes in students’ problem solving and comprehension, and give instructors the evidence to address them. Parsons problems have been extended in many directions (interaction traces [Helminen et al. 2012], adaptive difficulty [Ericson et al. 2018], faded blocks [Weinman et al. 2021], and mobile accessibility [Kluthe et al. 2026]); however, we found no instructor support tool to analyze students’ log traces, leaving the problem-solving process invisible to instructors. Without visibility into which blocks caused repeated confusion and which distractors were consistently selected, instructors cannot identify which concepts deserve revisiting or which students may benefit from additional support. To address this gap, we develop Pulla by extending an existing Parsons problem tool with behavioral logging and an instructor-facing dashboard. Pulla presents per-student attempt histories and class-level difficulty patterns. The system instruments Parsons assignments to collect fine-grained interaction traces, including the complete sequence of intermediate solution states between the first block placement and final submission. These behavioral traces are designed to enable instructors to identify recurring difficulty patterns and intervene before those patterns compound into deeper misconceptions. We ran a pilot study in an upper-division Java course at the University of Houston, and later deployed it in an online, self-paced introductory Python course at Aalto University, where 176 students worked on three exception-handling Parsons problems. We address four research questions: 

RQ1 How long do students take on a problem and how many moves do they make, and how does this differ between those who solve it at the first attempt, those who solve it after failing, and those who never solve it? 

RQ2 Which distractors carry the failed submissions within each problem, and how does the class arrive at them? 

RQ3 Do difficulty patterns exhibited by a student on one problem reappear in subsequent problems? 

RQ4 How many students complete the whole assignment without a single error, and do they stay on that path from one problem to the next? This paper makes two primary contributions:

2 Related Work 2.1 Parsons Problems as a Formative Assessment Tool 

Parsons problems have been studied extensively across contexts and skill levels [Du et al. 2020]. Sev-eral platforms support their delivery at scale: the js-parsons library 1 powers web-based courses and was used by Helminen et al. [2012] to capture interaction traces in Python problems; Rune-stone [Ericson and Miller 2020] embeds them in interactive textbooks with adaptive scaffolding; Epplets [Kumar 2018] offers credit based on actions taken to reach a solution; and MobilePar-sons [Karavirta et al. 2012] extends the format to iOS and Android. A recurring design choice is the use of distractors. Harms et al. [2016] and Smith et al. [2023] found they increase time-on-task by 14–50% and reduce success rates, so distractor presence shapes the solving process. More recently, CodeTailor [Hou et al. 2024] uses an LLM to generate personalized puzzles from a student’s own incorrect submission. The format has also been extended to support adaptive difficulty [Ericson et al. 2018], faded blocks [Weinman et al. 2021], and accessible mobile delivery [Kluthe et al. 2026]. Despite these advances, the platforms we reviewed merely report correctness and attempt counts but provide no built-in mechanism for instructors to diagnose how students arrive at their solutions, such as which distractors they select, how often they backtrack, and what difficulties those choices reveal. What these platforms leave unrecorded is the sequence of intermediate arrangements between a student’s first block placement and their final submission. 

2.2 Process Data and Instructor-Facing Feedback 

Helminen et al. [2012] demonstrated the diagnostic value of interaction traces in Parsons problems, revealing patterns such as backtracking and block cycling that correctness scores cannot expose. Price et al. [2017] extended this to block-based programming through iSnap and later proposed ProgSnap2 [Price et al. 2020] as a cross-institutional standard for recording programming events from first interaction to final submission. Benedetti et al. [2026] show what is lost when that record is missing. Analyzing written student submissions on algorithmic tasks, they find that when submissions lack explicit solution steps, the reasoning process that produced them remains opaque to analysis. Process data has also been used to choose what to show to a student. Karavirta et al. [2012] offered an optional hint that recalls an earlier visit to the same arrangement and what followed it; Maharjan and Kumar [2020] cluster trails of edit distance to the solution and propose feedback derived from them, without implementing or evaluating it; Kumar [2023] highlights the next incorrect line once the first complete arrangement has been submitted; and Tithi et al. [2025] derive chunks from traces and pair them with stepwise hints and self-explanation prompts, which improved accuracy on the targeted rules but left the overall posttest score unchanged. Using the history of a session is therefore not new in itself. What varies little across this work is who receives the result, since in these systems it is the student. Among the Parsons studies we reviewed, the only one that names the instructor as a recipient is a two-page abstract in which the tool collects comments written by students rather than a diagnosis derived from their actions [Moloughney 2026].

Process data has therefore served as a research instrument and as a source of feedback for students, but not as much as a resource for instructors, and our tool addresses it by exposing each student’s complete block-level interaction history within the instructor dashboard. 

2.3 AI-Assisted Analysis of Student Work 

Phung et al. [2025] found that expert rubric ratings of AI-generated programming hints tend to overestimate student-perceived usefulness, with most mismatches arising when experts rated a hint as high-quality but students found it unhelpful. A subsequent deployment by the same group [Phung et al. 2026] integrated AI-generated hints with an escalation mechanism allowing students to request human instructor feedback when AI support fell short; their results showed that instructor feedback was incorrect or insufficient roughly half the time, even among instructors with domain expertise. Sölch and Krusche [2026] integrated LLM-based assessment into a production learning management system, generating rubric-aligned feedback on student UML submissions for instructor review before delivery. Recent work has shown that students rate AI-generated lecture slides comparably to human-authored ones and cannot reliably identify which slides are AI-generated [Leinonen et al. 2026]. These approaches take either a student artifact (code, UML) or course content as input and process it without access to the student’s problem-solving process. Our approach instead takes a behavioral trace as input and delivers its analysis to the instructor, who remains the decision-maker for any intervention. 

3 System Overview 

Pulla extends CodeCheck [Horstmann 2026], an open-source web-based programming assessment platform, with a behavioral instrumentation layer designed to capture students’ problem-solving processes while working on Parsons problems. Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow of the tool. Rather than recording only final submission outcomes, the tool captures fine-grained interaction events (Table 1) associated with each submission attempt, alongside the per-block feedback returned at each check. These events are persisted for subsequent analysis and together form a behavioral trace, a chronological record of the decisions students make before arriving at a final submission. These intermediate states remain invisible in on-demand feedback systems [Helminen et al. 2012]. To support instructional decision-making, the tool provides a web-based instructor dashboard that transforms these traces into actionable insights. Instructors can inspect per-student attempt histories and activity logs (panels G–H), optionally trigger AI-assisted analysis of problem-solving difficulties for individual students (panel I), and generate a class-level summary of common difficulty patterns as a slide deck that the instructor can present in the browser or download as PowerPoint (panel J). 

3.1 Design Rationale and Implementation 

3.1.1 Client-Side Event Capture. The tool captures interaction events at the browser level. This design decision is what makes the intermediate states visible, since block placements, removals, and retries are recorded as they happen (Table 1), before the student decides to submit. Server-side instrumentation would capture only the final arrangement at each submission, losing the within-attempt behavior that is most diagnostic of conceptual difficulty.

3.1.3 AI Analysis Pipeline. When an instructor triggers the analysis for a student, the system serializes the student’s complete attempt history for the selected assignment, representing each submission as an ordered sequence of block identifiers paired with their per-block color-coded feedback (green, yellow, or red, as described in Section 3.2). This structured trace is passed to GPT-4o via the OpenAI API, with a prompt that instructs the model to identify recurring conceptual difficulties evident in the pattern of incorrect placements and distractor selections across attempts. The model returns a short list of labeled difficulty patterns, each with a brief natural-language explanation, displayed to the instructor as the difficulty report (panel I, Figure 1). For class-level slides (panel J), a separate two-stage pipeline first extracts a small set of recurring difficulties from a sample of at most twenty student traces, then classifies every student’s attempt history against that shared list, and the six difficulties that most students were mapped to become the deck. Both analyses run on demand. The per-student report is requested from the Student Profile View and the class-level pipeline from the submissions page, and the output of either is stored for the instructor rather than delivered to the student. The same model was used in the pilot and in the main deployment. Section 6.1 reports how the instructors experienced these features in practice.

4 Method 4.1 Study Design 

We evaluated Pulla in two stages (Table 2). A pilot in an upper-division software design course at the University of Houston tested the instrumentation, the instructor dashboard, and an initial set of exception-handling problems in classroom use. The main deployment, in an introductory programming course at Aalto University, is the source of all results in Section 5. We report the pilot to document how the tool and the problems were developed, not as a second cohort for comparison, since the two courses differ in programming language, course level, student population, and instructional format. 

4.2 Pilot Study 

The pilot ran during Spring 2026 in an upper-division Java course on software design at the University of Houston, a large public research university in the United States. Sixty-eight students submitted at least one arrangement, producing 560 submissions. Students solved the problems through the institution’s LMS using university credentials, which allowed traces to be linked across assignments; student identifiers were pseudonymized before analysis. The course used Parsons problems on several topics, including design patterns; exception handling was the one topic it shared with the curriculum of the main deployment. 4.3 Main Deployment 

The main deployment took place in an introductory Python course for non-CS majors at Aalto University in Finland, delivered online and self-paced. Students opened the problems through a direct link without institutional authentication, and the platform assigned each of them a randomly generated pseudonym, so no personal identifiers were recorded. The pseudonym is stable across the three problems, so a student’s work can be followed from one to the next without being identifiable, and 179 of the 182 students who placed a block did so on all three problems. This is what makes the comparisons across problems in Sections 5.3 and 5.4 possible. We analyzed all activity on the exception-handling assignment during Summer 2026, in which 194 students opened the assignment, 182 placed at least one block, and 176 submitted at least one arrangement. Together they produced 819 submitted arrangements and 3,675 block placements and removals, a mean of 20 block moves per student across the three problems.

4.4 Materials 

The assignment comprised three Parsons problems on exception handling (Table 4). In each, students arranged blocks into a Python function that signals an error condition correctly. Every distractor corresponds to one of three errors: returning a value instead of raising an exception, raising an imprecise or wrong exception type, or checking the wrong condition (Table 5). Problems were presented in order (P1–P3), but students could move on without solving the current one. 

4.5 Measures and Analysis 

We reconstructed each student’s solving session from the event logs (Table 1) and the submission snapshots. For each problem, we report the number of students who submitted at least one ar-rangement ( 𝑛 ), the mean number of submitted arrangements, the percentage of students whose first submission was correct, and the percentage of submitted arrangements containing at least one distractor. Error categories are computed over the students with at least one such arrangement (Err. 𝑛 ), where a student counts toward a category if any of their submissions contained a distractor of that category, so categories can overlap. exception type; return instead of raise covers distractors that return a value; wrong condition covers a guard that does not delimit the error case correctly; and wrong order covers correct blocks submitted in the wrong position. We report the wrong condition separately rather than folding it into either neighboring category. The block raises the same exception as the correct one, so it is not a confusion about which exception to raise, and it is a distractor rather than a correct block out of place, so it is not an ordering error. Only P3 includes such a distractor. A revisit is a submission identical to an earlier submission by the same student on the same problem. To summarize trajectories at the cohort level, we built one first-order Markov model per problem from all students who placed at least one block (179 in P3), following Kumar [2023], who analyzes Parsons puzzle solving with a first-order Markov transition matrix over the lines a student acts on, bounded by dummy start and end states. Our states describe the whole solution area rather than a single line. A state is the arrangement in the solution area after one event, or a failed or passed submission, and the model is bounded by a non-emitting start state and an absorbing end state. Because it is computed from estimated rather than known probabilities, 𝐻 (𝑠 ) is biased downward at states with few visits, and the map therefore reports the visit count 𝑛 next to 𝐻 

for every state. Entropy over transition probabilities has been used in the same way to characterize programming behavior from log data [Mai et al. 2023]. The map draws a transition as a solid arrow when its probability is at least 15%, and as a dashed arrow when it is below 15% but occurred at least 10 times. A state is drawn if it accounts for at least 3% of all transitions, was visited at least 10 times, or is the target of an arrow that meets either criterion. The predictable path consists of the states reachable from the start through solid arrows only; all other states, and the arrows that touch them, are drawn in grey. A step is predictable when at least 𝜏 = 15% of the students in that state took it. Timing comes from the same event log. Time on a problem is the sum of the intervals between consecutive events in that student’s session, discarding any interval longer than ten minutes as time spent away from the tool; the time between moves is the median of those intervals. Because sessions are short and a few are very long, we report medians and quartiles alongside means and sample standard deviations. On each problem we group students into those whose first submission was correct, those who failed at least once and then solved the problem, and those who never solved it. Comparisons between two groups of students are reported as a risk ratio, the percentage in one group divided by the percentage in the other, with Fisher’s exact test rather than a 𝜒 2 test because some expected cell counts fall below five. Where a family of related tests is run, one per error category, we report both the raw 𝑝 and the value after Holm’s correction across that family. Confidence intervals on risk ratios use the log method. Where we compare one such comparison

against another, we use a permutation test with 20,000 relabelings [Ernst 2004], which keeps each student’s two outcomes together. 

4.6 Ethics 

The data collection followed the ethical guidelines of the university where the data was collected, which do not require a separate ethics review when the data are anonymous. In the main deployment students were identified only by the pseudonym the platform generates, so no personal identifiers were recorded; in the pilot, identifiers were pseudonymized before analysis. 

5 Results 5.1 RQ1: How Students Work Through a Problem 

The event log records when every block was placed and removed. Every placement and removal carries a timestamp (panel B, Figure 2), so the time a student spent between one move and the next can be measured. On each problem, we divided the students into three groups. The first group submitted a correct arrangement on the first attempt, the second failed at least once before solving the problem, and the third never solved it. Table 6 reports how long each group spent, how much time passed between their moves, and how many blocks they placed and removed. Students who failed before solving the problem spent the longest on it, between two and three times the median of those who solved it immediately. Students who never solved it, however, spent almost as little time as those who solved it on the first attempt, with medians of 18 seconds against 15 on P3, 41 against 44 on P2, and 48 against 29 on P1. Students who never solved the problem acted fastest, every 2.3 to 2.8 seconds, and removed almost nothing, a median of one removal on P1 and none on P2 and P3. Those who solved the problem at the first attempt were the slowest between moves, at 3.8 to 6.7 seconds, with a median of zero removals. The long sessions belong to the students who failed before solving the problem, who placed five to seven blocks where the solution needs three or four, and removed two or three. They therefore look like productive struggle, in the sense Warshauer [2015] gives the term, although the traces cannot show whether the struggle led to learning. Students who solved at the first attempt placed the minimum number of blocks the solution requires and removed none, but took the longest over each move, between 3.8 and 6.7 seconds, which is consistent with thinking before acting. Students who never solved the problem built sessions of a similar size, three or four blocks with almost no removals, but moved through them fastest of all. Their sessions are short because they stop early, not because they go straight to the answer.

5.3 RQ3: Common Difficulty Patterns Across Problems 

Three of the four errors of Table 7 appear in all three problems and can be followed across them: choosing the wrong exception type, returning a value instead of raising an exception, and placing correct blocks in the wrong position. The fourth, the wrong condition, is left out because only P3 offers a guard distractor. We follow the 171 students who submitted on all three problems, identified by the pseudonym the platform assigns them, which is the same across the three, and count a student as making an error on a problem if at least one of their submissions there contained a distractor of that kind. The question is whether making an error on the first problem says anything about the problems that follow. We therefore divided the students according to what they did on P1 and compared how often each group made the same error on P2 or P3 (Table 9). The comparison group consists of students who did not make that error on P1 but had the same two later opportunities to make it. Of the 27 students who chose the wrong exception type on P1, 63% chose it again later, against 35% of the 144 who had not (risk ratio 1.78, 𝑝 = 0.010 by Fisher’s exact test, 𝑝 = 0.020 after Holm correction across the three errors). Of the 16 who returned a value instead of raising, 62% did it again, against 25% (2.48, 𝑝 = 0.003, corrected 𝑝 = 0.010). Of the 9 who misplaced correct blocks, 67% did it again, against 27% (2.45, 𝑝 = 0.019, corrected 𝑝 = 0.020). All three differences remain statistically significant after correction, and none of the three confidence intervals includes equal risk. They run from 1.24 to 2.56 for the exception type, from 1.56 to 3.96 for returning a value, and from 1.45 to 4.15 for misplaced blocks. Taken together, a student who makes one of these errors on the first problem is between 1.2 and 4.2 times as likely to make it again on a later one, depending on the error and on where within each interval the true value falls. The intervals are wide because few students make each error on P1, namely 27, 16 and 9 respectively. The direction and the significance therefore hold, but the point estimates are imprecise, and the 67% reported for misplaced blocks rests on 6 students. The wrong exception type reappears mostly on P2, where 56% of the students who made it on P1 made it again against 24% of the rest, and evens out on P3, 22% against 19%. 5.4 RQ4: Students Who Never Fail 

Twenty-seven students, 16% of the 171 who submitted on all three problems, solved every problem at the first attempt. Solving the first problem without error is therefore informative about the second problem, and we cannot show that it is informative about the third. Membership of the group cannot be predicted from an earlier problem, but the traces identify it once the assignment is done, which is when an instructor would decide what to set these students next. 

6 Discussion 

The overall aim of this work was to make the problem-solving processes of the students more visible to the instructors. The tool ran first in a Java pilot and then in a Python course with 176 students, whose submissions revealed four recurring errors (Table 7) and whose event log made the trajectories behind them visible.  

> ACM Trans. Comput. Educ., Vol. 0, No. 0, Article 0. Publication date: 2026. 0:20 Prol et al.

A student who chose the wrong exception type on P1 was 1.8 times as likely to choose it again later, and the two other errors we could follow gave 2.5 and 2.4 (Table 9), so an instructor who sees one of these errors once can expect to see it again. Which later problem it reappears in depends on the problem rather than on the student, since the exception type came back mostly in P2 and returning a value instead of raising in P3, so the moment to address an error is set by what the assignment asks next. The intervention therefore belongs before the problem that will ask for the same concept again, rather than after the assignment is complete. On every problem the students who never solved it spent about as long as those who solved it at the first attempt, yet the two groups were doing opposite things. Those who never solved it moved every 2.3 to 2.8 seconds and stopped, while those who solved it at the first attempt took 3.8 to 6.7 seconds over each move and submitted a correct arrangement (Table 6). A dashboard reporting only correctness and time on task would show them as two short sessions with different outcomes and offer no reason for the difference. The two groups also call for opposite responses, since those who stopped need to be reached before they give up on the topic, while those who solved at the first attempt are the ones an instructor would extend, once the traces identify them at the end of the assignment (Section 5.4). The same traces show how cheaply a failed submission can be repaired. On P3, the most common move after a failure removed only the guard, the correct guard followed in 75% of those cases, and that arrangement passed in 97% (Figure 4). With three candidate guards in the bank and a red marker on the offending block, a student can reach the correct arrangement by elimination without ever reasoning about why the bound needs -len(items) . Per-block feedback therefore buys a fast recovery at the price of turning a conceptual question into a search, and the narrower the set of alternatives, the more that search pays off. A correct final arrangement is not by itself evidence that the concept behind it was understood, and only the trace distinguishes the two cases. Whether the students who repaired this way understood the bound afterwards is not something the traces can tell us, and isomorphic variants of the same problem [Tilanterä et al. 2025] could be one way to find out. Knowing which distractor carries the failures also feeds back into problem design. A distractor that almost nobody selects adds little, while one that carries most of the failures, such as the incorrect guard in P3, marks a concept close enough to the correct answer to be confusable. Trace data makes that distinction measurable rather than a matter of intuition, and it suggests a way to calibrate difficulty. The next version of a problem can be built around the distractors that proved confusable, retiring those that did not. It also raises a question this study cannot answer, namely what makes a distractor confusable in the first place, whether it is proximity to the correct concept, surface similarity of the code, or familiarity from earlier material.

7 Limitations 

Several limitations apply. First, all reported results come from a single course, institution, and topic, so we cannot say whether the patterns generalize to other programming courses or topics. Two of the four error categories were already the dominant failures in the upper-division Java pilot, but it differed from the main deployment in too many respects to serve as a comparison. Second, access was anonymous, so we have no information on students’ backgrounds and cannot link their traces to other course activities. Third, the course was self-paced, and the students who attempted the assignment may not represent the full enrollment. Fourth, both deployments were single assignments in single courses, so we have no evidence on how the tool behaves at a larger scale or in courses of other kinds. The method also bounds what can be observed. Traces record which block a student placed and when, not why. The same distractor choice can reflect a misconception, a guess, or a slip, and without verbal data we cannot tell them apart. Students could only reveal the errors that the distractors offered, and the three problems covered a single topic, so other misconceptions about exception handling remain unobserved. The per-block feedback also shapes what students do after a failed submission, because it marks exactly which blocks to change; the trajectories we report may therefore not transfer to tools with binary or delayed feedback. Because students worked unsupervised and at their own pace, we cannot know whether they consulted others or external resources, and long pauses between events may reflect work outside the tool. Finally, the trajectory analysis is largely descriptive. The Markov model also assumes that the next state depends only on the current one, an assumption we adopt from Kumar [2023] without testing it. A student who reaches an arrangement after several erasures may well continue differently from one who reaches the same arrangement directly. Solution states were reconstructed from interaction events and checked against the recorded submissions; in 4 of 540 trajectories the reconstruction was inconsistent with them, most likely because of work carried over from an earlier session. The thresholds of the cohort map are display decisions. Only two instructors used the dashboard, so their accounts are not evidence that it is useful to instructors in general. We show that the traces make difficulties visible to instructors, not that seeing them changed what instructors did or what students learned; demonstrating that would require comparing courses in which instructors did and did not have the dashboard. Distinguishing misconceptions from guesses would require verbal data that these traces do not contain.

8 Future Work 

Six extensions follow directly from the findings. First, replicating the deployment in other pro-gramming courses and topics would test whether these patterns generalize. Second, isomorphic variants [Tilanterä et al. 2025] would separate understanding from accidental correctness. Third, automated puzzles from recurring distractor selections [Hou et al. 2024] would target these pat-terns directly. Fourth, real-time alerts on repeated failed arrangements [Price et al. 2017] would enable earlier intervention. Fifth, an in-dashboard state-transition graph would surface cycles and regressions automatically. Lastly, analyzing the quality of the AI-generated reports about common errors systematically would be important for quality assurance.
