Abstract
While there is a plethora of AI literacy and informatics frame-
works, in practice it is difficult for designers of educational
materials and educators to decide where to start and how to use
these in learning activities. At the same time, recent reviews
show that despite the fact that most frameworks emphasize
the socio-technical nature of AI, in practice interventions tend
to center on preparing learners to use AI. To address these
issues, we ask: How can multiple stakeholders negotiate and
translate AI literacy framework ideas into common language
that can be used to design introductory learning materials for
primary school children, teachers and parents? We introduce
five entry points into AI literacy that were developed from
a computational empowerment perspective through a partic-
ipatory design process involving primary teacher educators,
curricular designers, media producers, and researchers. We
present three vignettes that illustrate how the entry points
were generative in the design of learning activities, learning
tools and media content for school and home. This paper con-
tributes an account of the participatory development of five
entry points that serve as generative and negotiable resources
for designing educational materials.
Introduction
As artificial intelligence continues being integrated into ev-
eryday life and society at large, increasing attention has been
given to young people’s AI literacy in order for them to
“to navigate the presence of AI in daily life and to develop
skills to use it ethically and creatively” (OECD and European
Union 2026). Within the AAAI community, efforts guided
by the five big ideas (Touretzky et al. 2019) have highlighted
the need to support everyone from children to older adults
in developing AI literacy (Xu et al. 2026; Kasinidou et al.
2026). However, many AI literacy efforts, particularly in re-
lation to generative AI, fundamentally lack a critical and
reflective stance towards technology (Gu and Ericson 2025).
Furthermore, many efforts center on supporting people to
learn how to use AI (Kalantzis and Cope 2025), lacking an
agenda of empowering children to understand and participate
in AI and make informed decisions about their relationships
to technology.
We build on the ideas of computational empowerment
(CE) (Iversen, Smith, and Dindler 2018) to argue that chil-
dren should go beyond learning how to use AI. Computa-

While AI literacy frameworks and broader computing ed-
ucation frameworks (e.g., the EU Informatics for All frame-
work (Caspersen et al. 2022)) are helpful in guiding the
content of AI literacy interventions, in practice it is difficult
for the designers of educational materials and educators to
decide where to start (Yang et al. 2026). As Casal-Otero et al.
(2023) show, enacting frameworks in learning activities re-
mains a challenge, as these are rarely adapted to the specific
contexts of school. Designers of learning materials indeed
can be overwhelmed in choosing among frameworks. To ad-
dress this issue, in this paper, we ask: How can multiple
stakeholders negotiate and translate AI literacy frame-
work ideas into common language that can be used to de-
sign introductory learning materials for primary school
children, teachers and parents? We introduce five entry
points into AI literacy that were developed through a par-
ticipatory design process involving diverse stakeholders, in-
cluding researchers, teacher educators, curricular designers,
and media producers. We define entry points as conceptually
grounded starting points for the design of AI literacy mate-
rials. This paper shows how these co-designed entry points
supported different actors to maintain a shared, generative
foundation while designing diverse AI literacy materials.
To illustrate how the entry points can guide AI literacy
efforts, we present three vignettes that demonstrate how we
have used them in the processes of designing learning ma-
terials, writing screenplays for a children’s TV show, and
creating learning activities for parents and children. Based
on these vignettes and our broader experiences from the

AI Literacy and Computational Empowerment
AI literacy continues to gain traction and attention from ed-
ucators and policymakers with little agreement on what it
means (Grover 2024). A recent definition from the OECD
and European Union (2026), wherein this research is con-
ducted, states:
“AI literacy represents the technical knowledge,
durable skills and future-ready attitudes required to
thrive in a world influenced by AI. It enables learners
to engage, create with, manage and shape AI, while
critically evaluating its benefits, risks and ethical im-
plications.”
Such a definition puts value on both technical and socio-
ethical aspects of AI. This is not a surprise since most AI
literacy frameworks, from the five big ideas (Touretzky et al.
2019), to the United Kingdom’s SEAME framework (Waite
and Garside 2023), to Long and Magerko’s (2020) compe-
tencies and the UNESCO framework (Miao, Shiohira, and
Lao 2024), emphasize socio-ethical aspects of AI. However,
a recent review of the literature shows that AI literacy in-
terventions centered around generative AI lack a critical and
reflective stance towards technology (Gu and Ericson 2025).
Oftentimes, such interventions also lack a technical stance
towards technology, centering on preparing young people
to use AI tools rather than to understand how AI systems
work (Kalantzis and Cope 2025). Furthermore, while literacy
frameworks are useful in guiding AI education efforts, there
is little evidence of how these are put into action. One exam-
ple of an approach that does focus on practice is Vartiainen
and Tedre (2025) who articulate a pedagogical approach to
introduce AI literacy ideas in K-12.
Whereas policymakers around the world continue to de-
bate how to prepare young people for a present and future
with AI by introducing AI literacy and to discuss which
frameworks to adopt, enacting frameworks can be a chal-
lenge. Researchers have documented that such enactment can
be difficult due to knowledge gaps between framework au-
thors and designers of learning materials and educators (Yang
et al. 2026) and lack of clear guidance that accounts for local
contexts (Casal-Otero et al. 2023; Mutawa and Sruthi 2025).
AI literacy interventions for primary school children also
face challenges as frameworks tend to emphasize concepts
that are applicable for older children, centering on middle
school and high school (Yeter, Yang, and Sturgess 2024).
Furthermore, the wide range of concepts and applications
can be overwhelming for teachers and designers to grasp and
make decisions about where to start.
Beyond the challenge of where to start, we argue that we
should critically consider what the goals of such literacy ef-
forts are and how distinct interventions support such goals.
AI literacy in the broadest sense includes important skills
and competences needed for a contemporary labor market,
including knowing how to use and operate AI systems. How-
ever, learning job skills and how to use AI cannot truly pre-
pare learners to fully participate and make decisions about
the role of AI in their lives. A computational empowerment
approach centers on how children develop understanding of
the technologies they encounter in their everyday lives, trans-
formative agency to participate in a socio-technical world,
and critical hope to imagine better futures. CE is fostered
through construction and deconstruction activities in which
learners are protagonists or the “main agents” in designing
and evaluating systems (Iversen, Smith, and Dindler 2017).
We argue that CE has a lot to offer in guiding AI literacy in-
terventions. First, CE centers on children’s lived experiences,
making connections to the everyday interactions that chil-
dren have with AI. Second, CE addresses both technical and
socio-ethical aspects of computing through the construction
and deconstruction of technology. Third, CE is expansive,
going beyond learning in the classroom. In this paper, we
describe an AI literacy project that aims to support children,
teachers and parents in learning about AI at home and in
school through learning activities, learning tools and media
content. In particular, we center on how different stakeholders
negotiated and translated ideas from frameworks into entry
points that could facilitate the use of framework concepts
in the design of introductory learning materials for primary
school children, teachers and parents.


Participatory Design Process
This paper describes an ongoing participatory design (PD)
process within the Ultra:Bytes project, from which the five
entry points have emerged. Ultra:Bytes is a multi-partner
project that aims to strengthen AI literacy among K-6 chil-
dren in Denmark. It brings together a national public-service
media company; a teacher education college supporting ed-
ucators, schools, and municipalities with teacher education;
a private foundation supporting STEM education research
and young people’s motivation in STEM; and two interdisci-
plinary research groups, one based in Northern Europe and
one in the Northeastern United States, with expertise in AI,
participatory design, and learning sciences.
The decision to organize this work through PD follows
the project’s computational empowerment orientation. CE is
rooted in PD because it connects understanding technology
with the capacity to question and shape it (Iversen, Smith,
and Dindler 2018). In this project, this principle also applies
to the professionals who design the media content, learning
technologies, and learning activities through which children
and their teachers and parents encounter AI. Following Bød-
ker (2003), participation was not treated as simply introduc-
ing these stakeholders to new AI concepts. The aim in the
project is to enable them to understand AI and AI literacy
well enough to question our shared AI conceptions and de-
cide how AI literacy frameworks should inform their own
work. We therefore considered stakeholder ownership of the
entry points necessary for them to have a meaningful frame
for developing AI literacy learning materials throughout the
project.
Before the first partner workshop, the European research
team compared the Five Big Ideas in AI (Touretzky et al.
2019), the AI literacy competencies proposed by Long and
Magerko (2020), and the 11 core topic areas of the Infor-
matics Reference Framework for School (Caspersen et al.
2022). This comparison identified conceptual overlaps and
produced a set of provisional topic areas for AI literacy in K-6
education. With a CE lens in our work, we prioritized top-
ics that connected technical and socio-ethical perspectives
and could be related to children’s everyday experiences with
AI. The European and US-based research teams then con-
solidated and refined these topics through a series of online
workshops.
At the initial partner PD workshop, the researchers pre-
sented the provisional topic areas as starting points for dis-
cussion, not as a fixed specification of what the project
should produce. Media producers, teacher educators, and
learning-technology designers examined the topics from the
perspective of their own professional practices. They consid-
ered how each topic might be expressed through narratives
and streaming content, learning tools, or facilitated learn-
ing activities for K-6 children. The discussion did more than
translate predefined AI concepts into different formats: the
stakeholders’ questions, priorities, and practical experience
also changed how the topics were worded, emphasized, and
related to one another. The first working formulations of the
entry points emerged through this exchange between the re-
searchers’ provisional topics and the stakeholders’ expertise.
These formulations were then used in subsequent workshops
and development activities. Table 1 shows how the resulting
entry points developed from this process can be traced back
to the three frameworks.
As partners used the entry points to develop media con-
tent, learning technologies, and educational activities, am-
biguities and different interpretations prompted further ne-
gotiation within the research teams and between researchers
and stakeholders. Through this process, the entry points took
their current form and came to function as flexible and com-
mon objects (Star 1989): recognizable across stakeholders,
yet adaptable to each professional practice. Because partners
could adjust rather than simply receive the entry points, it
supported coordination and shared ownership. Consequently,
the entry points became both products and means of ongoing
negotiation in the project. This became apparent in a map-
ping exercise in which different stakeholders mapped how
they adapted and adopted the entry points to use them in the
design of learning materials.
In the following sections, we first present the five entry
points in their current form and then show how they have
been used in ongoing design work. We do not argue that these
five entry points alone are sufficient to foster AI literacy or
that they constitute a universal framework. Our contribution
is an account of how these entry points, negotiated with pro-
fessional expertise and kept open to revision, can support the
collaborative development of materials for K-6 children with
an emphasis on computational empowerment. The plasticity
of the entry points is central to this role. It enables design-
ers, media professionals, researchers and teacher educators
to have a shared generative starting point to foster AI literacy.
Five Entry Points
Here we describe these five entry points, provide examples
from existing research on CE, and highlight their relationship
to existing frameworks.
Data is created
Data are representations that are created to describe phenom-
ena in the world. This entry point centers on data as an artifact
that is actively produced by humans. Children use AI sys-
tems when playing games, learning, and listening to music;
however, their interactions with data are often obscured, and
data is often framed as something that is passively collected
instead of actively created. Within CE, this entry point has
been previously explored, for example, by having learners
produce and analyze personally meaningful music data and
cocreate playlists while critically reflecting on data practices
(Liu et al. 2026). Other work has involved learners in ex-
amining the datasets of faulty AI systems (Moilanen et al.
2026). As Hardy, Dixon, and Hsi (2020) argued, engaging
learners in producing data for personally relevant purposes
can support them to develop their agency. This entry point
relates to Big Idea 3, Competencies 12 and 13, and the Data
and Information core topic of the EU’s framework.

Anthropomorphism in AI systems
Anthropomorphism refers to the tendency to attribute human
characteristics, such as empathy, intention, and understand-
ing, to AI. This occurs even though AI systems compute pre-
dictions are based on data, and cannot think or understand in
the same way humans do. Yet, recent studies show that peo-
ple increasingly perceive AI systems as human-like (Cheng
et al. 2026). Chatbot interfaces and consumer products that
target children (e.g., AI toys such as Curio) replicate decep-
tive human-like characteristics. While anthropomorphism
has not been widely addressed in CE literature, research
shows that it is pervasive in the design of interfaces for
children (Madushika Jayathilake and Ma 2026) and it has
been argued to contribute to misunderstandings and obstruct
students’ learning about AI if not addressed in learning ac-
tivities (Whyte, Kirby, and Sentance 2024). This entry point
relates to Competency 10 and the human-machine interaction
topic of the EU’s framework.
AI as a design material
AI as a design material refers to how AI systems are designed
by humans. While children engage with AI systems, these
may be perceived as autonomous, intelligent, and/or magi-
cal. We argue that learners can participate in designing AI
systems by creating and modifying them, defining what mod-
els should make predictions about, designing datasets, and
choosing parameters, as well as designing the interfaces that
people use to interact with AI. Within CE, research shows
that framing AI as a design material can support learners
to understand the possibilities and limitations of AI (Bil-
strup et al. 2022b; Kahila et al. 2026). Several studies show
that when learners participate in designing AI systems, their
perceptions shift from seeing these as autonomous to under-
standing them as human-designed artifacts (Druga and Ko
2021; Morales-Navarro et al. 2026). This entry point relates
to Competency 10 and the Digital Creativity topic of the
EU’s framework.
Intentionality in AI systems
Intentionality concerns the choices, values, and purposes that
humans embed in AI technologies through design. This en-
try point highlights that AI is therefore not neutral; rather, it
reflects the intentions and priorities from which these tech-
nologies are created. Children encounter technology at home
and in school, and adults often have opinions about it. How-
ever, the intentions and values behind how such technologies
were designed are not always clear for children and the adults
around them. CE research has involved learners in reflecting
on the values and ethical stances embedded within AI sys-
tems (Bilstrup, Kaspersen, and Petersen 2020), this work
has also highlighted how considering values and intention-
ality should be part of the process of designing AI systems
(Bilstrup et al. 2022a). Other research has engaged learners
in reflecting on the real-world consequences of such design
decisions (Hilke et al. 2026). This entry point relates to Com-
petency 10 and the Responsibility and Empowerment topic
of the EU’s framework.

The five entry points share a human-centered socio-technical
perspective on AI that highlights the role of humans as de-

Vignettes
We use narrative vignettes (Erickson 2012) to portray how
the entry points are enacted in the context of the project.
The vignettes were iteratively developed in four steps: the
authors identified focal episodes in the participatory design
process, gathered transcripts and documents of such events,
collaboratively discussed the entry points enacted, and wrote
the vignettes.
Vignette 1: Designing a Learning Tool for School
As AI literacy has not been formally introduced into Den-
mark’s K-6 curriculum, teacher educators have limited ex-
isting resources for introducing AI. In particular, a group of
educators designing learning activities for children aged 10-
12 wanted to create activities to foster understanding of how
chatbots are designed and generate outputs. They wanted
these activities to relate to the everyday experiences that
children may have with commercial chatbots. The resources
available were either too black-boxed or too complex for K-
6 students. The educators wanted a new learning tool that
could close this gap and provide some transparency into how
chatbots work. In particular, they wanted a tool that could
make transparent that data is created and that AI systems
make predictions. Using those starting points, the challenge
was addressed in a collaboration with researchers through
the design of Chat:Bytes (see Figure 2). Chat:Bytes was de-
veloped by researchers but iteratively evaluated with teacher
educators to assess how the entry points were manifested into
the design.
From a technical perspective, Chat:Bytes combines a se-
lection of classic NLP techniques (whitespace tokenisation,
TF-IDF scoring, Markov Chains) and packages them into a
chat interface that allows users to prompt and receive ex-
plainable responses. In deciding the features of the tool the
educators and researchers agreed that Chat:Bytes should be
designed to highlight how data is created and how it in-
fluences the answers that chatbots provide. Chat:Bytes was
created to use only a small dataset of around 100 sentences.
The interface made the dataset visible and explorable so that
students could directly see how training data influences the
answers of Chat:Bytes. The explorable dataset and data vi-
sualization of the predictions made by the chatbot increased
transparency and made the entry point AI systems make
predictions tangible. Furthermore, the content of the dataset
represented controversial views, for example, that cats are
better pets than dogs, to enable discussions about how the
data created for an AI system can have implications in the
outputs users encounter.
As the tool developed, the design team wondered if this
was an opportunity to support learners in engaging with AI
as a design material. This led the team to brainstorm how
the tool could support learners to modify the chatbot and
embed their own intentions. To allow students to influence
the design of Chat:Bytes, the interface was designed so that
students could add new sentences to the training data and
thereby influence the chatbot to answer differently about spe-
cific topics or add data about new topics to enable Chat:Bytes
to provide meaningful answers. By allowing students to add
additional training data to Chat:Bytes, it also enabled them
to embed their own intentions. For example, in a design
meeting, Thomas, a teacher educator, showed how he could
change the intention of the chatbot by adding training data
that included negative opinions about cats and positive opin-
ions about dogs. In this vignette, we showed how working
together teacher educators and researchers used the entry
points as starting points to design a tool and learning activi-
ties around it.
Vignette 2: Creating Media Content for the Home
The Danish Broadcasting Corporation (DR), a partner in
the project, decided to create 12 8-minute episodes of a TV
show targeting children ages 7 to 11 called Bablo. This is a
fictional live-action TV show that happens in a library where
children encounter problems and go on adventures. Bablo
is not explicitly about AI, each episode has a hidden “AI
message” such that it can be used in classrooms to discuss
that message.
A managing editor at the media company used the en-
try points, for instance anthropomorphism in AI systems,


and data is created, to draft a set of messages about AI
that were discussed and iterated together with researchers.
The screenwriters then wrote initial drafts for possible sto-
rylines. These storylines were also reviewed by researchers
who provided feedback before the screenwriters wrote them
into full-fledged screenplays for the episodes. Below we pro-
vide examples from two screenplays to demonstrate how the
entry points supported the writing process.
In the first example episode, the idea was to provide chil-
dren with a story about anthropomorphism. In this episode
Malini is playing with Sally’s stuffed toy, LotteAxel. Lot-
teAxel is described by the children as being upset by this.
At the end of the episode, Sally admits that it may be her
feelings, and not LotteAxel’s, that are getting hurt. In the
episode, Mailini gets very concerned. Sally then explains
that “It’s just a game [...] LotteAxel doesn’t actually have
feelings, you know. She’s just a stuffed animal.” Sally is sur-
prised by this, as Mailini always talked about the stuffed toy
as if it were a human with feelings. The episode was written
to convey that humans have a tendency to anthropomor-
phize non-humans by giving them human traits. It highlights
how Sally attributes human feelings to the toy to serve as
a conversation starter in the classroom about how humans
anthropomorphize toys and, with the guidance of teachers,
make connections to how humans give human-like charac-
teristics to AI systems.
In a second example episode, the producer, researchers and
screenplay writers agreed to highlight how data is created
and how, in creating datasets, designers embed their own
intentionality. In this episode Bo and Sally are very bored
when Sally’s bigger sister, Anna, brings an old gumball ma-
chine that is full of game suggestions written on post-its.
However, Bo and Sally do not enjoy the games because the
machine is filled up with Anna’s favorite games. At the end
of the episode, Bo and Sally realize they can add their own
post-it notes to the gum machine. This show, without explic-
itly addressing AI, was designed to be used in the classroom
to reflect on how data is created.
Vignette 3: Designing a Recommendation System
Learning Activity Bridging School and Home
contexts
At the same time as the broadcasting company screenwrit-
ers were writing the episodes described above, the educa-
tors started creating learning activities that could be used
at school and at home. By using the data is created, and
AI systems make predictions entry points, the educators
came up with an idea about an activity around an unfair
machine, where the output from the machine would favor
specific users. They explained that with such a machine,
children could reflect on how the data used by the machine
was created and how data influenced the suggestions made by
the system. The educators developed this idea into an activity
where lower elementary school children would pick a game
from a physical box in the classroom, which only contained
games that were mainly played when their grandparents were
children. In the activity the children then had to redesign the
game into something they would like to play. The educators
brought this idea to a workshop with researchers; there, dif-
ferent stakeholders discussed the potential of bridging this
idea with the episode from the media company described in
Vignette 2, as these both involved recommendation systems.
In this meeting the educators and researchers brainstormed
that a food recommendation system could potentially be used
as an activity that could bridge the school and home contexts.
Researchers then built a web app that showed how recom-
mendation systems make predictions based on existing data.
The app was designed to recommend food and games using
an initial dataset skewed towards adults’ ideas of what good
games and food are. It was designed so that children and their
parents could add new food and game ideas to the dataset and
delete data points. They could also add the same idea several
times to the dataset to increase the probability of getting a
certain food or game recommended.
The analogue activity and the web app were designed to
bridge what children learn at school and at home. The plan is
that children will first use the analogue version of the activity
at school, then transition to the web app to modify the data
to their own interests. Then they can bring it home and show
what they have made to their parents and potentially trick
them with a recommendation system they have designed with
their own data and intentions. This vignette illustrates how
diverse stakeholders used the entry points to design activities
that complement each other and that can bridge what happens
at school and at home.
Discussion
In this paper, we asked: How can multiple stakeholders
negotiate and translate AI literacy framework ideas into
common language that can be used to design introductory
learning materials for primary school children, teachers,
and parents? Our experience suggests that one way of ad-
dressing this challenge is through the development of entry
points. We reported on how the conceptually grounded entry
points became generative starting points developed through
a participatory process that translated ideas from existing AI
literacy and informatics frameworks into shared reference
points for the design of educational materials.
Despite the fact that there are numerous frameworks to
guide AI literacy efforts, educators and designers often strug-
gle to translate these frameworks into practice (Yang et al.
2026). The five entry points suggested in this project provided
a shared vocabulary that teacher educators, media producers,
curricular designers, and researchers could use in their de-
sign processes. Rather than prescribing learning objectives,
the entry points enabled stakeholders to develop different
manifestations of AI literacy ideas across learning tools, ed-
ucational activities, and media content. Our three vignettes
illustrate this generativity: the same entry points informed
the design of an explainable chatbot, narratives for children’s
television, and recommendation-system activities connect-
ing school and home.
Translating Frameworks
The need for entry points arises from a distinction between
specifying what matters for AI literacy and supporting ed-
ucational designers in deciding where and how to begin.


Existing frameworks provide rich accounts of competencies,
concepts, and areas of knowledge. Yet, as discussed earlier,
the frameworks themselves do not resolve the problem of
applying them. Frameworks may remain difficult to translate
into concrete educational practice because of knowledge gaps
between framework authors, designers, and teacher trainers,
insufficient guidance for local contexts, and the breadth and
complexity of the concepts involved.
Entry points address the translation of AI frameworks into
the process of designing new educational materials. They do
not replace the AI frameworks, reduce them to a new tax-
onomy, nor provide another comprehensive account of what
children should learn about AI. Instead, they offer a layer of
translation between frameworks and actual design practices.
We made this distinction visible in Table 1, where each entry
point can be traced to Touretzky et al.’s (2019) big ideas,
Long and Magerko’s (2020) framework and the EU’s Infor-
matics Reference Framework (Caspersen et al. 2023). Entry
points therefore represent a deliberate reduction in compre-
hensiveness in exchange for generativity and actionability.
They make selected ideas available as starting points around
which educational materials can be imagined, discussed, de-
signed and ultimately implemented.
This translational function was particularly important from
our outset of computational empowerment (CE). As argued
earlier, computational empowerment shifts attention from
learning to use AI towards supporting children to understand,
question, construct, deconstruct, and ultimately participate in
shaping contemporary technologies.
The entry points preserve this CE orientation in a form that
can inform design. Data is created directs attention towards
human practices involved in producing representations of the
world; AI systems make predictions foregrounds the proba-
bilistic rather than deterministic character of AI outputs; an-
thropomorphism in AI systems opens questions about how
interfaces shape human understandings of machine agency;
AI as a design material positions AI as something that hu-
mans can construct and modify; and intentionality in AI
systems foregrounds the values, choices, and purposes em-
bedded through design.
A Participatory Approach
The entry points cannot be separated from the process
through which they were produced. These were not derived
by researchers from existing frameworks and subsequently
handed to practitioners for implementation. Although re-
searchers initially compared existing AI frameworks and rec-
ommendations and identified provisional topic areas, these
topics were explicitly introduced into the participatory de-
sign process as starting points rather than fixed specifications.
Teacher trainers, media producers, and learning-technology
designers subsequently interpreted them from within their re-
spective professional practices, and their questions and con-
cerns shaped how the entry points were articulated.
In respect to this process, the entry points should conse-
quently be understood as both products of participation and
infrastructures for continued participation. This distinction
matters. From a computational empowerment perspective,
participation was not conceived as consulting practitioners
about a framework already determined by researchers. Con-
sistent with the Scandinavian PD tradition (Bødker 2003;
Bødker et al. 2022) underpinning computational empower-
ment, stakeholders need sufficient ownership to question re-
searchers’ assumptions about AI literacy and determine how
framework concepts should inform their professional work.
Limitations
Our paper has several limitations. First, the contribution of
the entry points concerns the design process, not the ef-
fectiveness of the resulting materials. The three vignettes
demonstrate how teacher educators, media creators and re-
searchers used the entry points to create educational mate-
rials. They do not demonstrate that the entry points them-
selves support children’s AI literacy, nor do they establish
that teachers or parents will interpret the resulting materi-
als as intended. We cannot claim that these five entry points
are sufficient to foster AI literacy or CE among learners, a
limitation consistent with the scope we established when in-
troducing them. Future research should follow the materials
to investigate how teachers, children, and parents appropriate
them.
A second limitation concerns the cultural and institutional
conditions under which the entry points emerged. CE itself is
rooted in the Scandinavian participatory design tradition, and
the PD process reported here similarly assumes that profes-
sional stakeholders should have meaningful opportunities to
question concepts, negotiate priorities, and shape the result-
ing educational agenda. These conditions are not culturally
or institutionally neutral. Rather than treating this as a prob-
lem to be resolved through prescriptive standards, it raises
an important question for future research: Which aspects of
entry points can travel across contexts, and which need to be
renegotiated locally?
Finally, we cannot claim that the five entry points are ex-
haustive or universally applicable. Rather, they should be
understood as situated outcomes of the particular partici-
patory process through which they emerged. Their formu-
lation reflects the specific stakeholders, practices, and con-
cerns brought together in this project. In our case compu-
tational empowerment heavily influenced the development
of the entry points. Other PD processes, involving different
stakeholders or situated in different contexts, may lead to the
development of different entry points.
Contribution
Despite these limitations, our contribution extends beyond
the five specific entry points presented in this paper. We
reported on how PD can provide a process for translating
comprehensive AI literacy frameworks into generative and
negotiable resources for designing educational materials. For
researchers, entry points offer a way of bridging frameworks
and situated design practice without prescribing how frame-
works should be put into action. Thus, rather than adopting
our five entry points as a fixed set, we invite researchers to
consider how and what entry points they might collabora-
tively develop together with their partners.
