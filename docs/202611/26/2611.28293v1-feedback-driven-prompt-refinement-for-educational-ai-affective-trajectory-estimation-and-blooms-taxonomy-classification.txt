# Feedback-Driven Prompt Refinement for Educational AI: Affective Trajectory Estimation and Bloom’s Taxonomy Classification 

Josh Dean , Member, IEEE ; Karan Patel; Gaurangi Raul; Yu-Zheng Lin , Member, IEEE; Jesus Pacheco ;Sicong Shao , Member IEEE; Shalaka Satam ; Soheil Salehi , Senior Member, IEEE; Pratik Satam 

Abstract —Contribution: This study has shown that a shared prompt-refinement procedure, guided by distinct affective and cognitive feedback signals, can improve held-out reference agree-ment and multi-label classification while reducing affective-generation variability without executor parameter updates. 

Background: Industry 4.0 has increased demand for person-alized technical education across interconnected computing and engineering domains, while prompt sensitivity and generation variability have complicated dependable interpretation of instruc-tional dialogue and learning outcomes. 

Research Questions: The study has examined whether task-specific feedback can improve affective-reference agreement, gen-eration consistency, and cognitive classification without updating executor parameters. 

Methodology: A secondary language model has revised execu-tor prompts using executor-output discrepancies against model-generated affective references for four dialogues and annotated Bloom labels for 70 learning outcomes; separate sets of 100 EduTalk conversations and 1,000 outcomes have supported held-out comparisons. Small refinement sets, unvalidated affective ref-erences, and absent learner-outcome measures have constrained interpretation. 

Findings: Affective mean absolute error has decreased from 0.742 to 0.702 and mean generation standard deviation from 0.307 to 0.238, while Bloom micro-F1 has increased from 0.687 to 0.893 and exact-match subset accuracy from 0.418 to 0.636. These findings have supported prompt refinement as a candidate approach for developing adaptive technical instruction, while its effects on workforce learning remain to be established. 

Index Terms —Adaptive education, affective trajectory estima-tion, Bloom’s Taxonomy, Industry 4.0, large language models, prompt optimization, workforce education 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# THE Fourth Industrial Revolution integrates cloud infras-tructure, Internet of Things (IoT) networks, and smart manufacturing into cyber-physical environments [1] that re-quire workers to connect knowledge across networking, sys-tems programming, embedded systems, and other interde-pendent domains. Industry 4.0 systems become more tightly coupled. Workforce education must accommodate varied prior preparation and learning pace while helping learners apply technical concepts across authentic contexts [2]. Adaptive educational systems therefore need to coordinate instructional-content access, course-specific knowledge, learner-state repre-sentations, and language-model-mediated response generation. Prior evaluations have examined complementary functions that inform such architectures. Slide-based instructional assistance supported on-demand explanation, course-document retrieval grounded tutoring responses in domain materials, and dialogue analyses represented affective and cognitive learner states [3], [4], [5]. These findings motivate the integrated architecture presented here, while the current evaluation tests prompt refinement separately on held-out tasks. A focused question remained whether systematic prompt refinement improved a fixed executor’s behavior across distinct pedagogical objec-tives, including continuous affective estimation and structured cognitive classification. In Industry 4.0 workforce education, the need to connect technical knowledge with equipment operation, fault diagnosis, and system-level reasoning has mo-tivated instructional approaches sensitive to both the cognitive demands of learning objectives and the difficulties learners express in dialogue. Although large language models have expanded opportunities to interpret these complementary di-mensions, sensitivity to instructional wording and variability across generations have raised questions about the dependabil-ity of the resulting analyses. These concerns have motivated the present investigation of prompt refinement as a means of strengthening dialogue-based affective estimation and cogni-tive classification within the broader development of adaptive technical education. This study has evaluated a shared prompt-refinement pro-cedure for utterance-level affective trajectory alignment and multi-label Bloom’s Taxonomy classification, using task-specific feedback to revise executor instructions without up-dating model parameters. Separate refinement and held-out sets have supported comparisons between initial and selected prompts, with model-generated trajectories supplying affec-tive references and annotated learning outcomes supplying cognitive targets. The findings have indicated improved ref-erence agreement, classification performance, and affective-generation consistency; the study has not assessed student learning, retention, or achievement. Evaluating these com-plementary tasks within one framework has characterized the potential and evidentiary limits of prompt refinement for adaptive technical education. II. R ELATED WORK

A. Affective and Cognitive Learner-State Modeling 

Adaptive instruction depends on representations of both cognitive objectives and affective change over an interaction. Bloom’s Taxonomy provides a framework for describing the cognitive complexity of learning objectives [11]. Affective modeling, in turn, has represented utterance-level states as trajectories whose variation can be analyzed across repeated model inferences. The present study brought these comple-mentary tasks into a common prompt-refinement evaluation. LLM-MC-Affect modeled utterance-level affect as a latent distribution, used repeated stochastic inferences to estimate central tendency and dispersion, and derived dyadic inter-action indicators from sequential cross-correlation, estimated lag, and trajectory slopes [4]. Its evaluation compared six LLMs, used 20 Monte Carlo samples per dialogue turn, and examined temperature sensitivity and instructional interaction patterns. The analyses showed that mean trajectories remained comparatively stable as decoding temperature changed, while estimated dispersion increased, and that model families dif-fered in their ability to represent affective turning points. This work therefore showed how affective ambiguity and interpersonal coupling can be analyzed beyond deterministic sentiment labels. At the same time, its controlled case studies used a simulated instructional-dialogue dataset. Its variance measure may conflate linguistic ambiguity with model and decoding effects, and its cross-correlation indicators did not es-tablish causal influence or alignment with human-rated affect. Those qualifications were especially relevant when model-derived affective estimates were reused as feedback signals for adaptive systems. Related research on reputation analysis has distinguished sentiment expressed in text from the text’s implications for a target entity, demonstrating that negative sentiment can coexist with positive reputation polarity [12]. This distinction provides a useful conceptual caution for educational affect modeling, where agreement with a polarity reference has not independently established the validity of an inferred learner state. 

B. Behavioral Representation Across Domains 

Outside education, behavioral monitoring has used reference models and complementary observations to interpret system activity. A multi-level Bluetooth intrusion-detection system has represented protocol activity through observation flows and n-grams to distinguish normal from abnormal behavior [13]. At the same time, multi-layer anomaly detection has combined system, process, and network observations in a shared repre-sentation [14]. These studies provide methodological analogies for reference-based evaluation and contextual representation. However, they do not establish educational validity or the effectiveness of prompt refinement. 

C. Automated Prompt Optimization and the Present Study 

Research on prompt sensitivity has shown that meaning-preserving changes in prompt formatting can affect language-model performance in few-shot evaluations [15], raising methodological questions about the dependability of results from a single prompt presentation. For educational classifi-cations and dialogue-based estimates, this evidence has moti-vated attention to prompt presentation alongside task-specific instruction design. However, the present study has compared initial and selected prompts without isolating sensitivity to formatting or other presentation features. Prompt engineering often relies on manual trial and error. In contrast, automated approaches treat natural-language instruc-tions as candidates that can be generated, tested, and refined against task-specific feedback. Automatic Prompt Engineer and Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) have used language models to generate and evaluate candidate instructions with-out updating executor parameters [16], [17]. Self-Refine has instead used iterative self-feedback to revise generated outputs, providing a related feedback mechanism rather than an equiv-alent prompt-optimization procedure [18]. These approaches have informed feedback-guided refinement, although their ob-jectives and evaluation settings have not necessarily reflected the pedagogical criteria and learner-context constraints of adaptive education. Education-oriented post-training has used authentic learning data to shape model behavior [19]. Course-specific knowledge provision supplies external evidence at inference time. At the same time, prompt-level refinement remained complementary because it targeted how an executor interpreted task instructions and feedback without changing model parameters or requiring the prompt itself to encode all domain knowledge. Related research on generative digital twins has adapted LLM-based forecasts by updating structured observation windows and aggregating repeated predictions without task-specific parameter training [20]. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

This study has examined whether a shared feedback-driven prompt-refinement procedure improves affective tra-jectory estimation and cognitive classification while executor parameters remain fixed. Initial and selected prompts have been compared on held-out measures of reference agreement, classification performance, and generation variability. Prior educational studies have informed the broader architecture, but their datasets, interventions, and outcomes have not been pooled with the present experiments. The design has evaluated model behavior rather than instructional efficacy or learner achievement. 

A. Integrated Adaptive-Learning Architecture 

Figure 1 synthesizes instructional-content preparation, course-knowledge access, learner-state representation, and context-sensitive response generation. The experiments have evaluated only the model-level prompt-refinement loop, repre-sented by dashed feedback components; content preparation, knowledge access, and platform delivery have supplied ar-chitectural context without being integrated or experimentally evaluated here. The interconnected demands of Industry 4.0 workforce education have motivated the selection of two complementary analytical tasks within this architecture: affective trajectory estimation, which represents sentiment as an instructional di-alogue unfolds, and multi-label cognitive classification, which characterizes the demands articulated in learning outcomes. For equipment operation, fault diagnosis, and system-level rea-soning, these representations could inform how instructional support responds to expressed difficulty and how learning objectives combine multiple cognitive processes. The ex-perimental design has therefore examined whether a shared prompt-refinement procedure can improve these underlying analyses through distinct feedback signals, establishing a basis for subsequent investigation of their contribution to adaptive technical instruction. PRISM has provided a conceptual precedent for com-bining learner dialogue and digital-twin observations with sentiment analysis and knowledge-augmented guidance [9]. Photogrammetry-derived equipment models could supply vi-sual context [10], while generative digital-twin methods have represented evolving process behavior from structured obser-vations [20]. A proposed semiconductor-training extension of PRISM describes equipment-specific exercises in fabrication procedures, operation, and troubleshooting [21]. These exam-ples identify potential deployment contexts. Their integration with the refinement loop and their educational effectiveness are outside the scope of this evaluation. Algorithm 1, adapted from prior work [3], specifies how learner context, retrieved course material, and response eval-uation could connect within the architecture. Its retrieval and tutoring stages were not executed in the present experiments.

E. Data and Experimental Design 

The affective task used EduTalk, approximately 1,280 teacher–student dialogues annotated for conversation-level bi-nary sentiment [8]; the present trajectory task instead used utterance-level model-generated references. The cognitive task used multi-label learning outcomes from the dataset reported by Li et al. [22]. Table II specifies the models, tempera-tures, refinement and evaluation sample sizes, feedback, and measures. Both tasks were evaluated zero-shot without task-specific fine-tuning, and held-out examples supplied no refine-ment feedback. Four affective refinement conversations did not overlap with the 100 test conversations, while 1,000 Bloom outcomes were reserved separately from the 70 refinement examples. The records do not specify sampling procedures, Bloom refinement-partition sizes, label frequencies, or split-generation methods; consequently, interpretation remains lim-ited to the reported samples. Section III-D preserves the refiner instructions, and the table note identifies remaining replication limits. 

F. Evaluation Measures 

For the affective task, the mean executor prediction across repeated generations supplied the trajectory used to calculate mean absolute error (MAE) against the deterministic LLM-generated reference; average standard deviation across held-out conversations summarized generation variability. For cog-nitive classification, we averaged predictions across repeated executor runs. Micro-F1 across the six Bloom’s Taxonomy labels served as the primary measure, subset accuracy required an exact match across all labels for an outcome, and stan-dard deviation summarized run-to-run variability. The evaluation has examined two analytical functions rel-evant to adaptive Industry 4.0 workforce education. It repre-sents sentiment across instructional dialogue and characterizes the cognitive demands of learning outcomes. Comparisons between initial and selected prompts have assessed whether feedback-driven refinement improves reference agreement, classification performance, and generation consistency on held-out inputs, providing evidence about the model behavior underlying these functions. Although equipment operation, fault diagnosis, and system-level reasoning have motivated their potential application, the evaluated dialogues and learning outcomes have supported task-level analysis rather than direct assessment of workforce training. 

A. Sentiment Trajectory Alignment Results 

On a held-out test split of 100 non-overlapping student dialogues from the EduTalk Sentiment Dataset, the optimized prompt Popt reduced the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) relative to the deterministic reference trajectory from 0.742 to 0.702, representing a 5.39% relative error reduction. Simultaneously, the average standard deviation ( σ) across independent stochas-tic decoding runs decreased from 0.307 to 0.238, representing a 22.48% reduction in output variability. Table III summarizes these quantitative metrics. Lower MAE indicated closer agree-ment with the deterministic Nemotron reference, rather than independently validated affective accuracy. Lower generation standard deviation indicated greater consistency, not uncer-tainty about a learner’s true affect. These aggregate measures did not establish reliable detection of particular emotional transitions or improved instructional decisions. 

B. Case-Based Affective-Trajectory Analysis 

Two selected EduTalk dialogues provided case-based evi-dence of the trajectory patterns and run-to-run variation sum-marized by the aggregate results. In the Cold War exchange, the mean sentiment estimates for the student and teacher declined together; in the French Revolution exchange, the student trajectory shifted from negative to positive during the narrative-oriented interaction. Shaded regions show standard deviations across ten repeated sentiment-analysis attempts for each dialogue. These repeated generations characterize output variability and are distinct from prompt-refinement iterations. The cases provide descriptive context for the affective task. However, they do not add independent observations to the held-out sample or establish that the instructional strategy caused the observed changes or improved learning. These history-based dialogues have illustrated the affective-analysis procedure in general educational interactions. Their relevance to Industry 4.0 instruction rests on the proposed analytical function, while their validity for equipment-specific dialogue and technical troubleshooting remains to be established.

A. Interpretation in Relation to Prompt Optimization Litera-ture 

The endpoint improvements have been consistent with the model-generated search and feedback-guided revision mech-anisms of APE, OPRO, and Self-Refine [16], [17], [18]. However, the study has not compared optimizers, expert-authored prompts, or fine-tuning, or isolated individual prompt changes; relative superiority and the causes of improvement therefore remain unresolved. Course-grounded immersive tu-toring [3] has addressed access to domain evidence, whereas the present loop has targeted interpretation of task instructions and feedback. Their integration remains a hypothesis for future evaluation. 

B. Cost and Measurement Scope 

Refinement has required additional executor and refiner calls, but timing and token-cost measurements were insuf-ficient to quantify overhead or assess real-time suitability. Findings from a separate slide-processing pipeline do not quantify refinement costs [5]. Future evaluations should report end-to-end latency, token use, and prompt-update frequency alongside task performance. 

C. Validity of Model-Generated and Annotated Targets 

The affective task used a deterministic Nemotron refer-ence, making the comparison reproducible. This use of a fixed reference differs from LLM-MC-Affect’s use of re-peated inferences to characterize trajectory variation [4]. Nei-ther approach provides objective or human-validated ground truth. The measured reduction in error therefore indicated closer agreement with that reference, not improved diagnosis of learners’ emotional states. Reduced generation variability indicates greater output consistency but does not establish improved affective validity. Because repeated-inference dis-persion may reflect both model stochasticity and meaningful interpretive ambiguity, future evaluation should test whether prompt refinement preserves disagreement observed among human raters [4]. The Bloom task used labels associated with Li et al.’s learning-outcome dataset. Li et al. reported coding by a trained annotator and independent review of 30% of the objectives, with Cohen’s κ increasing from 0.63 to 0.80 after label revision [22]. These source-level checks support annota-tion provenance but do not independently establish the validity or label distribution of the subset evaluated here. Follow-up studies should use expert review, multiple human raters, inter-rater agreement, and subgroup error analyses before using either signal to support consequential instructional decisions. Exploratory research on agent-generated textual artifacts has combined model-assisted interpretation with subsequent human verification while acknowledging influences from prompts, model providers, and human intervention [23]. These methodological limits reinforce the need to independently validate educational interpretations before using model-derived signals to guide instruction. 

D. Implications for Adaptive Education 

Affective signals and adaptive prompts may eventually support responsive instruction, but the present experiments measured model outputs rather than learner engagement, per-sistence, retention, or equitable outcomes. An earlier PPE-training pilot has reported higher task hit rates and shorter completion times with adaptive difficulty, although unequal participant groups and repeated observations have limited causal interpretation [8]. Prior work situated personalized tutoring and technical instruction within educational contexts [3], [4], [5], but neither those studies nor the present of-fline evaluation established that sentiment-based adaptation improves learning. Such use would require learner-centered validation, transparent instructor oversight, and assessment of IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON EDUCATION 10 

differential errors before affective inference informed instruc-tional decisions. 

E. Limitations and Future Research 

The evidence has remained limited to endpoint comparisons on fixed held-out sets and small refinement samples, without independent repetitions of the full optimization procedure, random-seed analyses, confidence intervals, or inferential tests. Generation standard deviation has not supplied a confidence interval for the measured improvement, and neither human validation of affect estimates nor learner outcomes has been assessed. Follow-up studies should log each prompt update, model version, prompt, sampling decision, decoding setting, and call budget, and report independently repeated opti-mization runs with confidence intervals. Comparisons with expert-authored prompts and established optimizers should use matched token and latency budgets. Human-rated affect with agreement statistics, audited Bloom annotations and per-class errors, and supervised laboratory pilots measuring task completion and learner feedback should precede adequately powered multi-site studies of retention and transfer. VI. C ONCLUSION 

Feedback-driven prompt refinement has improved measured performance without executor parameter updates: across 100 EduTalk conversations, reference-based sentiment MAE has decreased from 0.742 to 0.702 and generation standard devi-ation from 0.307 to 0.238; across 1,000 learning outcomes, Bloom micro-F1 has increased from 0.687 to 0.893 and subset accuracy from 0.418 to 0.636. These task-level findings have complemented course-grounded educational architectures [3] without establishing human-valid affect recognition, opti-mization convergence, or learning gains. Human validation, optimizer comparisons, and supervised learner studies remain necessary to determine whether these analytical improvements support adaptive Industry 4.0 workforce education.
