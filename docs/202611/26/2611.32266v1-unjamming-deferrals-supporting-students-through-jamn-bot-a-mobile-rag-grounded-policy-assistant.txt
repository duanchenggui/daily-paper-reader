Abstract —In some educational institutions, workflows such as the exam deferral process can be slow, inconvenient, and inconsistent, which creates the need to implement novel digital systems. This paper presents Schedulater, a mobile app for man-aging university exam deferrals. JAMN Bot is a Large Language Model (LLM)-powered chatbot integrated into Schedulater that provides context-aware support to help users understand the deferral process. The system features a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline using Ollama (Llama 3.2 3B) for self-hosted inference which enables policy-grounded responses without sacrificing privacy. We evaluate the system using a 43-item benchmark spanning Exam Deferral, Privacy, Scheduling, Cross-Policy, and Adversarial & Out-of-Scope questions, and report inter-rater agreement among our human evaluators along-side retrieval hit-rate metrics. Subsequently, we utilize an LLM-as-a-judge approach with GPT-5.4, revealing slight-to-moderate alignment with our raters that improves with answer quality, and check the ranking against a panel including Gemini 3.5 Flash and Qwen3.5 397B. The results of our study display trade-offs between open-weight and frontier models, as well as LLM-human evaluation alignment with answer quality. Schedulater demonstrates the feasibility of integrating lightweight LLMs into university administrative workflows with structured retrieval. 

Index Terms —Educational Chatbot, Mobile Applications, Large Language Models (LLMs), Retrieval-Augmented Gener-ation (RAG), Institutional Policy Navigation

B. Existing Gaps 

Many universities have developed personalized chatbots; however, current systems are more focused on tutoring, en-gagement, or broad LLM access, which leaves a gap in policy navigation support. The University of Michigan utilizes two different AI-powered tools: Go Blue and Maizey. Go Blue is an AI assistant for campus life that provides real-time information for campus services, including dining and transit [4], which allows users to upload their own data and documents for a personalized experience. Faculty use the arXiv:2611.32266v1 [cs.CL] 24 Nov 2026 second tool, Maizey, to facilitate tutoring for their courses. Maizey uses Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to en-sure responses come directly from the uploaded materials. The University of California, Irvine launched ZotGPT ClassChat, a tool where instructors upload course materials so the chat-bot can provide students with in-depth explanations to their questions [5]. Arizona State University launched the CreateAI platform, providing students, faculty, and staff access to over 50 generative AI models to which they can add instructions and system prompts for customization [6]. They also have a chatbot, Sunny, that assists students with course navigation and scheduling tasks [7], [8]. JAMN Bot is different from the aforementioned chatbots in that it is preconfigured with policies and integrated into the workflow, to provide students with immediate responses grounded in institutional policy documents. It combines aca-demic workflows with AI for students to receive guidance without delays. 

C. Contributions 

The contributions of this work include the design and implementation of an LLM-based chatbot integrated in an exam deferral scheduling system within a university setting, along with a case study evaluating its effectiveness as a policy assistant. From this case study, we report three findings: our raters agree strongly on grounding but less so on correct-ness, model scale does not predict answer quality under a grounding-strict rubric, and the serving stack affects LLM-human agreement on correctness but not on grounding. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. AI and Mobile-Based Learning Systems 

Due to recent advances in AI-driven educational tools, the ecosystem of mobile-based assistants utilized in academic and administrative tasks continues to grow [9], [10]. Two popular examples of these learning applications are the platforms Duolingo and Quizlet, both of which demonstrate how AI can be embedded into everyday workflows for many people broadly in the education space [10], [11]. These systems, which provide adaptive explanations, practice support, and per-sonalized feedback, highlight the effectiveness of AI tools on their users due to the improved accessibility and engagement for these mobile-based learning environments [9]. 

B. LLM-Powered Assistants and Chatbots in Education 

Historically, the early adoption of AI chatbots in educational institutions focused primarily on answering common questions for students [12]. However, only more recently have they been developed to utilize LLMs, which are more capable of handling open-ended questions from students [9]. Aside from pure capability improvements, these LLM-based systems have enhanced user experience when prompted to explain concepts, summarize material, generate practice questions and subsequent answers from course content by supporting more natural interactions [13]–[16]. Despite limited integration into institutional workflows, there is a growing demand for and a shift towards more conversational educational support systems. 

C. RAG in Educational Systems 

RAG is a key technique that grounds responses in exter-nal knowledge sources to improve the factual reliability of language model outputs [17]. In these systems, user queries retrieve the relevant context from a collection of structured documents, which are then provided to the model during generation [17]. This approach reduces hallucinations and im-proves factual consistency compared to standalone LLMs [18]. In the context of educational environments, RAG has been applied to question-solving systems, tutoring support, and other academic assistance tools. Using RAG in LLM-powered tools enables the models to generate explanations based on lecture notes, textbooks, or institutional documentation [9]. These properties make RAG particularly suitable for institu-tional applications where accuracy and traceability are critical. 

D. Small Models vs Frontier Models in Deployment 

JAMN Bot is powered by Llama 3.2 3B, a relatively small language model [19]. Smaller models are easier to deploy and can be better suited for privacy-sensitive environments, such as universities, while being more efficient than large frontier models [20], [21]. Additionally, behavioural predictability and overhead reduction are maintained with RAG [17]. Notably, there will always be trade-offs between smaller local models and large frontier models [19]. Large models are capable of superior reasoning and broader world knowledge; however, they are also more costly, rely on external cloud APIs and providers which can raise privacy concerns, and require greater computational resources [22]. Institutions will also have to consider that frontier LLMs used via APIs are still subject to versioning and deprecation which impact their stability over time [21]. Ultimately, despite the limitations in capability, locally-served open-weight models offer adequate performance and allow behaviour verification due to fixed model files [20]. Although larger models such as Kimi K3 [23], GLM 5 [24], and Deepseek V4 [25] have become easier to serve due to advances such as vLLM [26], we consider them out of reach for most institutions. For on-device deployment, hardware constraints pose an even greater hurdle. 

E. LLM-as-a-Judge Methodology and Evaluation Practice 

Recent work in LLM evaluation explores the use of LLMs as automated evaluators of other models’ outputs, commonly referred to as LLM-as-a-judge frameworks [27]. This approach scores outputs on criteria such as correctness, relevance, and helpfulness. However, it has been shown in previous studies that LLM-as-a-judge evaluations encounter calibration issues, especially for subjective criteria like helpfulness and quality of reasoning, where an LLM’s judgements can systematically diverge from human annotations [27]. A notable issue with using a single LLM judge to evaluate multiple models is that the judge may favour its own answers. Following prior work [28], we score with a panel of independent judges and report the results in Section V-G. III. S YSTEM DESIGN 

A. Deployment Context: the Schedulater Platform 

Schedulater is a mobile platform designed to support exam deferrals in university settings. It connects students, instructors, and administrators within a single workflow for requesting, reviewing, and scheduling deferred examinations, while providing status updates throughout the process. The app uses a Jetpack Compose Android client and a Kotlin backend implemented with Ktor, and deployed on Railway. Firebase is used for authentication, push notifications (FCM), and document storage, while SQLite serves as the embedded vector store for retrieval operations (Fig. 1). The backend handles core functionality including deferral requests, mes-saging, room scheduling, and coordination between system components, including the JAMN Bot policy assistant. JAMN Bot is built into Schedulater, allowing students to ask questions related to the deferral process. When a query is submitted, it is sent to the backend, which manages the AI pipeline. It includes retrieving relevant knowledge, constructing the prompt, and forwarding the prompt to the self-hosted language model served via Ollama. The response is then returned to the client and displayed on the interface. Deploying the backend and LLM separately allows the model to run locally or as a dedicated service, enabling easier independent model updates that improve cost, privacy, and performance. 

B. UI Rationale 

JAMN Bot is embedded directly into the student interface and appears at the top of the messaging tab for easy access. This design choice is based on the concept that in-context assistance reduces task-switching and increases the likelihood of usage at the point where students experience uncertainty [29]. The JAMN Bot interface provides a conversational experience with a message history and citations that display the source document and the section of the retrieved context to provide improved transparency, as shown in Fig. 2. This allows users to verify the grounding of each answer and reduces over-reliance on model-generated content. The system enforces explicit refusal behaviour. When a query is blocked by guardrails or when retrieval yields no relevant information, the assistant returns a standardized refusal message without fabricated sources. JAMN Bot is designed to avoid specula-tive or unsupported answers by prioritizing correctness and institutional trustworthiness over completeness. IV. RAG P IPELINE 

A. Knowledge Base 

To effectively answer questions regarding policies, privacy, and scheduling, JAMN Bot utilizes a RAG pipeline. Pre-trained language models have promising capabilities; however, they are also prone to hallucinations [17]. Our LLM-powered chatbot ensures that outputs remain consistent by grounding its responses in Markdown documents describing MacEwan University policies 1 and the procedural workflow of Schedu-later. 

B. Retrieval and Indexing 

Markdown documents are split into chunks of about 1500 characters with a 100-character overlap during ingestion. This is done using a header-aware chunking strategy. Each chunk is embedded using the nomic-embed-text model [30] via Ollama and stored in a local SQLite database. At query time, the user’s question is embedded and compared against stored chunk vectors using cosine similarity computed in Kotlin. The retriever filters results using a similarity threshold of 0.3 and selects the top-6 most relevant chunks to construct the context. In practice the threshold never binds at this corpus size, as the lowest similarity we observed was 0.505, so every question retrieves six chunks. 

C. Prompt Construction and Generation 

The prompt is structured into three parts consisting of system instructions, the user query, and the retrieved context. The system instructions tell the model to only use information from the Schedulater knowledge base and to avoid unsup-ported or speculative responses. Responses are generated using our production-level LLM at temperature = 0.3, configurable through environment variables, keeping variation across eval-uation runs controlled. 

D. Guardrails and Reliability 

As previously mentioned, a layered guardrail system is implemented in the app for security. Prior to searching for an answer, JAMN Bot checks for malicious use, such as prompting to reveal sensitive data. If triggered, the query is blocked immediately. When a retrieval does not return sufficiently relevant context, the system produces a controlled

A. Setup 

The evaluation is based on a 43-item benchmark dataset spanning five operational categories relevant to the Schedu-later system: Exam Deferral (10), Privacy (5), Scheduling (5), Cross-Policy (15), and Adversarial / Out-of-Scope (8). Each question is paired with a manually written ground-truth answer. The dataset is designed to evaluate both factual retrieval and workflow reasoning within a university policy context. The benchmark includes a structured adversarial split of Hard Adversarial prompts (items 5, 34, 36, 39) as well as Soft Adversarial / Out-of-Scope (OOS) prompts (items 37, 40–43). Scoring was performed at two distinct points in this study: first, to evaluate the production deployment of JAMN Bot (Llama 3.2 3B Q4 K M via Ollama on Railway) against human raters and an LLM judge, and second, to evaluate the performance gap between models of different sizes via OpenRouter [34]. In both scoring instances, the AI-generated answers in response to the benchmark questions were scored against the same rubric on a 0-2 scale on Groundedness (G) and Correctness (C). Groundedness measures whether the re-sponse is supported by the knowledge base, while Correctness measures whether the responses are accurate and factually correct. The inter-rating consensus was taken as the average of three raters (R1, R2, R3). For Cohen’s linear-weighted kappa [35], which takes an ordinal category as its input, the consensus was taken as the median of the three raters rounded to the nearest integer. The LLM judge, GPT-5.4 via OpenRouter, was given a system prompt with three calibration examples, which were high (G=2, C=2), mid (G=1, C=1), and low (G=0, C=0). These examples were used to aid in aligning its evaluations with the human rubric. All judging was performed at temperature 0 so that the scoring is as close to deterministic as the hosted API allows, and so the variance we observe comes from the answers generated rather than the evaluation [36]. For the multi-model comparisons, seven models were eval-uated using the same 43-item benchmark and precomputed retrieved chunks for each question to ensure fairness. The retrieval and prompting stages were re-implemented in Python for these runs, using the same chunking parameters, embed-ding model, similarity threshold, and preflight patterns as the deployed Kotlin backend, so that every model received iden-tical context. The evaluated models included frontier models such as GPT-5.4 [37] and Opus 4.5 [38], as well as open-weight models such as Llama 3.2 and 3.3 [19], Command R7B [39], and DeepSeek V4 Pro [25]. These models were chosen in particular to reflect possible deployment trade-offs between model size and performance. Across both tests, whether it be the intra-architecture serving comparison or the inter-model capability comparison, generation temperature was held constant at 0.3 across every model on every run in triplicate. Every query logs the retrieved chunks, similarity scores, latency, and a final response to be analyzed in the benchmark pipeline.

Agreement on these labels was substantial (α = 0.553), and three items were adjudicated as having no supporting chunk in the knowledge base. The implications for educational institutions are that, if they choose to adopt a RAG-based system, temperature has minimal impact on outcome variance, and that tuning effort is better directed at prompt structure, model selection, and serving precision, which Table IV and Section V-F show move the scores considerably further than sampling temperature does. VI. D ISCUSSION 

A. Effect of the Deployment Configuration 

Educational institutions facing cost or hardware constraints may want to prioritize smaller, cheaper, local models, and will often reach for a quantized model served locally. Our raters recorded a Correctness improvement moving from our deployed configuration to a full-precision hosted one, while the LLM judge recorded a much smaller one and the agreement on Groundedness did not change at all (Section V-F). The two configurations differ in precision, provider, and pipeline implementation, so we report the effect without attributing it to any single one. Notably, this means the judge is least aligned with our raters on exactly the configuration an institution would deploy to save money. The practical implication is that a quantized model’s accuracy should be validated against human raters rather than an LLM judge alone, as the automated judge under-reports the cost of quantizing. 

B. Raw Capabilities of RAG and Instruction-Following 

Many would expect that larger models would inherently outperform smaller models. However, our findings show that this assumption does not always hold. Observing the multi-model leaderboard comparing the Groundedness and Correct-ness between all seven models, with a few exceptions, most rankings feel predictable. The frontier models (GPT-5.4 and Opus 4.5) perform well, as expected. However, the rankings of the non-frontier models provide insight into our claim that bigger does not always mean better. Sorting the LLMs by approximate size in descending order, we get: GPT-5.4, Opus 4.5, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Llama 3.3 70B, Command R7B, Llama 3.2 3B, and Llama 3.2 1B, noting that the parameter counts of the closed models are not published. Notably, Table IV reveals that the larger DeepSeek V4 Pro model underperforms as it ranks 4th place (G&C = 1.636), sitting 0.130 below the much smaller Llama 3.3 70B (G&C = 1.766). That margin is larger than the run-to-run spread of roughly 0.08 we measured, so it is not an artifact of sampling. Despite DeepSeek V4 Pro’s verbose chain-of-thought, its rubric scores are lower than expected [45]. Due to the rubric’s penalization of content that extrapolates beyond the retrieved chunks, models that do not follow the instructions faithfully underperform. In general, the RAG benchmark rewards instructional disci-pline more than raw capabilities. Knowledge of model size is still valuable to infer usefulness to a different degree in the case of Llama 3.2 1B Instruct. Its combined G&C score of 1.173 can act as a useful performance floor. It is likely that models below this size would be more prone to invalid outputs and hallucinations, as the Instruct configuration implies that the model has already been fine-tuned to im-prove instruction-following capabilities. A constant systematic pattern appears regardless of model size, revealing that the items from Adversarial & OOS consistently drop the GPT-5.4 judge scores shown in Table V. While the preflight regex layer cleanly catches all four Hard Adversarial queries, OOS queries, which use paraphrasing or indirect injections, neg-atively impact the surface-pattern matching which correlates with the G&C scores- emphasizing the importance of a layered defence system [32]. With regard to deployment decisions in educational institutions, practical choices lie between a fine-tuned open-weight model such as Llama 3.2 3B Instruct and the frontier models. However, size-related deployment constraints such as privacy, cost, and latency will play a factor in institutions deciding which model to adopt. C. Broader implications for AI in Education 

We believe that our work can provide some key insights for the adoption of LLM-based assistants in higher education. Em-pirical data gathered on Groundedness and Correctness across various models allow us to view the trade-off made between locally deployed smaller open-weight models and frontier models accessed via API calls. Because the 1B model (G&C = 1.173) is likely insufficient for any meaningful student-facing product, educational institutions must, at minimum, rely on models similar to the 3B and 7B for open-weight models. Although the gap in the scores from Table IV indicates that the frontier models have improved performance over the others, the trade-offs would include added cost, latency, and reliance on external APIs which may be constraints at scale. As well, our quantization results show that the choice of model is not the only decision that matters, as the way a model is served affects how reliably its accuracy can be measured. Additionally, we believe that a student-facing AI assistant (even beyond the domain of exam deferrals) will naturally receive queries that may be confusing or adversarial similar to our testing. Because of this, we propose that our layered guardrail pattern (preflight regex, similarity threshold, and empty-retrieval refusal) is a justified design as a first line of defence for chatbots across different domains. VII. L IMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

JAMN Bot’s knowledge was curated by our team to rep-resent the Schedulater workflow and is not entirely represen-tative of any university’s policies and procedures for deferred exams. To improve the utility of JAMN Bot, future work would include expanding the chatbot’s knowledge base so that it can be helpful for policy beyond exam deferrals. If JAMN Bot were able to provide guidance on academic integrity, grade ap-peals, major and minor declarations, changing programs, study plans, stress management, or taking a leave of absence, it could become a centralized hub to help students with their academic lives. As well, further collaboration with faculty would help ensure that common student queries can be answered by the chatbot to potentially ease their workloads. Additionally, the chatbot should be enhanced to support the needs of faculty and administrators, as the current implementation is student-focused. However, extending JAMN Bot’s capabilities would likely require a larger LLM. At the same time, JAMN Bot currently relies on simple regex-based guardrails, which are limited in handling more complex queries. As its scope ex-pands, stronger guardrails would be needed to prevent negative outcomes and user experiences [32]. To make JAMN Bot as convenient as possible to use, we could perform A/B testing on users to see which placement they prefer for JAMN Bot. As of now, JAMN Bot is the first conversation that shows up when looking at the messaging tab, acting as more of a standalone feature. An alternative to this would be embedding JAMN Bot in the navigation bar. During manual testing, latency was also recorded, averaging just under one minute per query. To deploy Schedulater into real university environments, we would integrate a vector database to store chunks for more efficient retrieval [46] and cache common queries to reduce latency. Better retrieval would also address the items in Section V-H where the governing chunk was never surfaced, a correctness problem and not only an efficiency one. In the future, a more rigorous manual evaluation could be conducted to include a longitudinal study and additional human raters. If more rigorous testing were to be done, we would use a 7-point Likert scale for a more precise rating of Groundedness and Correctness. Two limitations remain from our own results. Our ten-chunk knowledge base with a fixed top-6 retriever leaves the set-membership retrieval metrics close to their random baselines, and measures such as NDCG would need a substantially larger corpus [46]. As well, although the judge panel largely preserved the model ranking with no anomalous self-score, GPT-5.4’s margin over second place is smaller than the run-to-run variation we measured, so self-preference can neither be confirmed nor ruled out at this benchmark size [28]. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we presented JAMN Bot, an LLM-powered policy assistant integrated into Schedulater, an application for university exam deferral workflows. JAMN Bot provides students with context-aware exam deferral guidance via RAG, using a self-hosted LLM served by Ollama. The evaluation of JAMN Bot shows that it performs most effectively on well-structured policy guidance, but still finds Out-of-Scope prompts challenging. Our three raters agreed strongly with one another (Krippendorff’s α = 0.894 on Groundedness), giving a stable reference for the LLM judge comparison, and an independent panel of three judges largely preserved the model ranking with no detectable self-preference. Model scale, however, did not predict answer quality under our grounding-strict rubric, as DeepSeek V4 Pro scored 0.130 combined below the much smaller Llama 3.3 70B, a margin wider than the run-to-run spread we measured, because the rubric rewards instruction-following over raw capability. We also found that the serving stack affects judge-human agreement on Correctness but not on Groundedness, which matters for institutions weighing whether to quantize. The results sug-gest that LLM assistants with well-defined guardrails can reduce confusion in university deferral processes. As well, the differences in performance between closed and open-source models, while present to some degree, inspire confidence for the deployment of open models in educational settings, as the top three models on our leaderboard are separated by less than the run-to-run variation we measured. Overall, in-stitutions considering similar systems should note that JAMN Bot highlights the importance of balancing privacy, accuracy, both monetary and human costs, safety, and the availability of accurate information when introducing an AI chatbot into student administrative workflows.
