Abstract 

Generative AI is increasingly used to support students during learning and problem solving, but its instructional value depends on how it is embedded in established learning approaches. Worked examples guide learners through the steps and reasoning of a solution; conversational AI may extend them by responding to individual questions. To isolate this added value, we compared a chatbot-delivered intelligent worked example with a matched static worked example and a lecture script. Nine recitation groups in an undergraduate elec-trodynamics course were cluster-randomized to the three conditions, and 86 students worked individually on a Gauss’s law problem. Concept-test accuracy and self-efficacy increased significantly in all conditions, but changes did not dif-fer significantly between groups ( p = .07 for concept-test accuracy and p = .38 

for self-efficacy). Four weeks later, in a reduced sample with selective participa-tion and pre-existing group differences, students in the static worked-example condition scored higher than those in the script condition ( p = .004 , d = 1 .22 ), whereas the two worked-example conditions did not differ significantly. Usabil-ity, but not perceived educational value, was higher for the intelligent worked example than for the script ( p = .011 , d = 0 .88 ). In a subsequent homework assignment on Amp` ere’s law with free choice of learning material ( N = 46 ),

# 1 Introduction 

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly becoming part of higher education. Alongside widely available general-purpose chatbots, universities are beginning to pro-vide institutionally managed AI platforms and course-specific tutoring systems at larger scales. These developments create new opportunities for immediate and indi-vidualized support, but they also raise a central educational question: Under which instructional conditions does conversational AI support learning rather than merely facilitate task completion? Addressing this question requires evaluations that are grounded in established principles of learning and instruction rather than in the technical capabilities of AI systems alone. 

Worked examples provide one such established instructional approach. By pre-senting a problem together with a structured solution, they can help learners identify relevant problem features, understand solution steps, and develop transfer-able problem-solving schemas (Atkinson et al., 2000; Renkl, 2014; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). Conventional static worked examples, however, provide the same guidance to all learners and cannot respond to individual questions or difficulties. Conversational AI may extend this format by allowing learners to request explanations, clarify indi-vidual steps, and access additional support when needed. An AI-supported worked example may thus combine the structured guidance of example-based learning with the responsiveness of a chatbot. Whether such conversational functionality provides additional instructional value depends critically on the comparison condition. A comparison with conventional course material, such as a lecture script, captures differences in instructional design as well as differences in AI use. In contrast, comparison with a matched static worked example that shares the same core solution structure allows the added value of conversational support to be examined more directly. This distinction is important because find-ings on educational chatbots and AI-supported tutoring remain heterogeneous and appear to depend strongly on how systems are designed and embedded in instruction (Kuhail et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2014; Wollny et al., 2021; Wu & Yu, 2024). Eval-uating AI-supported learning environments therefore requires both an appropriate non-AI comparison and outcomes that extend beyond performance, including learners’ cognitive and motivational experiences. A further challenge concerns whether students use AI-supported learning materials when their use is voluntary. The availability of an AI-based learning environment does not necessarily result in its adoption. Students may value immediate and individual-ized assistance while simultaneously expressing concerns about unreliable information, 2overreliance on AI, academic integrity, or the loss of independent problem-solving skills (Balabdaoui et al., 2024; Johnston et al., 2024). Existing research has often focused on general attitudes or intended use rather than on students’ actual choices between AI-supported and conventional learning materials in authentic course settings. Investigating both instructional effectiveness and voluntary material choice is there-fore important for understanding the practical educational potential of AI-supported learning environments. The present study addresses these issues in an undergraduate electrodynamics course, where students must coordinate conceptual, spatial, and mathematical rea-soning when solving physics problems. In a controlled intervention on Gauss’s law, students learned with an AI-supported worked example (hereafter referred to as the intelligent worked example), a static worked example, or the lecture script. The condi-tions were compared with respect to concept-test performance, self-efficacy, cognitive load, usability, and perceived educational value. In a subsequent exploratory phase on Amp` ere’s law, students could freely choose between the available learning mate-rials, and their choices were examined in relation to concerns and perceived barriers regarding AI use. By combining a controlled comparison with an investigation of vol-untary uptake, the study examines both the added instructional value of an intelligent worked example and whether students choose to use it under more authentic learning conditions. 

# 2 Theoretical background 

## 2.1 Worked examples in physics education 

Problem solving is a central component of university physics learning. For novices, however, solving unfamiliar problems independently can impose substantial demands because they have not yet developed the knowledge structures needed to guide an efficient solution. A worked example presents a problem together with a structured solution, allowing learners to study how a solution is constructed rather than having to discover it through search. In well-structured domains such as mathematics and physics, worked examples can therefore support the initial acquisition of problem-solving skills more effectively than conventional problem solving, a finding known as the worked-example effect (Atkinson et al., 2000; Renkl, 2014; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). Cognitive load theory provides a common explanation for this effect (Sweller et al., 1998). Because working memory is limited, novices may devote substantial resources to searching for a solution procedure. Worked examples reduce such unproductive search and can direct attention toward relations between problem features, underlying principles, and solution steps. Their instructional value therefore lies not merely in providing an answer, but in making the structure and reasoning of a solution accessible for learning. Effective example study nevertheless requires learners to process this reasoning actively. Successful learners tend to generate self-explanations that connect solu-tion steps to underlying principles and anticipate how the solution will proceed (Chi et al., 1989; Renkl, 1997). In physics, productive self-explanations similarly involve 3identifying the relevant principle, explaining why it applies, and clarifying the pur-pose of mathematical operations (Gjerde et al., 2022). Simply adding explanations or prompts, however, does not reliably improve learning; their effectiveness depends on the cognitive activity they elicit (Barbieri et al., 2023; Wittwer & Renkl, 2010). The amount of guidance required also changes with learners’ developing exper-tise. Detailed worked solutions are particularly useful while problem-solving schemas are still weak, whereas the same information may become redundant as competence increases. Guidance can therefore be gradually withdrawn through completion tasks or faded worked examples (Renkl & Atkinson, 2003). This reflects the broader expertise-reversal effect, according to which instructional support that benefits novices may become unnecessary or burdensome for more knowledgeable learners (Scheiger et al., 2023). These principles are particularly relevant in physics, where problem solving requires learners to coordinate conceptual understanding, representations, and mathematical procedures. Physics education research suggests that worked examples are most useful when they make the underlying problem structure and reasoning explicit rather than merely presenting calculations (Badeau et al., 2017; Schmidt-Weigand et al., 2009). Thus, effective example-based learning depends both on promoting principle-based processing and on providing an appropriate level of guidance. 

## 2.2 Supporting worked example-based learning through chatbots 

A limitation of conventional static worked examples is that their explanations and prompts are fixed in advance. Large language models (LLMs) may extend this format by responding to learners’ questions, requesting explanations, and providing additional clarification when needed. Within the ICAP framework, however, deeper engagement depends on what learners actually do rather than on the interface itself: conversational support may elicit constructive or interactive processing, but does not guarantee it (Chi & Wylie, 2014). Recent studies suggest that responsive AI support can be instructionally useful, but that its benefits depend on how the support is structured. In physics education, Becker et al. (2026) found that both an AI chatbot and a structured tiered-hint system reduced cognitive load more than static textbook-style explanations, while the chatbot showed no clear advantage over structured support. This illustrates why AI-supported envi-ronments should be evaluated against meaningful instructional comparison conditions rather than against conventional materials alone. Conversational support also creates risks that are directly relevant to learning from worked examples. Learners may accept AI-generated explanations without sufficiently processing or verifying them. Krupp et al. (2024), for example, found evidence of uncritical reliance on ChatGPT-generated solutions. The ISAR model describes the possibility that AI intended to support learning instead reduces productive cognitive processing as an inversion effect (Bauer et al., 2025). In addition, fluent but scien-tifically incorrect explanations may be difficult for learners to identify (Dahlkemper et al., 2023). Educational use of LLMs therefore requires a pedagogical strategy that promotes critical engagement rather than replacing learners’ own reasoning (Kasneci 4et al., 2023). A worked example used as the basis for an AI tutor must therefore be designed to elicit thinking rather than to substitute for it. 

## 2.3 Domain-specific context 

Gauss’s and Amp` ere’s laws provide compact relations between electromagnetic fields and their sources and can greatly simplify field calculations when appropriate symme-tries are present. Their application, however, requires students to recognize relevant symmetries, distinguish fields from flux or circulation, identify enclosed sources, and select an appropriate integration surface or path. Research in physics education has documented persistent difficulties with these conceptual and representational demands (Campos et al., 2023; Hernandez et al., 2023; Pepper et al., 2012). These character-istics make Gauss’s and Amp` ere’s laws suitable contexts for investigating structured problem-solving support. 

## 2.4 The present study and research questions 

The present study compares an intelligent worked example with a corresponding static worked example and the regular lecture script. Both worked examples use the same underlying solution structure and activation questions intended to promote self-explanation. In the intelligent worked example, an LLM presents the instructor-designed solution conversationally and provides additional support by responding to students’ questions, offering further explanations, and discussing their responses to the activation questions. The comparison with the lecture script therefore examines the overall instructional value of the AI-supported worked-example format, whereas the comparison with the static worked example more specifically examines the added value of conversational support while keeping the core instructional structure constant. In a subsequent exploratory phase, students could freely choose among the available learning materials. This complements the controlled comparison with an examina-tion of whether AI-supported materials are voluntarily adopted and whether material choice is related to concerns and perceived barriers regarding AI use. 

RQ1 (Group differences): How do students who learn with an intelligent worked example, a static worked example, or the lecture script differ with respect to (a) concept-test performance and learning gains immediately after the inter-vention and at follow-up? (b) changes in self-efficacy? (c) perceived cognitive load during learning? (d) perceived usability and educational value of the learning environment? 

RQ2 (Material choice and AI-related concerns): Which learning materials do students choose when their use is voluntary, and how is this choice related to concerns and perceived barriers regarding AI use? 53 Methods

The study was conducted in the undergraduate Experimental Physics II course at the University of G¨ ottingen, which typically introduces physics students to university-level electromagnetism in their second semester. The course combines lectures with weekly recitation sessions in which compulsory assignments are discussed. The study comprised two consecutive phases: a controlled intervention on Gauss’s law and a subsequent exploratory phase on Amp` ere’s law. Participants provided writ-ten informed consent when completing a study questionnaire or test for the first time. Self-generated pseudonymous codes were used to link responses across measurement occasions. 

Intervention phase – Gauss’s law 

The intervention followed a cluster-randomized pretest–posttest–follow-up design with three learning conditions and was conducted during the regular recitation sessions. Stu-dents had selected fixed recitation groups before the learning conditions were assigned. 6The nine groups were subsequently randomized to the intelligent worked-example con-dition (IG1), the static worked-example condition (IG2), or the lecture-script condition (CG), with three groups assigned to each condition while accounting for group-size differences. Each session lasted 90 minutes. Students first completed a pretest ( t0) assessing conceptual understanding of Gauss’s law and self-efficacy regarding its application. Gauss’s law had previously been introduced in the lecture. Students then worked individually and at their own pace on the same target problem, supported by the material assigned to their condition. Students in both worked-example conditions could additionally consult the lecture script. Material use was self-directed, and no additional instructional support was provided during the learning phase. Immediately afterward, students completed the posttest ( t1), including the same concept test and self-efficacy measure as at pretest, as well as measures of cognitive load, usability, and perceived educational value. Four weeks later, the concept test was administered again during the regular lecture (t2). Because participation at follow-up was independent of participation in the inter-vention, some students completed only this assessment. These students constituted a non-equivalent cross-sectional reference group and were not included in longitudinal analyses of learning gains. 

Exploration phase – Amp` ere’s law 

The subsequent exploration phase focused on Amp` ere’s law. Students completed a regular homework assignment requiring them to apply Amp` ere’s law and could freely choose whether to use an intelligent worked example, a static worked example, both worked examples, or neither as additional learning support. The regular lecture script remained available throughout the course and was not provided specifically for this assignment. Unlike the intervention phase, the target problem was completed outside the supervised recitation setting and submitted as part of the regular course work. After submitting the assignment, students reported which of the two worked exam-ples they had used and completed questionnaires assessing their concerns about AI use and perceived barriers to using AI-based learning support ( t3; Fig. 1). The explo-ration phase followed an observational design: students freely selected their learning materials, and neither the timing nor the intensity of material use was controlled or monitored. Information on the use of the worked examples was therefore based on students’ self-reports. 

3.1.2 Measures and materials 

This section describes the learning materials used during the intervention and explo-ration phases, as well as the instruments employed to assess students’ conceptual understanding, self-efficacy, cognitive load, usability and perceived educational value, and AI-related concerns and barriers. All learning materials and assessment instru-ments are provided in Appendices A and B. An overview of all scales is given in Tab. 2. 7Worked examples 

Students in both intervention groups learned to apply Gauss’s law using worked exam-ples developed specifically for this study (Fig. 2 and Appendix A.1). Both versions addressed the same learning objectives and presented an identical step-by-step solu-tion for calculating the electric field of a hollow sphere using Gauss’s law. The worked examples were designed according to established principles of example-based learning (Atkinson et al., 2000; Renkl, 2014). The solution was structured into clearly separated subgoals, each accompanied by a brief conceptual explanation. Activation questions were integrated at selected points in the solution process to prompt students to reflect on the underlying physical concepts and engage in self-explanation (Chi et al., 1989). The questions were open-ended and required students to formulate an explanation in their own words. The static worked example (Fig. 2a) was provided digitally as a PDF and presented the complete worked solution in a tabular format. For the complete static worked example see Appendix A.1. The individual solution steps and corresponding concep-tual explanations were arranged sequentially, with the activation questions embedded directly at the relevant points in the table. Students worked through the material independently and answered these questions without receiving feedback or additional support. The intelligent worked example (Fig. 2b) extended the static version by integrat-ing an AI-based chatbot into the learning environment. The worked example followed the same sequence of solution steps and activation questions, but provided conversa-tional support throughout the learning process. Students could use the chatbot to ask follow-up questions, request additional explanations, and discuss their responses to the activation questions before proceeding to the next solution step. Thus, the intelligent and static worked examples were identical in terms of content, solution structure, and instructional explanations, differing only in the availability of conversational, AI-based support. The intelligent worked example was implemented using ChatAI, the institutional AI platform provided by the University of G¨ ottingen (University of G¨ ottingen, n.d.). The underlying large language model was Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, with a tem-perature of 0.0 and a top-p value of 0.05. The intervention was conducted between May 5 and 7, 2026. The system prompt used to guide the chatbot’s responses is provided in Appendix A.2. During the exploration phase, students had access to both an intelligent and a static worked example on calculating the magnetic field of a long solenoid using Amp` ere’s law. Both were developed according to the same instructional design principles and followed the same structure as their respective counterparts on Gauss’s law used during the intervention phase.

Self-efficacy questionnaire 

Students’ self-efficacy regarding the application of Gauss’s law was assessed using a four-item questionnaire (Appendix B.3). The items were developed following the guide-lines for constructing self-efficacy scales proposed by Bandura (2006) and informed by the instrument developed by Freed et al. (2022). They addressed students’ perceived ability to understand the concept of Gauss’s law, identify suitable charge symmetries, construct appropriate Gaussian surfaces, and apply Gauss’s law to calculate electric fields. An example item is “I can assess whether the symmetry of the charge distri-bution is suitable for applying Gauss’s law.” Responses were recorded on a six-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 6 ( strongly agree ).

Choice of learning materials 

During the exploration phase, students freely chose which of the two learning materials to use. Questionnaire on AI-related concerns and barriers 

Students’ concerns about using AI were assessed using five items developed for the present study: two with reference to the perceived risk of use subscale of the TAME-ChatGPT instrument (Sallam et al., 2024), one to a study of ChatGPT use for homework assignments (Miljkovi´ c Kreˇ car et al., 2024), one to examples of flawed AI-generated physics content (Bitzenbauer, 2023), and one without reference to a pub-lished source. All items continued the stem “I am concerned that . . . ” and addressed potential effects of AI use on independent problem solving and critical thinking, as well as concerns about unnoticed errors and superficial understanding when learning physics with AI. An example item is “I am concerned that using AI may reduce my ability to solve problems independently.” 

Perceived barriers to AI use were assessed using five items developed for the present study and conceptually related to perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989). The items addressed difficulties in identifying relevant information, text-heavy responses, prompt formulation, the perceived ratio of effort to benefit, and the user interface. An example item is “The AI’s responses often contain too much text and are therefore difficult to navigate.” 

Responses to both scales were recorded on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 7 ( strongly agree ). Neither scale referred specifically to the intervention or its learning materials; they were therefore intended to capture students’ general concerns about and perceived barriers to AI use rather than evaluations of the specific learning environment (Appendix B.4).

## 3.3 Data analysis 

All variables were linearly transformed to a common range of [0 , 1]. Distributions of the continuous measures were considered sufficiently close to normal for parametric anal-yses based on their skewness and kurtosis and the sample size. Internal consistencies of the multi-item scales are reported in Tab. 2; results involving the extraneous cogni-tive load scale, which showed comparatively low reliability ( αC = .56), are interpreted with caution. For RQ1, between-group differences at individual measurement points were exam-ined using one-way ANOVAs, and changes over time using repeated-measures and mixed-design ANOVAs. The mixed-design analyses tested time × group effects for response accuracy and self-efficacy, load type × group effects for cognitive load, and scale × group effects for usability and perceived educational value. Where appropri-ate, Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons were conducted. Generalized eta squared (η2

> G

) was reported for ANOVA-based analyses (Olejnik & Algina, 2003), and Cohen’s 

d for significant pairwise differences. Individual change scores were additionally calculated for response accuracy and self-efficacy from pretest to posttest and, for response accuracy in the longitudinal follow-up sample, from posttest to follow-up. Group differences in change scores were 14 examined using one-way ANOVAs. Learning gains in response accuracy from pretest to posttest were also described using Hake’s normalized gain gH (Hake, 1998). ## 5.1 RQ1 – Group differences 

Across all three conditions, response accuracy increased significantly from pretest to posttest. This indicates that students developed their understanding of Gauss’s law during the instructional phase, but the absence of a significant time × group inter-action shows that the magnitude of this improvement did not differ reliably between the intelligent worked example, the static worked example, and the lecture script. Similarly, self-efficacy increased substantially across conditions without significant dif-ferences in change between groups. Thus, the present data do not support a general advantage of either worked-example condition in immediate learning or self-efficacy. This general learning effect should not be attributed exclusively to the learning materials. The pretest and immediate posttest contained the same items and were separated by only about 45 minutes, and no randomized condition without tuto-rial participation was included. Learning may therefore also reflect engagement with the target problem, repeated testing, the preceding lecture, or other features of the tutorial. The substantially lower follow-up performance of students who had not par-ticipated in the intervention is consistent with a benefit associated with participation in the tutorial phase, but this non-equivalent reference group does not permit a causal estimate of such an effect. 22 5.1.1 Patterns associated with worked-example-based support 

Although the worked-example conditions did not produce significantly larger imme-diate learning gains, some findings are consistent with differences in how students processed the learning materials. In the test combination analysis, pre–post effect sizes in both worked-example groups increased as greater weight was assigned to the explanatory component, whereas the opposite pattern was observed in the lecture-script condition. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that the worked examples placed greater emphasis on explanatory reasoning. Both examples combined an explicit solution structure with conceptual explanations and activation prompts intended to elicit self-explanation, instructional features that have been associated with productive example study (Atkinson et al., 2000; Chi et al., 1989; Renkl, 2014; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). The present analysis does not establish a differential effect on conceptual reasoning, but it suggests that the nature of the observed gains may have differed across instructional formats. The cognitive-load measures provide a similarly tentative indication of differences in the learning experience. A significant load type × group interaction was observed, with the intelligent worked-example condition showing the lowest descriptive extrane-ous cognitive load and the highest germane cognitive load, whereas the lecture-script condition showed the reverse ordering. The separate overall group effects for intrinsic, extraneous, and germane cognitive load were not significant, although germane cog-nitive load was significantly higher in the intelligent worked-example condition than in the script condition. This pattern is compatible with the cognitive-load rationale underlying worked examples: structured guidance may reduce unproductive search and allow more cognitive resources to be directed toward learning-relevant processing (Renkl, 2014; Sweller & Cooper, 1985; Sweller et al., 1998). However, this interpreta-tion remains tentative, particularly because the extraneous cognitive load scale showed comparatively low internal consistency. Students’ evaluations of the learning environments provide clearer evidence that the instructional formats differed in their perceived usability. The intelligent worked example was rated significantly more usable than the lecture script, with a large pairwise effect. Perceived educational value showed the same descriptive ordering but did not differ significantly between groups. Thus, the strongest evidence favoring the AI-supported worked example over conventional script-based support concerns the user experience rather than immediate learning performance. The delayed assessment adds a further, but more difficult to interpret, pattern. At follow-up, students in the static worked-example condition scored significantly higher than those in the lecture-script condition, whereas the intelligent and static worked-example groups did not differ significantly. This finding is compatible with the possibility that structured example study supported more durable learning. It does not, however, establish such an effect. Changes from posttest to follow-up did not differ significantly between groups, participation in the follow-up was selective, and the longitudinal sample was reduced from 86 to 57 students. Moreover, the ordering of the groups observed at follow-up was already partly visible at pretest within this reduced subsample. Pre-existing differences, selective attrition, subsequent self-study, 23 and other course-related learning experiences therefore provide plausible alternative explanations. Overall, the comparison with the lecture script suggests that structured worked-example support may influence how students engage with and experience physics problem solving even when this does not translate into a uniform advantage in imme-diate test performance. The findings are consistent with established worked-example theory, but they do not demonstrate that worked examples produced larger learning gains than conventional course material under the present conditions. 

5.1.2 Limited evidence for added value beyond the static worked example 

The comparison between the intelligent and static worked examples provides the more stringent test of the additional value of generative AI. Both materials shared the same solution structure, instructional explanations, and activation prompts; the intelligent version additionally offered conversational support. In terms of the ISAR framework, this comparison therefore most closely reflects an augmentation of an established instructional format rather than a wholesale redefinition of the learning activity (Bauer et al., 2025). Descriptively, the intelligent worked-example group showed the largest pre–post increase in response accuracy and the highest normalized gain. However, the time ×

group interaction did not reach statistical significance, change scores did not differ significantly between the intelligent and static worked-example groups, and posttest performance was comparable. In addition, the static worked-example group entered the intervention with a descriptively higher pretest score, leaving less room for nor-malized improvement. The larger gain in the intelligent condition should therefore be interpreted as a descriptive pattern rather than evidence that conversational AI produced greater learning. The test combination analysis points in the same direction. Greater weighting of the explanatory component increased the pre–post effect size in both worked-example groups. The pattern distinguishing worked-example learning from script-based learn-ing was therefore already present in the absence of conversational AI. This suggests that structured solution steps, conceptual explanations, and activation prompts may account for part of the observed emphasis on explanatory reasoning, while the present data do not isolate an additional AI-specific contribution. Similarly, self-efficacy increased strongly in both worked-example groups, but neither the change nor the posttest level differed significantly between them. Conver-sational support therefore did not produce a detectable additional effect on students’ confidence in applying Gauss’s law. The learning-process measures provide somewhat more favorable, although still inconclusive, indications. The intelligent worked-example condition showed the most favorable descriptive cognitive-load profile, and adding conversational functionality did not appear to impose an obvious usability cost. On the contrary, the intelligent worked example received the highest descriptive usability ratings and was rated significantly more usable than the lecture script. Yet the intelligent and static worked examples 24 did not differ significantly in the separate cognitive-load measures or perceived edu-cational value. These findings are consistent with previous evidence that responsive AI support can be integrated into physics learning without necessarily outperforming well-structured non-AI support (Becker et al., 2026). The central conclusion from RQ1 is therefore not that conversational AI improved learning beyond the static worked example. Rather, the intelligent worked example provided a usable and favorably experienced form of support without a detectable addi-tional learning advantage under the present conditions. This distinction is important because the instructional value of generative AI cannot be inferred simply from favor-able user evaluations or from comparison with less structured conventional materials. A well-designed non-AI alternative provides a considerably more stringent benchmark. 

## 5.2 RQ2 – Choice of learning materials and AI-related concerns 

RQ2 shifts the perspective from assigned learning conditions to voluntary material choice. This distinction is important because a learning environment can provide useful instructional opportunities without becoming the resource students prefer when they independently decide how to complete a course-related task. 

5.2.1 Favorable usability does not translate into voluntary uptake 

The most striking finding from the exploration phase is the limited voluntary uptake of the intelligent worked example. During the intervention, the AI-supported format had received high usability ratings and showed several favorable descriptive patterns. Nevertheless, when students later selected support for an Amp` ere’s-law homework problem, the static worked example was used by a majority of students, whereas the intelligent worked example was selected only rarely. None of the students originally assigned to the intelligent worked-example condition reported using it exclusively during the exploration phase. This discrepancy indicates that favorable experiences under assigned conditions do not necessarily predict voluntary adoption. Similar patterns have been reported for other educational AI systems. For example, only a minority of students used an optional LLM-based tutor when it was made available in a large computer science course (Bassner et al., 2024). The present findings extend this observation by showing that limited uptake may persist even after students have previously encountered and positively evaluated an AI-supported learning environment. One plausible explanation concerns the immediate purpose of material use. Dur-ing the exploration phase, students were completing a graded homework problem. The static worked example provided direct access to a complete and structured solution path. The intelligent worked example, in contrast, invited additional interaction and required engagement with activation questions. When the immediate goal is efficient task completion, students may prefer a resource that provides the required informa-tion with minimal interaction, even if a conversational environment offers additional opportunities for elaboration and individualized support. This interpretation would reconcile the low voluntary uptake with the favorable usability observed during the intervention. Features that are instructionally intended 25 to elicit productive cognitive activity may also require time and effort. Students’ choice of learning support may therefore reflect not only perceived educational value but also the perceived relation between effort and utility in the immediate task context. This possibility was not directly measured in the present study and should therefore be regarded as an interpretation for future investigation. The frequent selection of the static worked example consequently highlights the importance of comparing generative AI not only with conventional instruction but also with strong non-AI alternatives. Additional conversational functionality may provide useful learning opportunities without necessarily making a resource more attractive for voluntary use. 

5.2.2 AI-related concerns and perceived barriers provide limited explanation for material choice 

Students nevertheless expressed meaningful concerns about AI-supported learning. The strongest concern was that AI use might reduce their ability to solve prob-lems independently, an issue that is particularly relevant in university physics, where independent problem solving represents an important educational objective. Similar concerns about dependence on generative AI and the possible erosion of skills have been reported elsewhere (Sallam et al., 2024). These concerns, however, did not systematically correspond to students’ material choices. Students who used the intelligent worked example did not report significantly different concern levels from those who did not, and no significant differences emerged among the four material-choice groups. Perceived barriers were lower overall and like-wise showed no relationship with material choice. Together with the high usability ratings from the intervention phase, this provides little indication that practical dif-ficulties in using the AI-supported environment were the main reason for its limited uptake. Concerns and perceived barriers were also only weakly related to one another. This suggests that reservations about the consequences of AI-supported learning and difficulties associated with using an AI system represent different aspects of stu-dents’ perceptions. A student may find an AI-supported learning environment easy to use while nevertheless questioning whether reliance on it is beneficial for developing independent competence. The absence of a clear relationship between the measured attitudes and material choice suggests that additional factors may be important. These may include the immediate task goal, prior experience with generative AI, AI literacy, or students’ ability to recognize when conversational support is useful. Because these characteristics were not assessed, their role remains an open question. More broadly, the findings indicate that voluntary adoption of AI-supported learning cannot be explained solely by general attitudes toward AI or by perceived usability. 

## 5.3 Limitations 

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of the findings. First, although recita-tion groups were randomly assigned to the three conditions, students had selected 26 these groups beforehand, and the analyses did not account for dependencies within recitation groups. With only three clusters per condition and fewer than 30 students in each group, pre-existing differences cannot be excluded despite the absence of sta-tistically significant baseline differences. Causal interpretations of group differences should therefore remain cautious. Second, the study did not capture students’ actual use of the learning materials. No process or interaction-log data were available to determine how intensively students engaged with the worked examples, activation prompts, or chatbot, or to evaluate the AI-generated responses themselves. The study therefore assesses the effect of making different forms of support available rather than the effects of particular patterns of use. Third, interpretation of the follow-up results is limited by selective participation and the uncontrolled four-week interval. The longitudinal sample decreased from N =86 to N = 57, and group differences visible at follow-up were already partly present at pretest within this reduced sample. The additional students who completed only the follow-up constituted a non-equivalent reference group. Consequently, the observed follow-up differences cannot be interpreted as delayed causal effects of the intervention. Finally, the exploration phase was observational and based on relatively small and unequal groups. Material use was self-reported, and AI-related concerns and perceived barriers were measured only after students had selected and used the materials. These measures therefore cannot be interpreted as predictors of material choice. Together with the use of multiple outcomes and the moderate sample size, these limitations suggest that individual significant findings should be interpreted as part of the overall pattern rather than in isolation. 

# 6 Conclusion 

This study examined the instructional value and voluntary uptake of an intelligent worked example in undergraduate electrodynamics by comparing it with a corre-sponding static worked example and the regular lecture script. Students in all three conditions improved their conceptual understanding of Gauss’s law and self-efficacy, but these changes did not differ significantly between groups. The intelligent worked example showed favorable patterns in perceived cognitive load and was rated highly usable, yet it did not produce a statistically detectable learning advantage over the static worked example. Differences observed at the four-week follow-up cannot be attributed confidently to the intervention because of selective participation and pre-existing differences within the reduced sample. A different picture emerged when students could choose their learning support. Most selected the static worked example, whereas the intelligent worked example was used only rarely. Thus, favorable experiences with an AI-supported learning environment did not translate directly into voluntary adoption. Although students expressed substantial concerns about possible consequences of AI use for independent problem solving, these concerns, as well as perceived barriers, were not significantly associated with material choice. Instructional effectiveness, perceived usability, and voluntary uptake should therefore be treated as distinct dimensions when evaluating AI-supported learning environments. 27 The three-group design further illustrates why the educational value of genera-tive AI should be assessed not only against conventional course materials but also against strong non-AI alternatives that preserve the underlying instructional design. Under the present conditions, conversational AI provided a usable additional layer of support, but its incremental educational value beyond a well-designed static worked example remained uncertain. Future research should therefore move beyond asking whether AI-supported learning is generally effective and examine how learners actually use conversational support, under which task conditions it provides benefits beyond established instructional approaches, and what determines whether students choose to use it.
