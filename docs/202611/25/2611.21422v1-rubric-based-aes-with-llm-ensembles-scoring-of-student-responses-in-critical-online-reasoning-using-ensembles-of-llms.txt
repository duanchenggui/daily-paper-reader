Ensembles of LLMs

Abstract
Grading student responses is costly and time-consuming. Moreover, hu-
man annotators are prone to bias, fatigue, and rater drift, which further
limits grading reliability. Various methods have been employed to develop
automated essay scoring (AES) systems to reduce these costs and address
bias-related issues. These methods range from early text-feature-based sys-
tems to current machine-learning and transformer-based models. Specifically,
large language models (LLMs) offer the potential to streamline the develop-
ment of AES systems. However, they are subject to well-known limitations,
such as hallucinations and prompt sensitivity. This creates a need to ex-
plore the potential of a mixed-methods approach that leverages the synergies
among individual LLMs through ensemble formation.
We evaluate the potential of ensembles of LLMs to match human-rater
agreement when grading student responses in economics and medical educa-
tion across 103 criteria. We use 631 German-language responses and corre-
sponding human ratings by 2 to 5 annotators from a previously conducted
assessment.

1. Introduction
The grading or scoring of human-written texts, such as responses to tasks
in higher education, is a central component of educational research, but it is
also a lengthy and costly process [1, 2, 3, 4]. Human raters exhibit biases such
as halo effects, fatigue, and inconsistency, especially when evaluation rubrics
become more fine-grained and large-scale assessment is required [5, 6, 7, 8].
Consequently, the need for automated essay scoring (AES) systems in educa-
tion has long been recognised [9, 10, 11]. This is supported by the fact that
automation and machine learning (ML) have been used in education for a
variety of tasks [12]. Such methods not only lower costs but also enable appli-
cations such as real-time feedback during the writing process [13, 14]. More-
over, the success of large language models (LLMs), driven by their strong
performance and ease of use through natural language, has led to extensive
research and practical adoption of machine-learning methods [15, 16].
This raises the question of whether LLM-based approaches can perform
scoring tasks: while they may reduce human biases, they introduce biases of
their own stemming from the LLM and underlying training data, thereby po-
tentially compromising scoring results [17, 18]. Research, particularly beyond
the field of education, highlights these limitations: Zhou et al. [19], Wang
et al. [20] evaluate LLMs for paper reviewing and participant simulation,
reporting problems with long texts and harmful misrepresentation when hu-
mans are replaced by LLMs. In this vein, Ma [21] shows that “assigning a
human-like identity to LLMs does not produce human-like behaviors”, while
Piot et al. [22] suggest that, although LLMs are no substitute for humans,
they can produce similar results in terms of relative rankings.
Neverthe-
less, LLMs are widely used for annotation and assessment tasks, that is, as
‘LLMs as judges’; see, for example, LLM-Eval by Lin and Chen [23], MT-
2


Bench, Chatbot Arena [24], and the surveys by Gu et al. [25], Li et al. [26],
which provide overviews of relevant fields and applications. Research more
often highlights the opportunities and strong performance of LLMs. They
span a wide range of domains, both in education [27, 28] and beyond, for
example in medicine and healthcare [29, 30, 31], law [32], and finance [33].
Consequently, we face a dilemma: although LLMs continue to evolve and
are increasingly studied for their potential to complement or replace human
linguistic abilities, their limitations are also becoming clearer, calling their
broad applicability into question. Despite their widespread use and consider-
able potential, further research is needed to disentangle their strengths and
limitations, especially in the assessment of human-produced work. This is
exactly the task of the present article, with a focus on AES.
More specifically, we examine LLMs as means for rubric-based scoring of
responses in the context of critical online reasoning (COR). In COR research,
students are assessed on their ability to gather online information to com-
plete a task by writing a response to a question, with COR examined across
three phases or facets: (1) how they select sources and by which methods
(OIA, online information acquisition), (2) how well they assess source relia-
bility (CIE, critical information evaluation), and (3) their reasoning skills, as
reflected in their response (REAS, reasoning with evidence, argumentation,
and synthesis) [34]. With a focus on the third facet, REAS, we study the
potential of using LLMs for the automated scoring of responses using previ-
ously collected responses and ratings from assessments in German economics
and medical education. We provide empirical evidence on whether LLMs can
replace individual human annotators while matching their level of agreement,
i.e., without lowering the agreement of the rater group. Thus, our construct
is COR performance, while we examine student responses as items and the
features attributed to them by LLMs as observable indicators.
In this study, we address the following research questions:
RQ1 To what extent can LLM-based AES systems replace individual hu-
man raters in grading student responses while maintaining the level of
agreement among the raters?
RQ2 How robust are LLM-based AES systems across eight tasks spanning
the domains of economics and medicine?
RQ3 Which prompt- and model-based features have the greatest influence
on the performance of LLM-based AES systems?

2. Related Work
2.1. Background on AES
Automated essay scoring (AES) is the task of automatically grading writ-
ten texts according to one or more assessment criteria.
See Shermis and
Burstein [35], Lim et al. [36] for overviews of AES systems and methods.
A closely related task is automatic short-answer grading (ASAG); see Bur-
rows et al. [37], Haller et al. [38] for overviews. Based on the criteria for
short answers [37], the statements we analyse in this study share charac-
teristics of short answers, as they require external knowledge, are written
in natural language, and are assessed mostly on content, but they are less
restricted in length and openness. We therefore consider relevant research
from both areas. Starting with early AES systems such as Project Essay
Grade (PEG) [39], researchers first studied feature-engineering-based ap-
proaches [40, 41, 42, 43, 44] before increasingly relying on neural networks
that perform feature engineering as part of the training process [45, 46, 47, 48,
49]. Rather than relying solely on the latest developments in LLMs, several
approaches use a wider range of machine-learning methods to perform AES.
Wilson et al. [50] recently studied the automatic scoring of students’ scien-
tific argumentation skills without the use of LLMs, relying on an ensemble of
machine-learning algorithms. While they do not compare their results with
LLM-based approaches, their approach achieves strong performance while
avoiding LLM-specific limitations such as hallucinations. However, it still
exhibits bias, for example, by assigning lower ratings to English learners,
and requires more effort and expert knowledge to prepare.
With the use of LLMs, the preparation and development of such tools can
potentially be shifted to prompt engineering, while natural language as an
interface makes these systems more accessible to non-experts. The ability to
instruct LLMs in natural language and obtain explanations is also identified
as an advantage by Gao et al. [51]. They caution against focusing on En-
glish, especially in light of the limited amount of training data available for
low-resource languages in LLMs. Emirtekin [52] presents a review of LLM
use for automated assessment, giving an overview of domains, datasets and
4


languages. At the same time, newer reasoning models can reduce the need for
specialised fine-tuning [53, 54, 55, 56]. Current advances in multimodal [57],
agentic [58], and workflow-based AI [59, 60] make LLMs increasingly capa-
ble of scoring not only text data, but also image data (see vision language
models, and, more generally, multimodal LLMs [61, 62]) and process data
such as visited websites and keystrokes [63]. Although these developments
are outside the scope of the present study, they indicate how LLM-based
assessment may develop beyond written statements alone.
In light of these developments, the prospect of AES increasingly based on
LLMs arises. However, fundamental problems such as hallucinations remain
unresolved, especially for LLMs. This raises the question of whether com-
bining LLMs with prompting strategies can produce scoring results suitable
for supporting or even replacing human scorers. In other words, can LLMs
serve as scorers or annotators alongside humans? This question is the central
focus of this article.
2.2. LLM-based Annotation
The potential of using LLMs such as ChatGPT for annotation tasks has
been demonstrated across different domains and tasks, although performance
relative to human annotators varies: Gilardi et al. [64] show that ChatGPT-
3.5 outperforms crowdworkers, but not trained annotators, whereas Törnberg
[65] reports ChatGPT-4 outperforming both. However, Tavakoli and Zamani
[66] identify calibration issues and prompt sensitivity as persistent problems
even in recent models such as ChatGPT-4o and Claude 3. They therefore
propose a human-in-the-loop workflow to improve the reliability of LLM-
based annotation. Similarly, Wang et al. [67] use human-rated gold data
to verify LLM-generated annotations and report improved accuracy after
re-annotating failed annotations by human raters.
Borse et al. [68] investigate the replacement of a human rater.
They
compare ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-4.5 with the combined ratings of two
human raters and report reliability in terms of Cohen’s kappa. They conclude
that LLMs can replace human raters in the qualitative analysis of transcripts,
but “have limitations in coding themes that are not domain-specific”. Other
studies caution against replacing humans with LLMs, including Clarke and
Dietz [69] and Shen et al. [70]. The latter conclude that, while models like
ChatGPT and Llama can outperform other automated systems, they cannot
fully replace humans, and report problems with LLMs’ ability to distinguish
5


between results with similar performance, model bias, and decreasing corre-
lation with human judgments for higher-quality summaries.
Model bias in automated annotation is not unique to LLM-based meth-
ods. Amorim et al. [17] show that feature-based machine-learning models
trained on data from which human-rater bias has been removed perform bet-
ter. For LLM-based annotation, Weidmann et al. [71] find that Llama 3.1
and ChatGPT-4o are able to perform annotation and approximate human
judgments, but that the models’ bias is clearly noticeable and affects the
annotation. They show that using an ensemble method is not sufficient on
its own, but that combining two LLMs with opposing biases improves the
results. The responsible use of LLMs, and AI more generally, is studied by
Johnson and Zhang [72], who observe that the average score assigned by the
LLM is lower than that assigned by the human annotators. The authors
therefore conclude that evaluation should include fairness and explainabil-
ity. Similarly, Chen et al. [73] show that both humans and LLMs produce
significant biases, although these biases differ and vary in degree.
Taken together, these findings strongly suggest the need for human-based
validation of LLM-based annotation, even though human judgement is itself
not free from biases and should be taken into account when evaluating LLM-
based approaches. The findings motivate the approach in our study to com-
pare multiple LLMs and prompts with individual human ratings. We discuss
the potential and use of LLM-based tools and methods for AES specifically
in the next section.
2.3. LLM-based AES
Research on the assessment of written responses in education in both AES
and ASAG also shows ambivalent results. In 2024, Chamieh et al. [74] analyse
the use of LLMs to automatically score short-answer responses. They use
existing datasets such as ASAP in their evaluation and test GPT-3.5, GPT-
4, and Llama. The authors report low performance, which they were able to
improve by fine-tuning the LLMs. They conclude that there “currently seems
to be no basis for applying LLMs for short-answer scoring”, especially when
tasks require domain knowledge or complex reasoning. Using Llama 2 and
ChatGPT-3.5, Mansour et al. [75] evaluate LLM performance in automated
essay scoring on the ASAP dataset and compare it with conventional non-
LLM systems. They show the importance of prompt engineering for each
task and LLM separately and observe differences in prompt performance
between ChatGPT and Llama. Additionally, Llama’s performance fluctuates
6


much more than ChatGPT’s, further highlighting the differences between
more open and more closed LLMs. Compared with non-LLM systems, they
report much lower performance for both LLMs, highlighting the limitations
of models from just a few years ago. Conversely, in a different assessment
setting, Latif and Zhai [76] report an average performance increase of 9.1%
when comparing a fine-tuned GPT-3.5 model with a BERT-based approach
in an assessment task in science education.
Since these studies, multiple LLM generations have significantly improved
models’ reasoning abilities. Reasoning, as manifested, for example, in chain-
of-thought (CoT) prompting [77, 55], concerns the models’ ability to break a
task down into subtasks and elaborate on them in order to arrive at an overall
solution, thereby giving the models greater control over response generation.
Regardless of whether reasoning is explicitly elicited by prompting, newer
models are trained to generate reasoning traces or argumentation chains to
produce better answers; the approaches further discussed in this section focus
on such models.
Yancey et al. [78] compare ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 with human an-
notators using essays written by English learners and report that ChatGPT-4
performs on a par with conventional models. Very recently, Cong et al. [79]
also report performance matching or exceeding that of BERT-based models.
Lee et al. [80] compare GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in scoring science assessments
completed by U.S. middle school students and graded by human raters. They
observe that there was no single best-performing prompt across all tasks and
that LLMs are capable of generating explainable and interpretable ratings,
while the use of CoT prompting and few-shot examples improves accuracy.
Similarly, using student-writing assessment data from young students in the
U.S., Huang and Wilson [81] evaluate GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Claude
Sonnet 4 and compare them with ratings produced by trained professionals.
Their findings confirm that structured prompts and few-shot examples im-
prove Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) alignment [82, 83] and can achieve
performance comparable to feature-based systems, which they report remain
“the most reliable”.
The effects of rubric design are demonstrated by Frohn et al. [84], who
compare human ratings with ratings generated by 11 LLMs, including Claude,
Gemini, ChatGPT, and Llama variants, on short-answer questions in mathe-
matics and English. They score answers as correct or incorrect and evaluate
three prompts that provide (1) only the question, (2) the question plus rat-
ing criteria, and (3) examples. Although the authors investigate ASAG, their
7


comparison of multiple LLMs and different rubric information is directly rele-
vant to our multi-model rubric-based experiment setting. They observe that
the group of LLMs showed higher agreement reliability among themselves
than the human raters. Results are reported using Fleiss’ kappa and show
that LLM performance depends on rubric design: the more information pro-
vided in the prompt, the higher the reliability.
This phenomenon is also investigated in the AES field by Yoshida [85],
who use the TOEFL11 dataset, Claude 3.5 Haiku, ChatGPT-4o-mini, Gem-
ini 1.5 Flash, and Llama 3, along with three rubric variants. Except for Gem-
ini, they report higher QWK scores with more informative prompts; however,
the differences are small and not significant. They conclude that Gemini’s
lower performance may result from the longer prompt. While prompt differ-
ences were small, absolute scores reached only around 0.6, leading them to
conclude that AES using LLMs “is not yet ready for practical use”.
Kortemeyer [86] further compares the general-purpose LLM ChatGPT-4
with specialised or fine-tuned models for automated short-answer grading.
They show that ChatGPT does not match the performance of specialised
models; however, they note the advantage of not having to train the LLM.
Additionally, they test two variants, one of which provides the LLM with a
reference answer and yields comparable performance.
Shibata and Miyamura [87] study automated essay scoring by asking
LLMs to rank essay pairs rather than assign scores directly. The final score
is then computed by training RankNet [88] on multiple comparison pairs and
linearly mapping its output to the rubric value range. The method is evalu-
ated on two datasets using five LLMs: ChatGPT-4o and 4o-mini, Llama 3.1
and 3.2, and Mistral. It is found to outperform other prompting-based meth-
ods in most cases.
Working with student data requires great care with regard to data pro-
tection and privacy. Stasuik [89] compares locally hosted LLMs (Llama 3.1
and 3.2) with closed-weight commercial models (ChatGPT-4o-mini, o1, and
o3-mini) for scoring essays from English courses. Using mean absolute grade
difference, Stasuik shows that none of the tested models can replace human
assessment and that LLMs often assign lower scores than human raters. In
that study, newer LLMs with larger context windows performed better. Lo-
cally hosted models performed much worse; however, the newer Llama version
showed substantial improvement, suggesting that future models may become
more capable.
Emirtekin and Özarslan [90] study agreement between human annota-
8


tors and four closed- and open-weight LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2 Flash,
DeepSeek V3, and Llama 3.3) using university students’ short-answer texts.
They report moderate agreement in terms of the QWK metric, with much
higher agreement among the LLMs themselves.
Further, they show that
LLMs can efficiently grade lower-order cognitive skills, with criteria target-
ing factual and definitional aspects showing higher agreement than those re-
quiring inference or contextual awareness. However, Emirtekin and Özarslan
conclude that fully automated assessment is limited when higher-order skills
are considered and that LLMs cannot replace human raters in this case.
In this way, Tate et al. [91] report positive results for holistic AES using
ChatGPT-3.5 and 4 and find no statistically significant difference between
humans and ChatGPT.
In medical education, Quah et al. [92] highlight the importance of rubric
design and note that ChatGPT was unable to identify incorrect statements or
irrelevant content. They compare human and ChatGPT-4 performance using
IAA and correlation measures. Their results vary by assessed question but
still show potential for LLM-based automated scoring. Similarly, Deng et al.
[93] show that alignment with human ratings decreases as criteria become
more complex, from binary to more fine-grained criteria.
This research often demonstrates a limited assessment potential of LLMs.
That potential depends on various factors, including model maturity and
size. However, the use of large commercial models raises significant data-
protection concerns.
Overall, LLMs show greater potential for assessing
lower-order than higher-order cognitive skills. Based on the current state
of research, LLM-based AES systems cannot be recommended without reser-
vation for the latter.
This interim finding raises several questions that we address in this ar-
ticle: since LLM-based scoring cannot currently replace human scoring and
remains limited in the range of cognitive skills it can assess, the question
arises whether LLMs are better suited to supplement human raters. This
would require adapting the models to specific scoring criteria, either through
fine-tuning or specialised prompting techniques, provided that these criteria
do not merely target low-level skills. However, for data-protection reasons,
only non-commercial models that are available locally and do not transmit
any data to external providers can be considered for this purpose. Conse-
quently, the next section reviews related work on rater evaluation: given a
low IAA among raters, supplementing human raters becomes a challenging
task.

2.4. Rater Evaluation
A general overview of rater evaluation through the measurement of agree-
ment among annotators is presented by Artstein and Poesio [94], covering,
e.g., Krippendorff’s alpha or Cohen’s kappa. A more recent line of research
examines how annotator agreement and reliability can be measured and in-
terpreted, especially when using inexperienced raters or crowdworkers. In
such cases, reliance on absolute inter-rater metrics or thresholds, such as
Krippendorff’s alpha, is considered problematic by Wong et al. [95]. They
present a novel measure based on Cohen’s kappa, named cross-replication
reliability (xRR). In the same way, Andersen et al. [96] compare a set of
measures in the context of evaluating AES systems and propose an evalu-
ation method that considers acceptable ranges when multiple annotations
are available. These findings show that measures and thresholds should be
contextualised, e.g., through replication, rater experience, and the purpose
of comparison. In our study, we use rater agreement on human- and LLM-
assigned ratings. For this reason, we rely on the Alternative Annotator Test,
which is presented below, as a complementary measure.
When no human-generated gold data are available, for example because
of cost, another method is to use an LLM to judge the outputs of multiple
LLMs and generate a final result [97].
These judges, however, still have
to be evaluated against human annotations to determine their performance.
Guerdan et al. [97] study such judge systems under the assumption that
there is not always a single correct gold annotation, or that one annotator
may regard multiple ratings as correct. They find that such systems differ
both from one another and from the human annotators.
Wang et al. [98] present an evaluation framework based on Generaliz-
ability Theory (G-theory) and Many-Faceted Rasch Measurement (MFRM).
Notably, the authors report results comparing Claude (3.5 Haiku and Son-
net) with the widely used ChatGPT models (4o and 4o-mini). Claude out-
performs the ChatGPT models and is reported to perform on a par with
fine-tuned ChatGPT variants, further highlighting the rapid pace of model
development. Using the ASAP datasets, the authors show that LLMs per-
form well in relative ranking, but are limited in absolute scoring. Compared
with humans, who perform strongly on holistic scoring, LLMs significantly
outperform them on analytic scoring; however, both are affected by biases
such as halo effects.
The Alternative Annotator Test (alt-test), which we use for comparison
in our evaluation, is presented by Calderon et al. [99]. It is a statistical ap-
10


proach for evaluating whether human annotators can be replaced by LLMs
and whether an LLM “offers a better alternative to recruiting human annota-
tors”. Calderon et al. [99] position the alt-test as a more rigorous metric for
comparing an LLM with a group of human annotators than measures such
as IAA, which assess the group as a whole. Like IAA, the alt-test assesses
alignment with the human group rather than correctness. Calderon et al.
[99] apply the alt-test to six LLMs (ChatGPT-4o and 4o-mini, Gemini 1.5
Flash and Pro, Llama 3.1, and Mistral) and human annotators with differ-
ent skill levels. Based on the test, they show that at least one LLM can
replace human annotators in nine of the ten datasets, and that closed-weight
models outperform locally hosted ones. This insight is particularly relevant
in our context, as it highlights the challenges associated with our focus on
local models, which are largely indispensable for data-protection reasons. In
other words, achieving satisfactory alt-test results with local open-weight
models would demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in this setting.
Because the alt-test is a recent method with limited independent validation,
we use it as a complementary evaluation measure in combination with IAA
for evaluating replaceability.
2.5. Summary
Overall, the research shows the potential of LLMs for AES and highlights
the need for extensive model testing and prompt engineering. It also cautions
against overreliance on LLM capabilities, suggesting that any fully automated
approach must be validated by human raters in their intended assessment
setting for each combination of model, task, rubric or criterion, and prompt.
In this article, we study how a range of open, locally hosted LLMs from
different families rate student answers and examine, among other things,
whether they can supplement or even replace human annotators using inter-
annotator agreements and the alt-test.
Compared with other studies, we
evaluate previously collected, real-world responses in German, a less-studied
language, and economics, a less-studied domain. That is, we explicitly in-
vestigate the possibility of replacing human raters with local models in this
specific application area. We show that, under these conditions, individual
human raters can indeed be replaced without lowering the agreement among
the raters, even though previous research has generally reported negative
results for local models. This provides valuable insight into the potential of
rapidly advancing research on automating human capabilities in areas heavily
affected by data-privacy concerns.

3. Methods
We analyse the potential to replace human annotators by prompting
LLMs to automatically rate students’ statements against a set of prede-
fined criteria. Our approach combines a series of local models with various
criterion-specific prompting strategies. This allows us to achieve the contex-
tual sensitivity essential to our approach. We first present the application
domain and the data analysed in it (subsection 3.1). We then describe the
LLMs we evaluate and the prompting strategies we partly developed and
tested (subsection 3.2). Figure 1 summarises our approach graphically.
3.1. Data and Rubrics
We consider real-world statements as written responses to questions posed
to economics and social-science students (“economics” group), as well as
medicine and physics (“medicine” group) students, during an online assess-
ment of online reasoning in higher education in the Critical Online Reasoning
in Higher Education (CORE) project [100, 101]. The assessment was con-
ducted independently of this study. As part of the original assessment, stu-
dents were asked to search online for sources to support a written response,
which was then graded by a subset of two to five trained raters. Searching
and evaluating online sources is part of the assessed construct [34, 102]; the
students were not supplied with any curated reading materials beyond the
task and scenario. The questions are divided into two domains, economics
and medicine, each containing four tasks. We study the responses in the prac-
tical context (also referred to as clinical reasoning in medicine) in the CORE
assessment. The four economics tasks require students to answer questions
that ask for arguments both for and against a concept or topic introduced in
a scenario text:
• Nudging (NU) asks for arguments regarding the use of personalised
nudging to save energy.
• Pilot strike (PS) asks for arguments regarding the issue of pay gaps
and different pay scales for pilots.
• Startup (SU) asks for arguments regarding the key economic criteria
for selecting a location when relocating a startup.

The grading rubric comprises the following two task-independent criteria:
1. The FILTER criterion assesses whether the student engaged seriously
with the task and did not entirely miss the subject matter.
This
Boolean criterion is formulated as a “yes/no” question, with ‘no’ en-
coded as 0.
2. The COHERENCE criterion relates to the assessment of the response’s co-
herence, asking whether the student’s argumentation is coherent, con-
sistent, and free of contradictions. It is based on a discrete four-point
scale from 0 to 3, where 0 is the lowest rating, that is, the response is
completely incoherent.
We further consider the following five economics criteria in our analysis:
1. The Boolean REFERENCE criterion asks whether the student’s written
statement refers to the problem situation.
2. The Boolean CONCLUSION criterion asks whether the student’s written
statement reaches a clear conclusion.
3. The Boolean ARGUMENTATION criterion asks whether the student’s writ-
ten statement contains coherent economic argumentation. Note that
this criteria was not rated for the Pilot strike task. This task there-
fore only considers four specific criteria.
4. The ARGUMENTS_COUNT criterion asks whether the student’s written
statement contains comprehensible arguments. It counts up to four
arguments and is therefore based on a discrete five-point scale from
0 to 4, where 0 is the lowest rating, indicating that no argument is
provided.
17


5. The Boolean SPECIFIC_ARGUMENTS criterion asks whether the student’s
statement mentions both arguments for and against personalised nudg-
ing.
The rubric used to assess the economics tasks contains criteria that can be
grouped into the following three categories: The first category is concerned
with formal criteria, for which the rater needs to check for the existence of
specific elements. These criteria essentially act as filters at different levels:
(1) Has the student made an actual attempt to work on this task? (FIL-
TER criterion); (2) if the student has worked earnestly on the task, does their
response consider the stated problem? (REFERENCE); and (3) does the student
formulate a conclusion, or does the response remain unresolved? (CONCLU-
SION). The second category is concerned with the structure of the response.
This maps to the two non-Boolean criteria in the economics tasks: the rater
needs to consider the text in full and construct some form of argumentative
structure rather than only checking for the presence of specific elements.
They have to quantify the coherence (COHERENCE criterion) on a predefined
scale and count the number of arguments made (ARGUMENTS_COUNT). Finally,
the third category assesses domain knowledge.
Unlike the other criteria,
which can mostly be graded without specific domain knowledge, raters need
to apply their subject knowledge to judge whether students have correctly
done so in case of the ARGUMENTATION and SPECIFIC_ARGUMENTS criteria.
Depending on the particular task, 12 to 20 task-specific criteria are rated
for the medicine tasks, including the two shared criteria. All of these criteria
are Boolean criteria formulated as “yes/no” questions.
1. The A1 criterion asks whether the student has identified the most likely
differential diagnosis.
2. The B* criteria ask for additional mentioned differential diagnoses. For
the Circulation task, there are up to seven B* criteria.
3. The C* criteria differ by task: in task Breathing, they ask whether
measures indicated for acute care are listed; in Eye, whether tests to rule
out any incorrect differential diagnoses are mentioned; in Circulation,
whether an explanation of affected organs and systems is given; and in
Middle ear, whether at least one necessary test to confirm the sus-
pected diagnosis is provided in the response.

4. The D* criteria ask for tests to rule out or confirm differential diagnoses
(Eye and Middle ear), treatment options (Breathing), and organ-

5. The E* criteria ask whether therapies or measures are mentioned for
all tasks except Eye.
6. The following criteria only apply to the Middle ear task: The F* crite-

complications; and the H* criteria ask whether at least one plausible
sign of complications is mentioned.

of FILTER and COHERENCE, are not as directly comparable as the economics
criteria. Because of the naming scheme, we still present them grouped by
name, similar to the economics criteria, and provide a thematic grouping.
In contrast to the economics tasks, all require domain knowledge. The first
category contains criteria that ask for diagnoses and diagnostic testing. It
asks what the condition is and how it is confirmed or excluded. This group
includes the criteria A, B, C in tasks Eye and Middle ear and D in the same
two tasks. The second category includes criteria that are concerned with
symptoms, complications or clinical features: C and D for Circulation, and
G and H. The final group asks for treatments, or management more generally.
It comprises the remaining criteria: C (Breathing), D (Breathing), E and F.

lowest, or “default”, criterion value is 0.

3.2. LLM-based AES
We employ an LLM-based AES system to rate each student response on
the basis of the respective criteria per task. The LLM receives the student
text, the rating criteria, and optional additional information, and is then
prompted to assign a value to each criterion. In total, we experiment with 15
LLMs of various sizes, using Docker Unified UIMA Interface (DUUI) [118] as
the NLP framework and LangChain [119] and Ollama [120] for LLM process-
ing. All LLMs were queried with the same random seed and a temperature
of 0.8.
We fixed the temperature to the default value of LangChain and
Ollama across all models to provide a common configuration for evaluation
and did not perform model-, prompt-, or task-specific optimization regard-
ing these parameters.
Except for the GPT-OSS models, all models were
instructed to return JSON via the format parameter. Depending on the
prompt, we increase the context size (num_ctx) and leave the number of
generated tokens unrestricted (num_predict = -2) in all cases. The list of
nine studied LLMs in the main study is given in Table 5, and Table 6 shows
the twelve models of the Nudging-only study, six of which also appear in
the main study. Together, they include multiple versions and configurations
of DeepSeek, GPT-OSS, Llama, and Qwen, as well as MiniCPM-V, Mixtral
and Nemotron. Additionally, we include the MedGemma and Plutus LLMs,
which are trained on medical and economic data, respectively.

The prompts are mostly written in German, the language of the state-
ments. An example template for each prompt variant of the main study is
shown in Appendix F. All prompt templates of both studies are provided
as supplementary material, with placeholders for the task texts, scenarios,
rubrics, and student data. In the following, we summarise the prompt vari-
ants. We consider the following prompt variants in the main study.
• SIMPLE SCENARIO The prompts in this category ask the LLM to pro-
duce ratings for all criteria in a single request as a structured, predefined
JSON object. We provide the same information that was available to
human annotators in our annotation tool when they assessed the scor-
ing criteria and their value ranges. This includes the relevant scenario
text for the task question. Additionally, the LLM is informed that the
response comes from a student answering a question within a specific
domain. The prompt assigns the LLM the role of an annotation expert.
This variant serves as the baseline for the other variants, each of which
extends or modifies it.
• CHAIN NOANSWER SCENARIO These prompts make multiple requests to
the model, rating each criterion separately. Criterion definitions are
not provided in advance, but only when each criterion is requested.
However, the model has access to the full message history. This de-
composition relates to the chain-of-thought prompting [54], which has
shown to improve the accuracy of LLM-based scoring [80].
24


• FEATURES SCENARIO This group of prompts builds on SIMPLE SCENARIO
with respect to the definition of criteria and JSON output. Addition-
ally, we provide the LLM with a range of features derived from the
students’ answer texts.
Such text features are the basis of feature-
based AES [40, 41, 42, 43, 44], which remains competitive with LLM-
based scoring approaches [81]. These features include basic statistics
such as token, sentence, unique-token, and lemma counts, as well as
hate-speech and toxicity detection, emotion recognition, sarcasm de-
tection, topic classification, sentiment analysis, and readability mea-
sures. The following natural language processing (NLP) systems are
used to calculate these feature groups: Statistics are derived from pre-
processing the responses with spaCy [121] (efficient model). Additional
features are generated using implementations in the DUUI NLP frame-
work: The readability features comprise nine metrics, including the
Flesch-Kincaid grade level and the Automated Readability Index, and
are calculated using the implementation by DiMascio [122]. We use
text2ddc [123, 124] for topic classification using the Dewey Decimal
Classification scheme, as well as a transformer-based model using the
Media Topic schema of the IPTC [125, 126]. Sentiment analysis is per-
formed using the lexicon- and rule-based tool VADER [127, 128] and
two additional transformer models [129, 130]. All further features are
based on transformer models for detecting hate speech [131, 132], toxi-
city [133, 134], emotions [135, 136], and sarcasm [137]. All of these text
features are provided to the LLM as raw JSON containing the feature
group, model name, and values.
• OPT SCENARIO We study a prompt that was automatically optimized
using Claude Opus 4.7 [138]. Since LLM-based scoring is sensitive to
the prompt and no prompt performs best across tasks [75, 80], we let
the LLM optimize the SIMPLE SCENARIO prompt. In a second variant,
we instruct the LLM to generate a prompt based on all prompt tem-
plates studied in this paper. Claude was selected as a state-of-the-art
model during prompt development and limited to generating the up-
dated prompt. It was used as a complementary proprietary LLM to
the locally-hostable models used in our study. Following the data pro-
tection rules, the proprietary Claude model received only the prompt
templates, without any inserted content such as the training transcripts
or student data. The final prompt was fixed before evaluation and ap-
25


plied to all evaluated models in the study without change.
• TRAINING SCENARIO This prompt category extends SIMPLE SCENARIO
with additional context from a transcript of a rater meeting compris-
ing a general introduction to the assessment that covers the FILTER
and COHERENCE criteria, the annotation tool, and the rating of student
statements. The transcript is included in full in the prompt. Since
prior work reports higher agreement when more detailed rubrics are
provided [84], this variant gives the LLM some of the information that
the human raters received before rating.
In the Nudging-only study, we perform a focused follow-up on the Nudging
task. We use the same prompt classes as described above and test additional
prompts and models. One important distinction is the exclusion of the sce-
nario description. The question for the economics task Nudging is phrased
very directly as key arguments for and against personalized nudging to pro-
mote energy conservation, whereas the other tasks require knowledge from
the scenario text. For this reason, we omit the scenario from the prompts in
this experiment.
• SIMPLE and BASIC: These prompts mirror the SIMPLE SCENARIO prompt
from the main study but exclude the scenario text. We add the BASIC
prompt, an English prompt with a question–answer structure, that
mainly differs in the question posed to the students: The SIMPLE and
all other prompts focus on the written statement of arguments for and
against, whereas BASIC considers the scenario and the online research
needed to produce the answer.
• CHAIN: This prompt uses the CHAIN NOANSWER SCENARIO prompt as
a basis, again without the scenario text. In addition to the criterion
definition, each message also contains the full student response, so that
the answer is always kept in the most recent message. We prompt the
LLM for explicit JSON output and provide the criterion definitions
accordingly; in an alternative version, we do not specify an output
format and instead provide the definitions in plain text.
• FEATURES: Building on the FEATURES SCENARIO prompt group from the
main study, we add three sentence-relation features to the prompt. En-
tailment, justification, and elaboration scores are generated by prompt-
ing an LLM (DeepSeek-R1:70b) to assign a confidence score to each
26


sentence pair, for example, by asking whether one sentence entails the
other. We then use the confidence scores for the first–last and last–
first sentence pairs as two features in the criteria-rating prompt. The
features are provided to the LLM not only as raw JSON but also as
summarised text in this experiment. To this end, we use different LLMs
to generate texts that summarise the most important characteristics of
the student response and its text features. This summary is then used
in the rating prompt instead of the raw feature data.
• OPT: We optimise the SIMPLE prompt using the PE2 approach of Ye
et al. [139] instead of only relying on the zero-shot capabilities of an
LLM for optimisation as in OPT SCENARIO. The PE2 algorithm uses
one LLM to generate prompt variants, which are then automatically
validated by another LLM on a few examples. We run the optimisation
for five iterations, generating five new prompts each time (T=5, n=4,
m=5, k=5) and rank them by the proportion of correctly predicted
criteria. In this way, the OPT prompt uses an optimised system prompt
based on SIMPLE.
• TRAINING: We extend the TRAINING SCENARIO prompt by adding a
transcript of a task-specific presentation of the economics task. In this
experiment, we experiment with providing only the general discussion
or both parts combined, using either the full transcript or an LLM-
generated summary.
As in the main study, the prompts considered in the Nudging-only study

HERENCE and ARGUMENTS_COUNT, using dedicated prompts (37 prompts and

• COHERENCE VERY SIMPLE: We employ different prompts for the coher-

• COHERENCE STRUCT: Three prompts test minor formulation changes,
such as capitalisation and the placement of task context for the response

• COHERENCE STRUCT FEATURES: As with the all-criteria prompts, we in-

• COHERENCE STRUCT TRAINING: As in the previous prompts, we experi-

To account for unbalanced data, that is, not all responses were rated by
all human raters and not all models produced results for all responses, we
use the following strategies to calculate agreement. As a baseline, we treat
missing LLM ratings as if the corresponding criteria had received the lowest
value. This allows us to handle technical problems that may arise during
model querying or output parsing in a predictable manner for this analysis.
This leads to variants for handling the LLM ratings based on the filter rating:
(1) strict, which sets all other criteria to the lowest, or default, value when
the FILTER is false, as described above; and (2) lenient, which retains the
criterion values produced by the LLM. Lenient allows the model to recover
from an incorrectly rated FILTER criterion and can simplify and speed up
prompting, as a single request covers all criteria, reducing costs and improv-
ing performance, for example, in real-time feedback. In the remainder of this
analysis, we only consider the strict FILTER handling, since the ratings are
fully one-sided, i.e., positive for all tasks except economics Nudging. There,
we do not see any differences as the FILTER criterion is still extremely skewed.
We further evaluate whether a human rater can be replaced by an au-
tomatically generated LLM rating by examining inter-annotator agreement
using Krippendorff’s alpha [141], which measures how consistently annota-
tors agree beyond chance.
In this way, we assess how strongly a model
affects IAA and whether replacing a human rater with an LLM results in an
increase or decrease of the agreement in relation to the observed agreement
between the human annotators. A non-negative change in IAA indicates that
an LLM can be substituted for a human while preserving the overall group
agreement. However, it does not make any argument regarding the validity
of the rating itself, because no consolidated gold rating is available. We are
29


analysing whether LLMs have the potential to reach a level of agreement
comparable to the human annotators under the same rubric-based assess-
ment setting. Each human rater is replaced in the analysis. Our objective
is not to identify or approximate a specific individual rater using an LLM-
generated rating, but to evaluate whether LLMs can substitute raters in the
group while preserving overall group agreement. We select IAA for measur-
ing the results as we do not compare the LLM-generated ratings to a single
consolidated gold score, we treat the individual human ratings as equally
plausible and evaluate agreement over the group of humans and LLM-based
raters. Krippendorff’s alpha is used as the main method because it measures
agreement beyond chance, supports more than two raters and handles miss-
ing ratings [94]. These features are relevant to our study because the student
statements were rated by different numbers and combinations of human an-
notators. In comparison, QWK, a weighted form of Cohen’s kappa that is
widely used for evaluating AES, supports primarily two raters, and Fleiss’s
kappa requires equal amounts of ratings per item and assumes randomly
sampled annotators; Krippendorff’s alpha has therefore been recommended
as a general alternative in multi-rater evaluations [82, 142]. However, Krip-
pendorff’s alpha can be sensitive to imbalanced data and more difficult to
interpret. We therefore report unanimous agreement, Cronbach’s alpha and
ICC(3,k) as complementary measures of rater consistency [143]. Additionally,
we test whether the LLMs can replace at least 50% of the human raters at
a statistically significant level by employing the Alternative Annotator Test.
It can be run on a small sample of 50–100 cases with at least three human
annotators, without requiring consolidated gold annotations, and can incor-
porate annotator costs through a cost-benefit parameter. The test compares
each human annotator’s alignment with the rest of the group to the LLM’s
alignment with that same group, tests the difference for each annotator, and
computes the LLM’s win rate from the rejected hypotheses. Following the
authors of the alt-test, we use a cost-benefit parameter of 0.15, which they
present for skilled workers. We consider this skill level to be the closest to the
human annotators who rated the original student statements and addition-
ally assess the dependence of the parameter choice in a sensitivity analysis.
Finally, we take a further step towards full automation by examining changes
in agreement when two human raters are replaced, leaving at least one human
rater as the necessary source of verification. For this analysis, we replace all
combinations of two human raters with all unique model–prompt configura-
tions. In addition to the top-performing configurations, we examine the top
30


10 configurations to evaluate reliable models and prompts overall. That is,
we select the top 10 configurations based on their best performance per task
and criterion, measured by the highest IAA when replacing any of the human
raters, including cases in which the change is negative. We then rank them
by how often they appear in the top 10, grouped by domain and criterion and
evaluate their Jaccard similarity. In this way, we address research questions
RQ1 and RQ2.
3.3. SHAP Analysis
To study the influence of model and prompt choice, we perform a SHAP
(SHapley Additive exPlanations) [144, 145] analysis on a proxy model trained
to predict agreement. For this purpose, we train proxy models that predict
the observed IAA based on the experiment, prompt, and model characteris-
tics as input features. The proxy models are only used as explanatory models
for the SHAP analysis in this study and are not systems designed for pre-
dicting IAA in practical use outside of this study. We experiment with both
regression and classification models, first attempting to predict the actual
IAA value and, second, whether a non-negative change can be achieved. The
33 features include the task and task domain, the LLM and prompt, the LLM
family and size, and the prompt length, style, and output formats. They are
grouped as follows:
1. The model group contains the three model-related features: model size,
family, and full name.
2. The prompt group covers 17 features, including the prompt name, its
class as a categorical feature, and multiple binary features. These en-
code whether the prompt is a chain prompt, contains training material
or few-shot examples, is extended with textual features, contains the
scenario text, is optimized, uses a structured method to provide the
input, and specifies the expected output format.
3. The domain and task names are used in the task feature group, as well
as the criterion itself.
4. The rater group consists of only one feature related to the human
raters and contains the removed rater as a categorical feature.
31


5. The decoding group includes the LLM parameters temperature, for-
mat, and context size.
Note that temperature was the same in all
experiments.
6. The resource feature group summarises LLM application values, i.e.,
the mean, SD, min, and max statistics of the input and output tokens
processed. These values are calculated as averages per task.
We use Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGBT) models for both regres-
sion and classification. The targets are the IAA alpha value and Boolean
non-negative change, respectively.
We include baseline models using the
mean strategy for regression and most frequent strategy for classification.
For numerical features, we use a median imputer and standard scaler; for
categorical features, we use a most frequent imputer and one-hot encoder.
The models are trained using the scikit-learn [146] implementation with de-
fault parameters and all results are obtained using 5-fold cross-validation,
stratified for the classification model. We then perform a SHAP analysis
using its TreeExplainer on the proxy models to determine which experi-
mental characteristics contribute to the LLM performance. Additionally, we
examine the correlation between IAA and the model size as well as the num-
ber of processed tokens using Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients
calculated over the mean sizes per LLM–prompt configuration. This analysis
provides insights into research question RQ3.

by replacing one human rater with LLMs (subsection 4.2). We confirm our
results by using the Alternative Annotator Test as an additional metric (sub-
section 4.3) and then replace two human raters (subsection 4.4). We sum-
marise the results for RQ1 and RQ2 in subsection 4.5. Finally, we present the
results of the SHAP analysis for RQ3 (subsection 4.6) and the consistency
of the LLM-based ratings (subsection 4.7).

4.1. Human Rater Agreement
We present the inter-annotator agreements of the human raters based on
Krippendorff’s alpha [141, 147] using the interval difference function. The
results of the four economics tasks presented in Table 7 show, in some cases,
considerable differences in the agreement for the same criteria across different
tasks. Good agreement, with alpha values over 0.8 [148, 94] is only achieved
for the FILTER criterion in case of the two economics tasks Wind park and
Startup. For task Nudging, the agreement is only tentatively acceptable,
with a value of 0.68. Similarly, the criterion SUM_SCORE in tasks Wind park
and Pilot strike are placed within the same agreement range. The inter-
annotator agreement for the other criteria can be considered unreliable, with
values well below 0.5 in multiple cases. This is particularly evident for CON-
CLUSION in task Nudging, for which the alpha value is close to 0 and thus
only slightly above the level of chance.
We report unanimous rating agreement as an additional metric. In the
event of highly skewed rating distributions, a low IAA may result from dis-
agreement over a small number of instances of under-represented values,
which can have a strong impact on the agreement score.
This is visible
for the FILTER criterion in Pilot strike: here we see an unanimous rating
in 93% of the responses, but an IAA alpha of only 0.15.
The intraclass correlation coefficients ICC(3,k) range from 0.586 to 0.957,
indicating mostly good to excellent inter-rater reliability [149, 150, 151]. The
ICC results generally align with the Krippendorff’s alpha agreements. Sim-
ilarly, Cronbach’s alpha (calculated using the listwise deletion method)
estimates internal item consistency ranging from poor to excellent, with val-
ues from 0.35 to 0.953 [152, 153, 151].
These measurements emphasise the challenges involved in rating the eco-
nomics tasks based on the presented criteria. In our analysis, we acknowledge
that these human ratings differ and that no consolidated gold-standard an-
notation was provided as part of the original assessment data. We therefore
34


evaluate the LLM-based ratings in this multi-rater setting without assuming
or constructing a single gold-standard annotation. Instead, each human rat-
ing is treated as an equally plausible reference annotation, and the LLM is
evaluated by the agreement between the individual raters in the rater group
rather than against a single gold label.
Looking at the three groups of criteria, in the first group of formal criteria,
we notice extremely low human rater agreement on the CONCLUSION criterion
in the Nudging task. In contrast, the other two criteria display the highest
agreement values. This suggests a difficulty or misunderstanding regarding
what qualifies as a conclusion in the context of the task. This appears to
be a consequence of the ratings performed by rater B. However, this seems
to be specific to this task, as the other three tasks show agreement values
comparable with the other criteria. This task-difference is also evident for
the REFERENCE criterion, for which Nudging behaves differently: agreement
is relatively high with 0.648, while the other three tasks display alpha values
around 0.24. In the second group, which contains the structure criteria, no
clear distinction in agreement is evident when comparing the different value
ranges. Despite their larger value range, the two non-Boolean criteria exhibit
IAA scores in the mid-range. The IAA among the human raters varies in
third category comprising domain knowledge, with the ARGUMENTATION crite-
rion being lower except for the Nudging task. This could suggest a problem
with rater calibration. Since this criterion accepts only “yes” or “no” as a
rating, the raters might use different thresholds when assessing economic ar-
gumentation. Similar to the CONCLUSION criterion, rater B displays different
behaviour in task Nudging; however, this time, the difference appears only
in comparison with rater A and not with rater C.
In the pairwise Krippendorff’s alpha inter-annotator agreements between
the human raters we observe that the SUM_SCORE results are stable despite
the varying outcomes of the other criteria. For the other criteria, we notice
low agreements e.g., with rater B in task Nudging or rater A in Startup.
This suggests that there are systematic differences in evaluation rather than
random variation. This finding shows that, although the raters disagree on
multiple criteria, the composite score can compensate for differences in how
the human raters understand, evaluate and apply the criteria.

5. Discussion
Unlike other studies that conclude that LLMs are currently unable to
replace human annotators (e.g., [70, 74, 85]), our results suggest a more
promising outlook in line with studies reporting LLM performance matching
conventional models (e.g., [78, 79]). We show that, for most criteria, at least
one LLM–prompt configuration can replace an individual human annotator
in essay-grading tasks in educational economics and medicine without low-
ering the agreement among the raters (RQ1). We demonstrate this for eight
tasks across these two domains and per-task rubrics comprising 103 criteria in
total (RQ2). We confirm our finding using two metrics: (1) inter-annotator
agreement based on Krippendorff’s alpha, which shows replaceability based
on agreement patterns; and (2) the Alternative Annotator Test, which pro-
vides statistical significance and additionally accounts for the cost of the
55


human annotation. Our evaluation shows that the agreement is preserved
or increased for most criteria when replacing one or two human raters with
LLMs, and that multiple LLMs pass the alt-test, meaning they can replace
at least 50% of the human raters.
These are best-case results: the best configuration differs by task and
criterion, and most tested configurations lower the agreement.
Since we
compare the LLM ratings with those of the human raters and do not evaluate
their absolute quality, a change in agreement does not show whether the
LLM-based ratings are better or worse. An increase may also result from
replacing the only rater who rated a response correctly, while a decrease may
result from LLM ratings that are more accurate than those of the human
raters. The same applies to the alt-test, which also uses the human raters as
the reference. Instead, we address the question that arises in practice, where
gold-standard ratings are rarely available and human raters partly disagree:
whether an LLM can take the place of one of these human raters without
lowering their agreement.
We rely on real-world data from the original assessment, manually an-
notated by multiple trained human raters. Their inter-annotator agreement
shows that constructing a single gold label can be challenging (as shown
in Table 7). We therefore treat each rater’s score as an equally plausible
reference and compare our LLM ratings with those of all human raters: sep-
arately in the case of annotator agreement and combined using the alt-test.
In future work, this rating indeterminacy could additionally be evaluated
more explicitly, e.g., following the framework presented by Guerdan et al.
[97]. Although our aim is not to imitate individual raters, the replaced rater
matters: replacing a rater whose ratings deviate from the others can increase
agreement (Figure 3), and the SHAP analysis identifies the replaced rater as
relevant for whether a non-negative change is achieved (Figure 12).
While the alt-test shows that LLM–prompt configurations can replace a
human rater for all criteria in Nudging and nearly all criteria overall, includ-
ing SUM_SCORE, the IAA results show that the best agreement is achieved
by different LLM–prompt combinations. Using an ensemble of LLMs there-
fore maximises their effectiveness. However, our experiments with criterion-
focused prompts for task Nudging, in which the LLM is queried for only one
criterion rather than all criteria, show no clear improvement. This suggests
that the LLMs benefit from the explicit inclusion of multiple criteria in their
rating process and the interdependencies among criteria in the overall stu-
dent rating. Querying one criterion after another while keeping the message
56


history, rather than asking for ratings of all criteria at once, has been shown
to be effective for the medical tasks, where the number of criteria is much
higher than in economics. This directly relates to the finding that model size
and the number of tokens influence the outcome, as shown in the SHAP anal-
ysis RQ3. Using a larger model appears to be more important than prompt
design. However, our results also show that, for the majority of tested con-
figurations, the agreement change when replacing a human rater is negative.
Figure 14 shows the prompt size, measured as the number of input and output
tokens, compared with the change in inter-annotator agreement. Note that
the number of tokens can be considered only an estimate, as it comprises the
prompt query, the student response text, additional features, and the LLM
outputs. Although there is a general trend indicating that agreement alphas
increase with a larger number of tokens, the results also show that, for all
tasks, the best IAAs are not achieved with the largest prompt. Using an
LLM to grade student answers reduces costs and enables applications such
as real-time feedback, where prediction speed matters. This depends directly
on the number and size of the prompts.
The ability to use local LLMs is relevant because of AI regulations, data-
protection rules, and the need for reproducibility. Additionally, the use of
proprietary LLMs can be complex or forbidden (cf. [154, 155]) and expensive,
especially for larger reasoning models (cf. [156, 157]). Therefore, given the
data-protection reasons regarding the student statements, our evaluation is
restricted to locally hosted, open-weight LLMs. This conclusion is supported
by our results on model parameter size and by the best-performing models
based on the alt-test. These models include a mixture of sizes and fami-
lies, albeit with a focus on the Llama 3.3 70b LLM, which was also used in
the most experiments, suggesting that smaller models and shorter prompts
can still deliver sufficient performance. However, larger models tend to per-
form better and show greater influence than prompt design.
Our results
demonstrate the potential of LLM-based AES, particularly for open-weight
LLMs, which have been shown to perform worse than closed LLMs in pre-
vious studies (e.g., [75, 89]). We show that open state-of-the-art models can
reach agreement comparable to that among our human annotators. To this
end, while proprietary LLMs might still outperform open models, the qual-
ity of the latter has increased in the recent years and now enables their use
in this scenario. The same applies to the need for fine-tuning LLMs and
to the development and training of classical approaches. Although the re-
search by Chamieh et al. [74], Kortemeyer [86], Latif and Zhai [76] mentions
57


improvements achieved through fine-tuning, our study suggests that the gen-
eral knowledge of current LLMs already leads to agreement comparable to
those of human annotators. Not having to fine-tune or further train a LLM
makes it more feasible for non-experts to use and develop such a rubric-based
LLM-AES system. This improves the already high level of usability enabled
by the ability to control the LLM using natural language. The results still
show that, in the best case, a human rater can be replaced without lowering
the agreement for about 88% of the criteria. This finding further supports the
use of self-hosted open-weight models, reducing resource utilisation, reliance
on proprietary LLM providers, and issues related to privacy concerns.
Our results confirm that, while fine-tuning is not necessarily required,
the agreement is sensitive to the prompt, as also reported by, e.g., Man-
sour et al. [75], Lee et al. [80]. However, we also observe that the SIMPLE
prompts perform on a par with the more advanced ones when using the
Qwen 2.5 and Nemotron LLMs. The rubric definition in these prompts is
kept very concise. Research shows that LLMs perform worse when irrelevant
information is included in the prompt [158], and OpenAI’s recommenda-
tion for prompting state-of-the-art reasoning models is to use “straightfor-
ward prompts” [159]. By comparison, the better-performing prompts for the
Llama model are larger prompts, containing e.g., extracted textual features
or training transcripts. Considering the distribution of the top-performing
models and the results of the SHAP analysis, our results indicate advantages
of more complex prompts regarding most of the criteria covered in our study,
similar to the findings of Huang and Wilson [81].
We use a direct approach in LLM-based AES, where we ask the LLM for
a concrete value and interpret its answer as a rating without further process-
ing, except simple parsing or transformation. Using the output of an LLM as
input for another system – e.g., as feature generator for a classifier or another
LLM – rather than relying exclusively on the LLM’s output for the rating,
may improve performance. This also removes the need for model fine-tuning
but instead requires the construction of subsequent tools. Additionally, com-
bining the outputs of multiple LLMs can balance different model biases and
compensate for errors. See, e.g., Shibata and Miyamura [87], Sahoo et al.
[160], Baumartz et al. [161], who evaluate such a classifier-based method in
the domain of medical education for assigning holistic ratings. This should
also be evaluated in the field of economics and for more fine-grained rubrics
in future work.
To assess the reproducibility of LLM-based ratings, we examined how
58


consistent the LLMs are in their ratings (subsection 4.7). Since a temperature
of 0.8 permits stochastic generation, this also indicates whether the selected
model configuration results in unstable ratings. The high average consistency
suggests stability in the LLMs’ application of the rubric, which can facilitate
the evaluation of known biases in LLMs and fairness in AI use (cf. [72, 73, 71]).
For holistic essay scoring, earlier work by Tate et al. [91] found no statis-
tically difference between human and ChatGPT scores. More recent research
by Emirtekin and Özarslan [90], hovever, shows greater agreement for LLM-
generated ratings on criteria that are based more on factual and definitional
aspects. They mention, that a holistic score, such as the SUM_SCORE in our
study, is harder for an LLM to rate. By contrast, for a human rater, this is
an easier task than assessing analytical criteria. The results of our study in-
dicate that this is the case. We report non-negative IAA changes and mostly
high agreement values across all eight tasks for the best human-replacing
LLM. However, the SUM_SCORE is not predicted directly by the LLM using a
prompting approach, but is calculated from the values of the other criteria.
This is consistent with how the SUM_SCORE is generated from the human rat-
ings – human raters did not assign a holistic score directly. This means that
the quality of all other criteria factors into the outcome of the composite
SUM_SCORE. This shows that the performance differences can be smoothed
out by the SUM_SCORE for both human and LLM-generated ratings. Direct
prediction should be further explored in future work, and, ideally, also com-
pared with direct assignment by human raters.
In total, we see that the ratings generated by the LLMs do not lower the
group agreement when one human rater is replaced by one LLM for 91 of the
103 criteria. This trend continues when we replace two humans with LLMs:
we observe a tendency toward higher agreement among LLMs than among
human raters, a finding that was also reported by Frohn et al. [84].
Unlike the results presented by Deng et al. [93], we do not observe a
decrease in agreement for the non-Boolean criteria that we studied. Across
all categories, automatic LLM-based grading is able to produce ratings that
achieve a comparable level of agreement with the human annotators for all
criterion categories, i.e., regarding formal, structural and domain-specific cri-
teria in economics and medical education.
Our results also show that human review is still required before employing
LLM-based AES. While simple prompts can perform adequately, the varia-
tion in IAA scores and the influence of the tasks itself suggests a validation
is necessary, especially when introducing new tasks, questions, or domains
59


into the assessments. In practice, LLM-based scoring should therefore be
validated against human ratings per task and criterion before use, since the
best configuration varies. As starting point, our results suggest larger models
and prompts that include training material for economics and criterion-wise
chaining for medicine.
6. Limitations
This study evaluates whether LLM-based systems can replace human
raters in a group without reducing agreement. We do not assess or analyse
whether the LLMs produce objectively correct ratings. We provide empirical
evidence that current state-of-the-art open-weight LLMs can replace indi-
vidual human annotators in assessing student responses in economics and
medical education without lowering the agreement: our results suggest pre-
served or increasing agreement among the top performers when replacing
one or two human raters with LLM-based systems. However, the majority
of tested LLM–prompt configurations lead to decreasing agreement. This
indicates that a full replacement of all human raters is not possible and that
human validation is still required.
The central limitation concerns the quality of the human ratings we use
for reference in our evaluation. This is reflected by the level of disagreement
measured by Krippendorff’s alpha, especially in economics, where only 5 of
31 criteria reach at least tentatively acceptable agreement, and could indicate
an issue with the rubric we study. The student statements were evaluated
by two independent rater groups, which received task-specific instructions
and training, including trial ratings. However, no consolidated gold rating
is available for use in our study. Our results must therefore be interpreted
as empirical evidence in the context of the observed human ratings and can-
not make any assumption on the objective correctness of the LLM-generated
ratings. In particular, for criteria with low human agreement, not lowering
the agreement is a weaker requirement than for criteria on which the human
raters agree, and a change in agreement does not indicate whether the LLMs
are better or worse. We provide complementary measures using Cronbach’s
alpha and ICC(3,k) to offer another perspective on the human rating agree-
ment. Additionally, the Alternative Annotator Test further provides insights
into the replaceability in relation to the human rater group as a whole.
Further limitations concern the number of student responses we analyse,
as well as our restriction to a subset of tasks within each of the two disci-
60


plines. In addition, the tasks, their length requirements, and the rubrics were
designed separately for each domain as part of the original assessment, dif-
ferences between the domains, e.g., in human agreement, may partly reflect
these design differences and should be interpreted with caution.
We use only open-weight and locally hostable LLMs for data-protection
reasons. Consequently, we do not compare our results with proprietary mod-
els. While this may seem limiting at first, the literature reviewed in section 2
suggests that proprietary models could achieve even better scores because of
their larger training corpora and parameter spaces [89]. Our results show a
small to moderate correlation between model parameter size and observed
IAA-based performance.
Finally, we only consider criteria that do not require the LLM to use
other content than the students’ responses. Using agentic or tool-based LLM
workflows for on-demand access to additional data, such as online resources,
or using a RAG-based approach, should be considered in future work.
7. Conclusion
We have shown the feasibility of using open-weight LLM-based AES for
student assessments in the fields of educational economics and medicine. Our
results, which are based on real-world data from four tasks per domain, show
that, for most criteria, at least one LLM–prompt configuration can replace
an individual human rater without the need for fine-tuning while maintain-
ing comparable inter-annotator agreements, as measured by Krippendorff’s
alpha. For 88%, i.e., 91 of the 103 criteria in our rubric, the agreement is not
lowered when replacing one human rater, with similar results when replacing
two. At least one configuration also passes the Alternative Annotator Test
with statistical significance, confirming the potential to replace at least 50%
of human raters across 87% of the criteria. These are best-case results: most
tested configurations lower the agreement. We show that the best-performing
prompts and LLMs vary by criterion, however, there is no clear indication
that criteria-specific prompts lead to improvements. As no gold standard for
the ratings is available, our results show agreement with the observed human
raters rather than the absolute correctness of the LLM ratings. Performance
is associated more with model and prompt size than with prompt design.
There is no visible difference in LLM performance across the three criteria
categories; the models are able to assign ratings similar to those of the hu-
man raters for formal, structural, and domain-knowledge criteria across both
61


domains. While LLMs avoid the individual effects of the human raters they
replace, they introduce their own biases into the ratings. However, using
automated methods can lead to more stable assessments and enable faster
processes, including real-time feedback to students while they are writing.
Human validation is still needed to ground LLM-based AES systems during
development. Together, these findings suggest the use of LLM ensembles to
benefit from higher top performances per criterion and to mitigate model
and prompt biases.
