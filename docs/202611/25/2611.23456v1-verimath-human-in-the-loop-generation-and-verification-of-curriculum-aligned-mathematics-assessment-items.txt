Abstract 

Automatic generation of mathematics assessment items is a growing area in educational arti-ficial intelligence, yet classroom deployment remains challenging. Generated questions must sat-isfy multiple constraints: mathematical correctness, curriculum alignment, appropriate cognitive difficulty, and educator reviewability. While prior work has advanced text generation, reliable assessment authoring requires granular control over localized examination formats and expert educational judgment. To address this challenge, we present VeriMath, a generalized, human-in-the-loop framework for generating rigorous, curriculum-constrained mathematics assessment drafts. Using annotated reference items, the framework anchors generation to the requested ex-amination section and cognitive reasoning level, then applies sequential automated verification and guided repair before submitting drafts for expert review. We evaluate VeriMath through a national curriculum case study using 4,555 mathematics items from the Vietnamese Grade 12 curriculum and 50 distinct generation requirements covering multiple-choice, true/false, and short-answer formats. Across two model backbones, the framework produces verifier-accepted drafts for 94% (47/50) of requirements per run. Blind expert evaluations confirm high mathe-matical accuracy and curriculum alignment, with strict teacher-readiness assessments accepting up to 56.8% of generated items. Analysis of rejected drafts reveals persistent domain-agnostic challenges involving formatting, statement validity, and solution-step completeness. These find-ings demonstrate that VeriMath can bridge generative AI and dependable educational appli-cations by producing highly reviewable assessment drafts within tightly constrained national curriculum frameworks. 

Keywords: Automatic question generation · curriculum-aligned assessment · educational artifi-cial intelligence · human-in-the-loop systems · large language models · mathematics assessment

1arXiv:2611.23456v1 [cs.AI] 17 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

Mathematics assessment items are central to classroom practice, formative feedback and examina-tion preparation. They connect curriculum objectives, cognitive demand, response format, scoring evidence and learner interpretation. Educational taxonomies and item-writing guidelines show that assessment quality depends on alignment among learning goals, task demands and scoring decisions, not only on grammatical clarity (Anderson et al., 2001; Haladyna et al., 2002). In mathematics ed-ucation, this alignment is especially demanding because a problem statement, its representations, its solution path and its answer format must remain mathematically coherent for the intended learners (Verschaffel et al., 2020). Automatic question generation has long been studied as a way to reduce the cost of creat-ing learning and assessment material. Systematic reviews describe a broad literature that in-cludes template-based, rule-based, semantic and neural methods for educational question gener-ation (Kurdi et al., 2020). Recent instructional design research identifies pedagogical alignment and content reliability as persistent concerns in authoring supported by artificial intelligence (AI) (Kibar & Ilgaz, 2026). In mathematics, early and intermediate systems generated personalized or constrained word problems from structured specifications, equations, topics and context constraints (Polozov et al., 2015; Z. Wang et al., 2021; Zhou & Huang, 2019). More recent retrieval-based and memory-based approaches improve relevance and diversity by reusing related examples while pre-serving generation flexibility (W. Qin et al., 2024). Large language models (LLMs) extend this line of work because they can produce fluent ex-planations, adapt content to learner contexts and support educational content creation at scale. Research on large language models in education emphasizes opportunities for personalization and teacher support, but also stresses the need for critical checking, pedagogical grounding and human oversight (Kasneci et al., 2023). At the assessment-authoring level, Cassano et al. (2026) embed item-writing criteria in prompts for transcript-based multiple-choice question generation and evalu-ate outputs through instructors’ judgments. These findings establish evidence-informed validation and human judgment as necessary quality controls for educational use. Mathematics-specific studies further use generative models to create contextualized and per-sonalized word problems grounded in authentic contexts (Utami et al., 2024). Teacher-evaluated systems such as MATHWELL and EDUMATH show that solvability and answer accuracy do not by themselves ensure educational appropriateness or standards alignment (Christ et al., 2024, 2026). These developments motivate moving from isolated prompt outputs toward authoring workflows in which generation, verification and expert judgment are explicitly connected. The problem studied here is curriculum-constrained mathematics assessment item authoring. Given a target grade, curriculum learning outcome, assessment schema, response constraints and cognitive or difficulty specification, the goal is to generate an original item draft with a correct solution and usable metadata. The draft must be mathematically valid, aligned with the intended learning outcome, matched to the requested level, structurally compatible with the target item format, L ATEX-compilable and sufficiently novel with respect to prior assessment material. This formulation is broader than ordinary math word problem generation because it treats an item as an assessment artifact that must be ready for professional review. Existing methods leave several limitations for this setting. The instructional-design review emphasizes practitioner judgment, while the transcript-based question-generation study illustrates quality-constrained authoring and instructor evaluation (Cassano et al., 2026; Kibar & Ilgaz, 2026). For curriculum-constrained mathematics assessment, the corresponding design problem is to con-nect local outcomes, assessment schemas, difficulty conventions, solution verification, novelty and teacher readiness within one workflow. Template and symbolic systems can enforce structure, but 2they often provide limited linguistic and mathematical diversity. Neural and large language model-based systems improve fluency and variation, but they may still produce hidden assumptions, invalid official answers, ambiguous wording, curriculum mismatches or near copies of familiar items (Christ et al., 2024; Utami et al., 2024; Z. Wang et al., 2021). Synthetic mathematical data pipelines such as MathGenie, MetaMath, WizardMath and OpenMathInstruct 2 demonstrate the value of generated mathematics data for training reasoning models, but their primary evaluation target is often downstream solver performance rather than teacher readiness for classroom or examination use (Lu et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025; Toshniwal et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). These limitations motivate three research questions: (RQ1) Can large language models generate structurally complete mathematics assessment drafts under explicit curriculum, format and level constraints? (RQ2) Can a context-specific teacher-annotated corpus provide useful anchors for difficulty and style without encouraging surface reuse? (RQ3) How large is the remaining gap between automated verification and teacher judgments of readiness? These questions are important because educational deployment requires more than fluent generation. It requires a workflow that makes generated items controllable, inspectable and open to expert rejection. VeriMath addresses these questions through a configurable human-in-the-loop authoring frame-work for mathematics assessment drafts. A deployment supplies its assessment schemas, curriculum outcomes, cognitive or difficulty conventions, response constraints, annotated reference corpus and expert-review rubric. The framework retrieves reference items that match the requested schema and level specification. These references guide style, format and reasoning depth, while the generated item must remain original. A generator then produces a complete draft, and repeated verification checks mathematical correctness, requirement alignment, answer consistency and structural valid-ity. When verification fails, the failure report is used to revise the draft under the same requirement. The final output is not treated as an official examination item; it is a candidate for teacher review. The framework is therefore defined independently of any particular national curriculum, whereas each deployment is configured with context-specific educational resources and conventions. We instantiate and evaluate it through a Vietnamese Grade 12 national-curriculum case study. The reference corpus contains 4,555 teacher-annotated national and practice examination questions, and the experimental requirement set covers multiple-choice, true-or-false and short-answer formats. Two large language model backbones are evaluated through full-pipeline diagnostics and blind expert review. The central empirical issue is the difference between automated acceptance and teacher readiness, because this difference indicates which defects still require expert assessment judgment after automated verification. The study provides evidence from this single curriculum context rather than a cross-country validation. The main contributions are as follows: • A curriculum-constrained formulation of mathematics assessment item authoring in which assessment schemas, learning outcomes, cognitive or difficulty specifications, response con-straints and curriculum metadata are supplied by the deployment context. • A configurable human-in-the-loop framework that combines context-specific difficulty anchor-ing with repeated verifier-guided revision before a draft is accepted for expert review. • A national-curriculum case-study instantiation using a 4,555-item Vietnamese Grade 12 math-ematics corpus annotated by teachers for assessment schema and cognitive or difficulty level. • An empirical evaluation of this instantiation with two large language model backbones, includ-ing automated acceptance, runtime and token cost, embedding-based novelty, blind expert review and expert-marked error categories. 3Table 1: Positioning of VeriMath Relative to Major Lines of Mathematics Item Generation

# 2 Related Work 

Research on automated mathematics item creation has developed along three connected directions. The first direction studies automatic question generation and mathematical word problem gener-ation. It moves from structured generation and knowledge sources toward neural generation with stronger mathematical constraints. The second direction uses large language models for control-lable educational content generation, where teacher annotations, standards and authentic contexts become central quality signals. The third direction studies verification and evaluation, including reasoning verifiers, synthetic mathematical data pipelines and model as judge rubrics. VeriMath draws from all three directions, but it targets a stricter authoring problem in which local curricu-lum metadata, item format, difficulty anchoring, novelty and teacher readiness must be considered together. Table 1 summarizes how VeriMath is positioned relative to major methodological lines in mathematics item generation. 42.1 Automatic Question Generation and Mathematical Word Problem Gener-ation 

Automatic question generation has long been studied as an educational technology for producing learning objects, practice questions and assessment material. Kurdi et al. (2020) provide a broad foundation for educational question generation and show that evaluation practices vary across domains and generation settings. Within instructional design, Kibar and Ilgaz (2026) synthesize 28 studies and identify content reliability, pedagogical alignment and practitioner judgment as central considerations. In mathematics education, the generated item must also preserve conceptual coherence, valid representations and solvable structure (Verschaffel et al., 2020). General assessment research further shows that item quality depends on alignment among objectives, cognitive demand, response format and scoring interpretation (Anderson et al., 2001; Haladyna et al., 2002). These foundations motivate treating mathematical item generation as an assessment authoring problem rather than a surface text generation problem. Early mathematical word problem generation emphasized explicit control. Polozov et al. (2015) generate personalized word problems from structured specifications, mathematical models and stu-dent relevant contexts. This type of method is attractive because it exposes variables and con-straints to the author. Its main limitation is that hand designed structures restrict linguistic variety and make adaptation to complex examination formats costly. Neural generation increases diversity but introduces new validity challenges. Zhou and Huang (2019) generate mathematical word problems from equations and topics using a neural model with a fusion mechanism and an entity consistency objective. Z. Wang et al. (2021) improve problem generation with mathematical consistency and context constraints. Niyarepola et al. (2022) extend the task to multilingual generation, showing that language resources matter for broader educational access. Qin et al. introduce structure planning and knowledge enhancement to improve logical coherence (2023), and later use disentangled memory retrieval to retrieve related logical and scenario descriptions for generation (2024). These works establish important methods for controlling mathematical content, but they mainly focus on word problem quality rather than complete assessment artifacts. Recent work also connects question generation with instructors’ evaluation of usable assess-ment material. Cassano et al. (2026) build a transcript-based multiple-choice question workflow around item-writing guidelines and report a mixed-methods evaluation with seven instructors and 257 questions. Remaining relevance and wording issues illustrate why structural compliance alone is insufficient. Their study concerns video-based materials rather than national-curriculum mathe-matics. VeriMath focuses on the latter setting through teacher-annotated anchors, explicit schemas, iterative verification and repair, novelty screening and blind expert review. 

2.2 Controllable and Teacher Evaluated Educational Generation 

Large language models broaden the design space because they can generate fluent explanations, adapt wording to educational contexts and support authoring at scale. At the same time, education focused studies emphasize that generative artificial intelligence requires human oversight, pedagog-ical design and careful integration with teacher judgment (Kasneci et al., 2023). This perspective is important for mathematics assessment because a fluent generated question may still be misaligned with the intended learning outcome or unfair for the target learners. Recent work therefore moves from generic generation toward controllable and teacher evaluated generation. Utami et al. (2024) study contextualized and personalized mathematical word problem generation in authentic contexts and examine its influence on geometry learning. MATHWELL 5uses teacher annotations to target solvability, answer accuracy and educational appropriateness for mathematics word problems (Christ et al., 2024). EDUMATH extends this direction toward standards aligned and customized educational word problems with expert and model based evalu-ation (Christ et al., 2026). These systems show that educational quality must be evaluated with pedagogical criteria, not only answer correctness. Another line improves controllability through planning, collaboration and difficulty manipula-tion. Cheng et al. (2025) formulate educational mathematical question generation as a planning problem with reflection over objectives. Karbasi et al. (2025) use a collaborative multi agent frame-work to refine generated math problems under criteria such as clarity, relevance, answerability and difficulty matching. SAND Math generates novel mathematics problems and modifies their diffi-culty for synthetic training data construction (Manem et al., 2025). These approaches are method-ologically close to VeriMath because they introduce intermediate control signals. VeriMath differs by using context-specific teacher annotated difficulty anchors, enforcing configurable assessment schemas and measuring readiness through blind expert review. 

2.3 Verification, Synthetic Data and Evaluation 

Verification is a central issue because mathematical outputs can look plausible while containing in-valid assumptions, wrong answers or incomplete solutions. Chain of thought prompting encourages intermediate reasoning, and self consistency aggregates multiple reasoning paths to improve answer reliability (X. Wang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022). Cobbe et al. (2021) show that trained verifiers can rank candidate solutions for grade school mathematics problems. Lightman et al. (2024) show that process supervision can provide feedback at intermediate reasoning steps and improve mathe-matical reasoning reliability. These studies focus mainly on solving or judging solutions. VeriMath adapts the verification idea to item authoring, where the object under review includes the problem statement, answer, solution, metadata and format. Generated mathematics is also used to train stronger reasoning models. MetaMath bootstraps mathematical questions for large language model training (Yu et al., 2024). WizardMath uses reinforced instruction evolution for mathematical reasoning (Luo et al., 2025). MathGenie uses question back translation and rationale based verification when constructing synthetic data (Lu et al., 2024). OpenMathInstruct 2 scales open mathematical instruction data, while MathFusion combines mathematical instructions across sources to improve problem solving (Pei et al., 2025; Toshniwal et al., 2025). These methods demonstrate the value of generated mathematics data, but their primary success criterion is downstream solver performance. Assessment item authoring requires a different criterion, namely whether a teacher can safely review and deploy the generated item. Finally, model based evaluation offers scalable diagnostics but remains limited for educational readiness. G Eval uses large language models with generated evaluation steps and form based scoring for natural language generation quality (Liu et al., 2023). Zheng et al. (2023) study model as judge evaluation and document useful agreement with human preferences as well as biases such as position, verbosity and self enhancement effects. These findings motivate using automated diagnostics cautiously. # 3 Task and Dataset 

3.1 Task Formulation and Output Schema 

Let R = ( s, o, L, q, m ) denote an item generation requirement. The variable s ∈ S identifies an assessment schema from a set S defined by the deployment context, o is the curriculum learning outcome or standard, L is the context-specific cognitive or difficulty specification, q contains re-sponse constraints such as answer type and language and m contains curriculum metadata such as grade, subject, chapter and lesson section. Neither S nor the taxonomy underlying L is fixed by the VeriMath architecture. A deployment supplies its schema definitions and operational level conventions as part of the framework configuration. Given R and a reference corpus Dref , the goal is to generate an assessment draft X = ( P, S, M ), where P is the problem statement, S is the solution and official answer and M is the generated metadata. The tuple X should be mathematically correct, aligned with o, matched to L, valid under the requested assessment schema and sufficiently novel with respect to Dref . The output is treated as a teacher reviewable draft rather than a final examination artifact. In the Vietnamese Grade 12 case-study instantiation, SVN = {1, 2, 3} uses three schema iden-tifiers mapped to the item formats summarized in Table 2. The multiple-choice and short-answer schemas use a single level, while the true-or-false cluster schema uses L = ( ℓ1, ℓ 2, ℓ 3, ℓ 4) because it contains four independently judged statements. The response structure for this instantiation is determined by s and enforced by the generation and verification process described in Section 4. These three schemas are the configuration evaluated in this study, not a universal schema set required by VeriMath. 

3.2 Reference Corpus, Annotation and Difficulty Levels 

The case-study reference corpus Dref contains 4,555 mathematics problems collected from Viet-namese national and practice examination sources. Mathematics teachers annotated each item with its assessment schema and cognitive or difficulty level. The corpus is used to select difficulty anchors, estimate novelty by maximum embedding similarity and define the expected distribution of topics and formats. To reduce leakage risk, anchors are used only as format and difficulty references, while generated items are checked for novelty against the full corpus. The annotation protocol is designed as a two stage process: initial labeling followed by se-nior teacher adjudication of disagreements. # 4 Methodology 

Using the task notation from Section 3.1, VeriMath generates teacher reviewable mathematics assessment drafts under curriculum, schema and difficulty constraints supplied by the deployment context. It contains two main inference time components: Difficulty Anchoring, described in Section 4.1, and a Consecutive Verification Mechanism (CVM), described in Section 4.2. # 5 Experiments 

The experiments instantiate VeriMath in the Vietnamese Grade 12 setting and address two ques-tions aligned with its intended use as an assessment-authoring support tool. First, can the end-to-end framework produce structurally complete, novel, verifier-accepted drafts under the curriculum and format constraints of this case study? Second, do human teachers judge those drafts as ready for educational use? The first question is answered through the diagnostics in Section 5.2. The second is answered through the expert review and failure analysis in Sections 5.3 and 5.5. 13 5.1 Experimental Setup 

5.1.1 Models and Decoding 

The generation pipeline is evaluated with two backbones: Qwen3-8B and gpt-oss-20b . Within each backbone run, the same underlying model is used for generation, self-improvement, verification and correction, but each step uses a different role prompt. Thus, the verifier is a separately prompted role rather than an independent model family. Question generation uses random sampling with temperature 1.0. CVM requires three consecutive verifier approvals and allows up to three verifier-guided correction attempts before rejection. After verification, a separate L ATEX formatting pass is applied with up to three repair attempts. Novelty is measured by comparing each generated question with all 4,555 questions in the reference dataset using a multilingual sentence-embedding model. An automated LLM-judge route was also explored as a separate diagnostic role, but it is not used as primary readiness evidence in this study. The judge prompt asks the model to evaluate exactly four dimensions on a [0 , 1] scale: requirement alignment, level fidelity, language fluency and mathematical accuracy. It explicitly defines level fidelity as matching the requested teacher-annotated cognitive/difficulty level and requests valid JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) with those four keys and a brief feedback field. 

5.1.2 Data and Splits 

The teacher annotated reference corpus contains 4,555 questions and is divided by exam source into an anchor set and a separate evaluation set. This split helps reduce overlap from highly similar practice questions. Experimental requirements are created independently from the Grade 12 curriculum. Each backbone is evaluated on the same 50 requirements with the schema ratio defined in Section 3.3. After expanding the four statements in each true or false cluster from Table 2, the requirement set contains 80 learning-outcome slots: 67 from calculus/statistics and 13 from geometry. The historical dataset is used only for anchoring and novelty measurement, not for training the backbone models. 

5.1.3 Scope of Empirical Claims 

This study reports end-to-end full-pipeline runs and completed expert-review records. It does not report controlled baseline or ablation experiments. Therefore, the acceptance rates should not be interpreted as causal evidence for the independent contribution of Difficulty Anchoring, self-improvement, CVM, or formatting repair. The component-level claims in this paper are limited to their designed roles and to qualitative traces of verifier-guided revision. Claims about edu-cational readiness are based on blind expert review rather than on automated verifier decisions. All reported empirical evidence pertains to the Vietnamese Grade 12 instantiation and does not establish performance transfer across curricula or countries.

5.3 Human Readiness Evaluation 

The expert evaluation follows the blind protocol described in Section 3.2. Mathematics educators review generated items without seeing the generation source, model identity or process traces. They rate each item on four criteria. Mathematical correctness and answer consistency are recorded as one binary label, and alignment with the assigned learning outcome is recorded as another binary label. Match to the required cognitive or difficulty level and clarity or readiness for examination use are scored on a 1 to 5 scale. A generated item passes expert review when correctness and alignment both pass and the level match and clarity scores are both at least 4. When multiple ratings are available, the reported score is the average of independent ratings. Reviewers also mark error flags for wrong answer, invalid problem statement, multiple valid answers, missing or incorrect solution step, wrong learning outcome, too easy, too hard, ambiguous or missing condition and formatting or formula error. Fig. 4 separates binary criteria from 1 to 5 rating criteria. The intended review pool is the 50-item requirement set for each reported generation backbone. The quantitative analysis below uses completed expert-review records: 44 items for Qwen3-8B and 39 items for gpt-oss-20b . Each reviewer sees the problem statement, official answer, solution, requested schema value s, target curriculum learning outcome and requested cognitive or difficulty level. A reference question from the historical corpus may be shown for difficulty, format and novelty calibration. Table 11 is the central quality result. Correctness and curriculum alignment are high, but expert pass rates are much lower because readiness also requires level fit and clear exam-style presentation. The difference between the 94.0% verifier-accepted rate and the approximately 56% expert-pass rate shows that CVM is useful as a screening and repair mechanism, but automatic verification is not sufficient for final educational use. VeriMath should therefore be deployed as a draft authoring aid with a human quality gate, not as an autonomous examination generator. The expert labels also provide a human reference for future judge calibration. In this paper, they are reported directly rather than converted into automated-judge agreement claims. 

5.4 Novelty and Leakage Checks 

Embedding-based novelty may fail to detect reused templates, especially when a generated item changes only numerical values while keeping the same story and solution method as an anchor. The present paper therefore reports embedding novelty only as a pipeline diagnostic. We do not treat the novelty scores as a complete leakage audit. For assessment authoring, a teacher-facing system should also support nearest-neighbor inspection, symbolic-template comparison and manual review of any generated item whose solution structure is close to a historical question.

# 6 Discussion 

6.1 Position Within a Digital Transformation Platform 

VeriMath can serve as a curriculum-constrained item-authoring and verification layer within broader digital assessment environments. In such environments, generated drafts can feed teacher-managed workflows for question-bank construction, test assembly, delivery, scoring and revision. This role follows the broader educational principle that generative artificial intelligence should remain con-nected to pedagogical grounding and teacher judgment (Kasneci et al., 2023; Kibar & Ilgaz, 2026). In the present Vietnamese implementation, this layer is integrated into a broader web platform that supports teachers and students preparing for the national high school graduation examination in mathematics. The long-term goal of this implementation is a connected educational environment in which teachers can create assessment items, assemble tests, deploy examinations to their classes, collect student responses, apply automatic grading and inspect critiques of generated items and student solutions. Such an environment forms a closed workflow from item authoring, test delivery and automatic scoring to diagnosis, feedback and teacher revision. Figure 5 illustrates the platform modules. This implementation is an example of deployment in one national context; the present 20 study evaluates the item-authoring component rather than the platform as a whole. 

6.2 Strengths and Validation Evidence 

A main strength of the study is that it formulates generation as curriculum constrained assessment authoring rather than open ended text production. Each draft is required to satisfy a specified item format, curriculum learning outcome, cognitive or difficulty level, mathematical answer, solution explanation, novelty condition and teacher review requirement. This framing is important because mathematics items are pedagogical and assessment artifacts, not only fluent linguistic outputs (Verschaffel et al., 2020). This design focus is consistent with the instructional-alignment concerns identified by Kibar and Ilgaz (2026). A second strength is the explicit separation between reusable authoring mechanisms and local adaptation resources. By design, what changes across educational systems is not the anchoring, verification and repair architecture itself, but the curriculum metadata, assessment schemas, dif-ficulty conventions, reference corpus and expert rubric supplied to it. In the Vietnamese Grade 12 case study, the 4,555 item teacher annotated corpus provides examples of notation, topic dis-tribution, assessment schemas and difficulty conventions. Difficulty Anchoring uses this corpus to condition generation on nearby items without treating the anchors as templates to copy. CVM then requires repeated successful verification and uses failure traces for repair before a draft is accepted. This design makes the pipeline more auditable than a single prompt based generator because each accepted item can be linked to its requirement, retrieved anchors, verifier decisions and repair history. The case study demonstrates this adaptation mechanism in one context; it does not establish equivalent performance in other educational systems. The validation within this case study is deliberately multi layer. The same 50 requirements are tested with two model backbones, and both runs produce verifier accepted drafts for 47 require-ments. Operational diagnostics report acceptance, token cost, runtime, verifier calls and repair calls, while embedding based novelty checks examine similarity to the reference corpus. Most im-portantly, blind expert review evaluates mathematical correctness, curriculum alignment, difficulty match, clarity and final readiness under a teacher rubric. The results show high correctness and alignment, but only about 56% of completed review records satisfy the full readiness criterion. This gap is a useful result rather than only a weakness: it shows that automated verification can improve reviewability, but professional educational judgment remains necessary before classroom or exam-ination use. The need for expert checks also accords with the relevance and wording limitations reported by Cassano et al. (2026). The evaluation settings and rubrics differ, so these findings are a design comparison rather than a direct performance benchmark. 

6.3 Quality Gaps and Current Boundaries 

The remaining errors clarify what the current system does not yet solve. The most frequent expert marked issue is an invalid problem statement. This can occur when a draft has fluent wording and plausible formulas but still lacks a necessary condition, describes an impossible configuration or fails to guarantee a unique answer. Formula and formatting issues also remain because mathematical notation is sensitive to rendering, symbol placement and local examination conventions. Missing or incorrect solution steps indicate that a model may reach the intended answer while presenting reasoning that is incomplete, mathematically unjustified or unsuitable for the expected high school method. Several validity boundaries should therefore be recognized. First, the Recognition, Compre-hension and Application labels are teacher operational categories used in the studied examination 21 setting. They are not calibrated psychometric item parameters. Second, the verifier uses a separate role and prompt, but it is not a fully independent assessment authority. Verifier acceptance should be treated as evidence of improved reviewability, not as proof of mathematical or pedagogical readi-ness. Third, the empirical evaluation is limited to Vietnamese Grade 12 mathematics and the three assessment schemas in Table 2. Adapting VeriMath to another educational system would require replacing or contextualizing the curriculum metadata, schema definitions, anchor corpus, difficulty conventions, annotation protocol and expert rubric. The architecture permits such configuration, but whether comparable performance transfers across grades, subjects or countries remains an empirical question. The empirical evaluation also has practical limits. It does not include controlled ablation experiments that isolate the individual effects of Difficulty Anchoring, self-improvement, CVM or formatting repair. It does not compare the method against retrieval only, prompt only or teacher authored baselines under the same review protocol. Expert review is a strength because it is blind and rubric based, but the number of completed review records is still limited. The study also does not measure student learning gains, test equating, long term teacher workload reduction or performance in official high stakes examinations. For these reasons, the current system should be used for teacher reviewed draft generation and practice material authoring, not for autonomous official test production. 

6.4 Future Research Directions 

Future work should strengthen both the research method and the platform function. On the methodological side, controlled ablations can quantify the separate contributions of anchoring, repeated verification, repair prompts and novelty checks. Independent verifier model families, symbolic checks for answer uniqueness, rendering based formula inspection and template leakage detection can make automated review more reliable. On the empirical side, larger expert panels and classroom pilots can evaluate whether the platform reduces teacher workload, improves feedback quality and supports student preparation. The platform should also evolve from item authoring to a fuller digital assessment environment. Teachers should be able to select topics, assemble tests, deploy them directly to a class, receive automatic grading and inspect intelligent critique for both item quality and student responses. Teacher edits, rejection reasons and student performance logs can then become evidence for future corpus refinement and adaptive practice. This direction supports a closed educational ecosystem in which generation, testing, grading and evaluation inform one another while teachers retain final pedagogical authority. Another important direction is specialized research for gifted students and advanced mathemat-ical reasoning. Future corpora can select distinct topics, competition styles and examination types for higher difficulty settings, including topic focused tests, school team selection tests and advanced practice examinations. Recent multimodal benchmarks such as MathNet show the value of large, diverse collections for mathematical reasoning and retrieval across topics, languages and visual for-mats (Alshammari et al., 2026). In the Vietnamese setting, this line can guide the development of more challenging assessments and computer based learning environments that go beyond routine national examination preparation. 

# 7 Conclusion 

This paper presented VeriMath, a configurable human-in-the-loop framework for curriculum-constrained mathematics assessment authoring. The framework separates shared authoring and 22 verification mechanisms from the assessment schemas, curriculum outcomes, difficulty conventions, reference corpus and expert rubric supplied by a deployment context. It combines teacher annotated difficulty anchors with consecutive verification and verifier guided repair, producing drafts for professional review rather than treating model outputs as final assessment items. We instantiated the framework through a Vietnamese Grade 12 national-curriculum case study using 4,555 teacher annotated reference items and 50 generation requirements. With both evaluated backbones, 47 of 50 requirements reached verifier acceptance. Blind expert review showed high rates of mathematical correctness and curriculum alignment, while 56.8% and 56.4% of completed review records satisfied the strict readiness rubric. The main remaining failures involved invalid problem statements, formula or formatting problems and incomplete or incorrect solution steps. These findings clarify both the value and the boundary of automated verification: it can improve the quality and reviewability of generated assessment drafts, but it cannot replace expert teacher judgment. The broader implication is that responsible artificial intelligence for mathematics assessment should be designed as a teacher-supported authoring environment rather than an autonomous examination generator. The Vietnamese case study supports the feasibility of combining context-specific anchors with repeated verification and repair in one tightly constrained curriculum setting. It does not establish cross-country generalization. Instead, the framework provides a basis for adaptation by making local educational constraints explicit and configurable, while preserving traceability and professional oversight. Future work should focus on the failure modes identified by expert review. Stronger checks for statement validity, answer uniqueness, mathematical rendering and solution completeness are necessary because many errors are subtle and pedagogically consequential. Controlled ablation studies should measure the individual contributions of anchoring, self improvement, verification and repair. Evaluations with independently constructed corpora, schemas and expert rubrics from other grades, subjects and educational systems are needed to test empirical transfer while keeping educational validity as the central requirement.
