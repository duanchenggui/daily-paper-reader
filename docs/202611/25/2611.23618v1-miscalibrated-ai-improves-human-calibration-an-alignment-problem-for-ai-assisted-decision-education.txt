## ABSTRACT 

Using artificial intelligence (AI) well requires probabilistic thinking, and central to it is calibra-tion : expressing certainty that matches one’s actual accuracy. Calibration is a decision skill, but a notoriously difficult one to teach. Because AI systems typically generate a probability alongside every prediction, observing these probabilities may help humans learn to provide better calibrated judgments. This is one form of what we call AI-assisted decision education — using AI to teach the judgment skills that decision-making requires — of which learning by observation, as here, is among the simplest forms. In a preregistered experiment, novice participants (N = 300) judged the probability that images of white blood cells were cancerous, before and after exposure to a highly accurate (94%) convolutional neural network. A single model was shown in three ways, holding accuracy fixed while varying the certainty it expressed: its original probabilities, which were conservative and poorly calibrated for the model’s accuracy; a recalibrated version, well calibrated for the model and correspondingly certain; or categorical predictions carrying no probability information. When later tested without the AI, only the original, conservative display improved participants’ calibration. It did so by reducing over-extremity, without a condition-specific improvement in discrimination. Notably, the display that taught best was the one miscalibrated for the model. AI-assisted decision education therefore faces an alignment problem: the signal that best describes a model, need not be the signal that best supports the learner.

## 1 Introduction 

As artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly used to provide decision support in high-stakes domains, it is more critical than ever to ensure that people have the skills to appropriately express, interpret, and integrate uncertainty from many sources [Steyvers and Kumar, 2024]. Central among these skills is probability calibration . Better-calibrated individuals rely on AI assistance more selectively and appropriately, yielding superior collaborative performance [Ma et al., 2024], and they benefit more from AI assistance compared to their solo performance. This is especially true for those with high calibration but low initial ability, who may have the most to gain from AI assistance [Caplin et al., 2025]. Calibration also matters upstream of AI deployment: well-calibrated probability judgments from human annotators improve the quality of training data and, in turn, the accuracy and alignment of the AI systems trained on them [Epping et al., 2026a,b]. Improving human calibration - not just accuracy - is therefore a high priority for

Deciding when to defer to a model and when to override it—the appropriate reliance that is widely treated as a core competency of AI literacy [Ma et al., 2024, Long and Magerko, 2020]—is itself a probabilistic judgment, and so rests on calibration. Probabilistic thinking is in this sense an important target for decision education, and one whose value rises as more decisions are made with AI. A judge is perfectly calibrated if, across all instances where they assign probability p, the outcome occurs p percent of the time [Lichtenstein et al., 1982]. Human judgments are frequently miscalibrated, most commonly exhibiting over-extremity: assigning probabilities more certain (closer to 0 and 1) than their ability to discriminate between true states warrants [Griffin and Brenner, 2004]. Calibration is challenging for AI systems as well, which often require post-processing or fine-tuning to produce reasonably calibrated judgments [Guo et al., 2017]. In this paper, we test whether AI can help humans learn to provide better calibrated judgments. AI models commonly produce probabilities—an image classifier computes the probability of category membership, a large language model the probability of each possible next token—which carry information about the relationship between an input and the AI’s uncertainty, offering insight into underlying difficulty or typicality. If AI uncertainty is made available to a human learner, they might detect these regularities and use them to improve their own calibration. This could circumvent key limitations of prior calibration training, which has been dominated by multifaceted interventions combining expert instruction with personalized feedback [Lichtenstein and Fischhoff, 1980, Stone and Opel, 2000, Sharp et al., 1988, Kelly and Mandel, 2024, Stone et al., 2023]. Such training is difficult to scale: it requires expert pedagogy, often spans multiple sessions [Moore et al., 2017, Gruetzemacher et al., 2024], and needs many trials—often 100 or more—to estimate an individual’s miscalibration before feedback can be offered [Kumar et al., 2019]. AI probabilities, by contrast, provide a trial-by-trial signal at essentially no marginal cost, and transmit a different kind of information than the trial-by-trial signals that have failed in the past: neither outcome feedback nor proper scoring rules improve calibration [Baranski and Petrusic, 1994, Martin and Mandel, 2025]. An AI probability is a model of uncertainty—the degree of certainty expressed by another capable agent, stimulus by stimulus. We call this possibility AI-assisted decision education : using AI not to improve a particular decision, but to teach the judgment skills that decision-making requires — whether through adaptive instruction or, as here, mere exposure to an AI’s expressed uncertainty. Research on AI-assisted decision making asks how well people decide while the AI is present, and how its displayed confidence should be designed to support decisions [Vodrahalli et al., 2022, Corvelo Benz and Gomez Rodriguez, 2023]. Recent work extends this to humans who learn from an AI over repeated interactions, raising the prospect of assistants designed for long-run learning as well as immediate performance [Noti et al., 2025]. Whether such exposure improves people’s unaided judgments once the AI is withdrawn remains largely untested. Calibration is a demanding test case, given its resistance to conventional training. There is, however, a fundamental tension in using AI probabilities to teach calibration: the output best calibrated for the model may not be the output that best teaches the learner . A highly accurate, well-calibrated AI must be quite extreme. Consider an AI classifying images of white blood cells as blasts—immature cells, evidence of cancer when abnormally concentrated—or non-blasts. If it is correct in the vast majority of cases ( > 90% ), well-calibrated output requires high certainty on most trials; at the limit, a perfectly accurate AI’s calibrated judgments would be exactly 0% and 100%, functionally equivalent to outcome feedback, which is ineffective for teaching calibration [Baranski and Petrusic, 1994, Martin and Mandel, 2025]. Worse, for novices already prone to over-extremity, a highly certain AI could reinforce that tendency: Li et al. [2025] found that AI-assisted decision making persistently shifted self-confidence toward the AI’s mean confidence, worsening calibration for the initially over-confident majority. Patterns relating stimulus features to certainty may also be easier to learn among more variable predictions spanning the full probability range. In short, model calibration and learner calibration are distinct design targets, and they can conflict. This tension is an alignment problem at the human–AI interface. An AI’s probability output is a statement about the model—how certain it is, given its own accuracy—and simultaneously an intervention on the human who receives it. Alignment concerns this second function: it is a property of the communicated signal relative to the person who uses it, and is distinct from calibration, the signal’s fidelity to the model’s accuracy. Because the purpose of AI assistance is to serve the human [Hadfield-Menell et al., 2016], the signal best calibrated to the model need not be the one that best serves the human. For decision making, that signal is tuned to the decision being made now, with the AI present; for decision education, the target is the human’s unaided judgment later, once the AI is gone. This is the alignment problem we examine. This project investigates (a) whether exposure to AI probabilities facilitates learning of probability calibration in human novices, (b) whether any improvements in calibration coincide with improvements in accuracy and judgment informativeness, and (c) whether learning differs by the calibration and extremity of the AI’s output. Participants judge the probability that images of white blood cells are blasts (versus non-blasts), with AI predictions provided by a convolutional neural net (CNN) fine-tuned on human-annotated data from this task [Epping et al., 2026b]. The design’s key feature is that a single underlying AI, highly accurate ( ∼94%), was displayed to participants in one of three ways 2Miscalibrated AI improves human calibration PREPRINT 

that varied the uncertainty profile while holding accuracy fixed: its original probability output, which—because the model was trained on recalibrated human judgments and so expresses a human-like level of certainty rather than one matched to its own accuracy—is conservative and thus poorly calibrated relative to its accuracy; a recalibrated version of the same output, well calibrated for the model and correspondingly certain; or categorical predictions, a baseline stripped of probability information. After an initial training phase making subjective probability judgments with trial-by-trial ground-truth feedback, participants practiced with AI predictions displayed after each trial, and were then tested without feedback or AI. Our results show that participants exposed to the original, conservative AI—the display miscalibrated for the model’s own ability—learned to respond with less over-extremity, thereby substantially improving their own calibration. AI-assisted decision education is therefore possible. Importantly, the display that taught best was the one poorly calibrated for the model itself. Thus, probability displays optimized for model calibration are not necessarily optimized for human learning. 

## 2 Results 

In a preregistered experiment, novice participants (N=300) were given an opportunity to study examples of blast (cancer) and non-blast white blood cells before judging the probability that white blood cells were blast across three phases: (1) training with ground truth feedback, (2) practice with AI exposure, and (3) test without feedback or AI (Figure 1). Participants were given brief instruction in probabilistic judgment and were told that half of the cells in each phase would be blast. Performance in Phase 3 was incentivized by a $4 bonus with probability determined by their Brier score. AI predictions, coming from a highly accurate (94%) convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on probability judgments from skilled annotators in Epping et al. [2026b], varied by condition: original (poorly calibrated, frequently conservative) probabilities, recalibrated (well-calibrated, frequently certain) probabilities, or categorical predictions. Distributions of the output of each AI variant across all 300 stimuli can be seen in Figures 1 and S1. Our analyses compare behavior in pre-AI Phase 1 training (100 trials per participant) to post-AI Phase 3 testing (120 first judgments of non-repeated stimuli). We used Bayesian multilevel linear and logistic regressions with weakly informative or uninformative priors, with by-participant and by-stimulus random effects where appropriate. 

2.1 Performance Metrics 

Our central result concerns calibration, so we begin with reliability , a component of the Brier score measuring calibration error, where 0 indicates perfect calibration [Murphy, 1973]. All three conditions began with mean reliability ∼.065 in Phase 1: maximum a posteriori estimate (MAP) = .067 (95% highest density interval, HDI = [.056, .077]) for categorical, .063 ([.051, .073]) for original, and .062 ([.051, .073]) for recalibrated. Reliability was essentially unchanged in Phase 3 for categorical and recalibrated, but improved for original, dropping -.020 ([-.030, -.011]) to .041 ([.030, .053]). For context, the original AI output these participants observed had a reliability of .036, so their Phase 3 calibration approached, but did not reach, that of the display they saw. The original condition improved credibly more than both categorical (difference-in-differences, DiD O−C = -.021, [-.034, -.008]) and recalibrated (DiD O−R = -.026, [-.039, -.013]), which did not credibly differ from each other (DiD R−C = .005, [-.008, .018]). These metrics and their changes are shown in Figure 2. The same pattern holds for expected calibration error, which decreased only in the original condition (-.046, [-.063, -.030]) and credibly more than in categorical (DiD O−C = -.048, [-.072, -.025]); see Supplement. To interpret this calibration gain, we ask whether it was accompanied by improved ability to distinguish blast from non-blast. Resolution , another Brier component, is a skill metric ranging from 0 (no discrimination) to .25 (perfect discrimination). All conditions began similarly, though perhaps slightly lower in categorical: .047 (95% HDI = [.040, .056]) for categorical, .058 ([.049, .066]) for original, and .059 ([.050, .067]) for recalibrated. As with reliability, resolution improved credibly only within the original condition (+.010, [.004, .017]). However, this change did not credibly differ from categorical (DiD O−C = .006, [-.003, .014]) or recalibrated (DiD O−R = .008, [-.001, .017]), nor did recalibrated differ from categorical (DiD R−C = -.002, [-.011, .007]); all intervals include 0. We also examined classification (“binarized”) accuracy, treating judgments > . 5 as “blast”, < . 5 as “non-blast”, and exactly .5 as a random choice. All conditions began at or just under 70% in Phase 1: .673 (95% HDI = [.648, .700]) for categorical, .703 ([.673, .729]) for original, and .692 ([.662, .718]) for recalibrated. All three improved by Phase 3: +.026 ([.005, .044]), +.038 ([.016, .058]), and +.030 ([.010, .049]) respectively, with no evidence of between-condition differences (all 95% HDIs include 0). The calibration advantage of the original condition therefore cannot be attributed to a condition-specific gain in classification ability. These results indicate that calibration only improved for the condition exposed to the worse-calibrated original AI probabilities, and that this improvement was accompanied by little-to-no relative gain in discrimination. As can be seen in Figure S1, individuals in all conditions began with a tendency to respond with high degrees of certainty, with almost half of responses being ≤ .1 or ≥ .9. In order to characterize response and calibration errors while accounting for individual variability, we turn to the linear in log-odds model.

2.3 Blackwell Informativeness 

The reduction in over-extremity we observe for the original AI condition can be achieved in roughly two ways. (1) Individuals apply a global adjustment, shifting all judgments to be more conservative so that overall certainty better matches their ability to discriminate. (2) Individuals apportion certainty differentially across cells , so that expressed certainty tracks the difficulty of individual stimuli. The two differ in what participants would have learned: the first requires only a uniform shift in the level of certainty, whereas the second requires a stimulus-level mapping from cells to uncertainty. The minimal evidence of relative gains in resolution or accuracy for the original AI condition suggests that (1) plays a considerable role. Neither resolution nor accuracy can fully adjudicate between the accounts. Binarized accuracy records only which side of .5 a judgment falls on, so a participant could learn which cells are difficult and express that in graded confidence without altering any classification. Resolution does speak to both accounts, but it is an incomplete test: it captures only the spread of the distribution, and only from binned judgments. Two sets of judgments with identical resolution can even support opposite decisions at different intervention thresholds (see Supplement). Distinguishing the accounts thus calls for a criterion defined over the entire distribution rather than a summary of it. We therefore compare the Blackwell informativeness of recalibrated Phase 3 and Phase 1 judgments, a criterion defined over full judgment distributions. For any decision-maker, well-calibrated judgments that are a mean-preserving spread 

of another set are weakly preferable to it [Blackwell, 1951]; if Phase 3 is a mean-preserving spread of Phase 1, Phase 3 judgments are Blackwell more informative , supporting universally better decisions. Recalibration is what makes this comparison diagnostic. A purely global adjustment is undone by the recalibration step: if a participant shrinks every judgment toward .5 by a common transformation, fitting the LLO model recovers that transformation and returns the original recalibrated values. We therefore find no indication of stimulus-level learning: the gain in informativeness is slight and no greater in the two probability conditions, which carried stimulus-specific uncertainty information, than in the categorical condition, which carried none. We note that the criterion is evaluated graphically and the analysis is therefore descriptive rather than inferential. Its value is as converging evidence: the resolution and accuracy analyses likewise show no condition-specific gain in discrimination, and together this is the pattern a global rescaling of certainty would produce. ## 3 Discussion 

We asked whether exposure to an AI’s probability output can teach novices to express better-calibrated uncertainty, and, if so, whether the output that is best calibrated for the model is also the output that best teaches the learner . The 7Miscalibrated AI improves human calibration PREPRINT 

answer to the first question is yes, indicating the feasibility of AI-assisted decision education: simply observing a model’s probabilities, with no instruction and no adaptation to the individual, improved a skill that has resisted far more elaborate training. The answer to the second question is no. Only the original, conservative AI—the variant whose probabilities were poorly calibrated relative to the model’s own accuracy—improved participants’ calibration. The recalibrated variant, which was well calibrated for the model, produced no such improvement, and neither did the categorical baseline. The improvement was specifically a reduction in over-extremity . Participants in every condition began over-extreme, expressing more certainty than their discrimination warranted [Griffin and Brenner, 2004], and only exposure to the conservative AI lessened this tendency, with the linear-in-log-odds slope α moving toward one. It brought no relative 

gain in discrimination: resolution and binarized accuracy improved similarly across all three conditions, and the gain in Blackwell informativeness was no larger in the two probability conditions than in the categorical baseline, which carried no uncertainty information at all. Thus, participants did not acquire the metacognitive skill of learning which 

cells warrant more certainty. What transferred was a general level of certainty rather than a mapping from cells to certainty, consistent with evidence that people’s confidence drifts toward that of an AI they work with [Li et al., 2025]. This also clarifies why the conservative display helped while the certain one did not: novices were already over-extreme, so a highly certain AI—however well calibrated for its own accuracy—offered no corrective example, whereas the conservative AI modeled a degree of uncertainty close to what their performance warranted. The benefit therefore lies in the match between display and learner. These results connect several literatures. AI probability output is a scalable alternative to conventional calibration training [Lichtenstein and Fischhoff, 1980, Stone et al., 2023], conveying trial-by-trial uncertainty rather than summary feedback—though it may afford only global adjustment without improving the mapping between stimuli and certainty [Keren, 1991]. Our findings also complement work on human-aligned AI confidence, which shows that decision-makers integrate AI predictions most effectively when the AI’s confidence aligns with their own [Corvelo Benz and Gomez Rodriguez, 2023, 2025]: we provide a learning counterpart, in which an AI whose certainty sits below that of an over-extreme learner draws that learner toward better calibration. Practically, this implies that AI confidence displays may need to be calibrated not only to the model’s empirical accuracy but to the learner’s current state: a display can be miscalibrated for the AI and still teach a novice well if it models uncertainty appropriate for the learner. For highly accurate systems, whose well-calibrated outputs are necessarily concentrated near zero and one [Guo et al., 2017], deliberately tempered displays may better support learning, creating a tension between optimizing for the model and for the person. Notably, AI-assisted decisions can be more accurate when the AI is made more extreme, eliciting greater trust [Vodrahalli et al., 2022]—so what is optimal for human–AI collaboration may be the opposite of what is best for human learning. Several limitations apply. We studied a single perceptual task with novices recruited online, so generalization to other domains and to trained experts remains to be established; the three displays came from a single model at one accuracy level, and whether the pattern holds for less accurate or differently distributed models is unknown. Learning was assessed within the same session, so we cannot speak to durability or transfer. These point to natural extensions: displays might be adapted to each learner, tempering the AI’s certainty to their current ability, and tested across domains, expertise levels, and longer retention intervals. Our paradigm was also deliberately passive—participants judged, then observed—which makes it the minimal form of AI-assisted decision education. That minimal form moved calibration but not condition-specific discrimination, suggesting that adaptive instruction, in which an AI responds to a learner’s particular errors, may be required for stimulus-level learning. In short, exposure to miscalibrated AI probability output taught novices to hold beliefs that better matched reality. AI-assisted decision education is therefore feasible, but it inherits an alignment problem of its own: the probability that faithfully represents a model, and the signal that best teaches need not coincide. This does not license covertly misreporting model uncertainty. It implies instead that a model’s calibrated probability and a pedagogical assistance signal are distinct design objects: the former should remain available and accurately disclosed, while any transformation of the latter should be explicit, purpose-specific, and governed. Attending to that distinction may be essential to designing AI that helps improve human probabilistic thinking.

4.2 Experimental Methods 

After familiarization with blast and non-blast images, participants completed three phases of probability judgments: Phase 1 with ground-truth feedback after each judgment, Phase 2 with AI predictions according to assigned condition (categorical, original, or recalibrated), and Phase 3 with no feedback and a performance-based bonus. Comparing Phases 1 and 3 assesses the impact of AI exposure. 

4.2.1 Participants 

Data was collected from N = 300 participants after exclusions, 100 for each of three between-subjects conditions differing in the kind of AI output they observed in Phase 2 of the experiment: 1. original probability output of an overly conservative AI 2. recalibrated probability output of the same AI, now well-calibrated 3. categorical predictions from the same AI, serving as a control condition providing binary classifications (i.e., blast, non-blast) Sample size was based on a previous, similar experiment in which ground-truth feedback and AI predictions were presented together after every training trial rather than in separate phases. That experiment produced credible effects of AI variant with N = 100 per condition despite divided attention, so we expected the present design to yield effects at least as large and adopted the same sample size. Participants were U.S. residents recruited through Cloud Research, completing the study online via Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Participants were paid $3.00, plus a $4.00 bonus with chance equal to 1 minus their mean squared error (Brier score) on trials in the final testing phase (Phase 3). All procedures were approved by the Indiana University Human Research Protection Program Institutional Review Board (Protocol #24488), and all participants provided informed consent before beginning the study. The study was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/jckpb/overview?view_only=93b1cf3ce2e047e591fca6a129d553d9 ). A total of 327 participants were recruited and completed the experiment. 27 were excluded according to the preregistered criteria (i.e., failing either of two attention check questions or pasting responses for all text response fields), leaving a final sample of N = 300 , with 100 participants in each condition. The attention check questions were "Have you ever used a computer?" ("yes" or "no"), and "I work 14 months in a year." ("true" or "false"). Although we detected 48 participants pasting a text response at least once over the course of the experiment, 44 of them did so on less than four trials, and none pasted on more than 41% of trials with text fields, so no participants were excluded based on that criterion. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 77 years ( M = 41 .4, SD = 11 .9; 57.67% women, 41.33% men, 0.33% other, 0.67% prefer not to say; 76% White, 11% Black or African American, 7% Asian, 1% Native American or Pacific Islander, 3% multiple races selected, 1% other, 1% prefer not to say; 9.33% Hispanic or Latino, 87.33% non-Hispanic or Latino, 3.33% other). The analyses of reliability, resolution, binarized accuracy, and the linear in log-odds model were preregistered. The Blackwell informativeness analysis was not preregistered and is exploratory. 

4.2.2 Materials 

Stimuli consisted of 300 digital images of Wright-stained white blood cells taken from anonymized patient peripheral blood smears at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, as described in Trueblood et al. [2018]. The images were acquired using a CellaVision DM96 automated digital cell morphology instrument (CellaVision AB, Lund, Sweden). Ground truth was the unanimous classification of three hematopathology faculty at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, who also rated the typicality of each image on a 1–5 scale; averaged typicality ratings were used to assign images to five difficulty categories from very easy to very hard [Epping et al., 2026a]. Further detail on classification and typicality rating is given in Trueblood et al. [2018] and in the Supplement. For decision training and testing, the present experiment used the subset of 300 images from this larger stimulus bank that were used for machine-learning model training and testing in Epping et al. [2026b]. The remaining 249 images from the original stimulus set were not used as experimental stimuli here because they were used for crowdsourced label recalibration in that prior work; they were, however, used for stimulus familiarization. We selected one of the CNNs trained on the 300-image subset in Epping et al. [2026b] to generate the AI predictions presented to participants. 9Miscalibrated AI improves human calibration PREPRINT 

White blood cell classification provides a useful perceptual learning task because the stimuli are visually complex and vary in difficulty. Blast and non-blast cells differ along multiple morphological dimensions, and the non-blast category is heterogeneous because it includes multiple mature cell types. This complexity makes the task well suited for studying subjective probability judgments and calibration. 

4.2.3 AI model and output variants 

The AI predictions used in this study are from one of the CNNs trained and tested in Epping et al. [2026b]. CNNs tested by Epping et al. varied in how their training data was derived from crowdsourced judgments from skilled annotators. After pre-training on the ImageNet database, the particular CNN we used was fine-tuned to predict the crowdsourced mean subjective probability that a white blood cell is a blast. Crowdsourced labels were created by recalibrating individual judgments, averaging by stimulus, and recalibrating the aggregate, which reduced noise and bias and allowed the CNN to learn continuous, human-like uncertainty rather than discrete labels. The selected model was the most accurate of those investigated by Epping et al. [2026b] ( ∼94%) while remaining aligned with expert judgments [Hasan et al., 2024], but at a cost to calibration: its probability output is overly conservative. Note that, despite there being equal numbers of blast and non-blast, the AI predicted that 53.7% of the stimuli were blast (using a decision threshold of .5). Given the model’s high accuracy, a more appropriate calibration would have produced more certain outputs, closer to 0 and 1. We therefore recalibrated the model outputs using a linear-in-log-odds (LLO) model, which models the empirical log-odds of an outcome as a linear function of the probability judgment in log-odds and thus provides a monotonic correction of systematic miscalibration [Turner et al., 2014]. This created an equally accurate but appropriately certain AI variant. The same approach was used to recalibrate crowdsourced judgments in Epping et al. [2026b], and is described further below. The LLO transformation was restricted to correct only over- or under-extremity, altering certainty without shifting the decision boundary. The original and recalibrated variants therefore had identical classification accuracy ( ∼94%) but differed in apparent certainty. Because the original CNN was primarily under-extreme, recalibration made its outputs more certain, with probabilities ≤ .1 or ≥ .9 on over 80% of stimuli (Fig. S1), reducing reliability from .036 to .005. Finally, we created the categorical AI variant by binarizing the original model output probabilities, treating 50% as a decision threshold such that probabilities > . 5 become classifications of "blast" and probabilities < . 5 become classifications of "non-blast". 

4.2.4 Procedure 

The experiment was built using jsPsych [De Leeuw et al., 2023] and hosted on Pavlovia [Open Science Tools]. The primary experimental task was to provide a subjective probability that the white blood cell present in a given image was a blast cell (as opposed to a non-blast cell). Because participants were expected to be novices, they began by observing 40 examples (20 blast, 20 non-blast), sampled from the set of 249 images in proportion to the overall difficulty distribution and not seen again. Participants were then introduced to the probability judgment task, and the bonus rule was explained qualitatively: greater certainty would improve their chances if correct but sharply worsen them if wrong. Familiarization and instruction details are given in the Supplement. Participants then completed Phase 1, a training phase of 100 trials (50 blast, 50 non-blast) in randomized order. They were informed that cells were equally likely to be blast or non-blast. On each trial, participants viewed a cell image and entered a probability between 0% and 100% that it was a blast, after which the true cell type was displayed alongside their response. Responses and feedback were self-paced. Aggregate performance feedback, in the form of the bonus probability they would have earned, was shown after every 50 trials. Of the 100 trials, 20 came from a common set repeated in every phase (10 blast, 10 non-blast, balanced across difficulty), with the remaining 80 sampled without replacement per participant in proportion to the difficulty distribution. Details are given in the Supplement. Participants were then introduced to the AI, described as trained to classify cells as blast or non-blast with an accuracy of about 94%, and told that they would see its predictions instead of the true cell type in Phase 2. Phase 2 comprised 100 trials (50 blast, 50 non-blast) structured as in Phase 1, again with aggregate feedback every 50 trials and the same 20 repeated common stimuli. After each judgment, participants saw the image, their own judgment, and the AI prediction, shown as either a probability rounded to the nearest percent or a categorical classification, depending on condition. Finally, participants completed Phase 3, a testing phase of 180 trials in randomized order with no feedback of any kind. Of these, 20 were the repeated common set, 120 (60 blast, 60 non-blast) were sampled without replacement in proportion to the difficulty distribution, and 40 were repeats drawn from those 120, used for intra-rater reliability baselines. Stimuli sampled for one phase were removed from the pool for later phases. 10 Miscalibrated AI improves human calibration PREPRINT 

After Phase 3, participants completed an exploratory survey on the perceptual attributes underlying their judgments [Tsutsui et al., 2023], their everyday use of AI, and demographics; these responses are not analyzed here. Participants were then told whether they had won the bonus, which 77% did. Mean completion time was 56.5 minutes (median 48.2, SD 32.1). 

4.3 Analytical Methods 

All analyses share a common approach: comparing Phase 1 to Phase 3 gives the expected change in a variable of interest before and after AI exposure, and comparing those changes across conditions, as in a difference-in-differences analysis, accounts for baseline differences and provides the primary test of whether AI variants produced distinct shifts. Only first judgments of each stimulus were used, giving 100 Phase 1 and 120 Phase 3 trials per participant. All of the models described below were fit with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) in Stan [Stan Development Team, 2025] using the CmdStanR [Gabry et al., 2025] and brms [Bürkner, 2018] packages in R (v4.4.0). Each model was run using 8 chains with 1,000 tuning iterations and 8,000 post-tuning iterations, for a total of 64,000 post-tuning iterations. Convergence was assessed using ˆR ( ˆR < 1.01 indicates convergence) and bulk and tail effective sample size (ESS). Across all models, all parameters and estimands had ˆR < 1.002 , and ESS values were well above conventional thresholds. Weakly informative or uninformative priors were used. Full diagnostics and priors are reported in the Supplement. 

4.3.1 Reliability and Resolution (Calibration Error and Discrimination) 

We assess judgment quality via the Brier score decomposition into uncertainty, reliability, and resolution [Murphy, 1973], which separates how well judgments are calibrated from how well they distinguish blast from non-blast cells. We focus on reliability and resolution, as uncertainty depends only on the overall frequency of blast cells, fixed at 50% throughout. Details on interpreting reliability and resolution are provided in the Supplement. 

Reliability is a measure of calibration error: the mean squared difference between subjective probabilities and the actual relative frequencies among trials where that probability is given. Let xj ∈ [0 , 1] denote the judgment on stimulus (cell) 

j, with true cell type yj = 1 for blast and yj = 0 for non-blast; reliability summarizes the correspondence between xj

and the observed Pobs (yj = 1 | xj ). Perfect calibration gives a reliability of 0, and the worst possible value of 1 occurs when judgments are completely certain but always wrong. 

Resolution measures how well judgments distinguish blast from non-blast cells relative to the overall relative frequency: the mean squared difference between the overall frequency ¯y and the actual frequencies for different judgments 

Pobs (yj = 1 | xj ). With ¯y = .5 as in our experiment, resolution ranges from 0, for an individual responding 50% to every cell and thus providing no information beyond the base rate, to .25, earned when blast and non-blast cells are judged with mutually exclusive probabilities. For each participant and phase, judgments were divided into five equal-width bins, and within each bin we computed the mean judgment and observed blast frequency. Reliability and resolution were then computed as weighted mean squared differences, with weights proportional to bin counts. Because participants often round to multiples of 5% or 10%, bin assignment can depend on whether boundaries are left- or right-inclusive; we therefore averaged metrics across both definitions. After calculating the reliability and resolution for each participant in Phase 1 and Phase 3 separately, we model each as the outcome in their own Bayesian linear multilevel (mixed-effects) regression. The models include condition and phase as predictors and allow intercepts to vary by participant to account for individual differences in performance that carry across phases. These and all later variable effects are modeled as being normally distributed. Once a model has been fit, we summarize the posterior by computing the quantity of interest for each posterior sample and reporting its maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate and 95% highest density interval (HDI). We did this for the mean predicted metric for each condition at each phase, its change from Phase 1 to Phase 3, and the difference between conditions in that change. This emphasizes estimands that directly address our research questions rather than regression coefficients, which can be difficult to interpret in complex models [Arel-Bundock, 2026]. Parameter estimates are provided in the Supplement.

4.3.3 Characterization of calibration errors 

Reliability captures the overall degree of miscalibration but not its form: whether judgments are more or less certain than warranted ( over-/under-extremity [Griffin and Brenner, 2004]), or systematically too high or too low ( over-/under-prediction ). To distinguish these, we used the linear in log-odds (LLO) model [Turner et al., 2014, Epping et al., 2026a, Tversky and Fox, 1995, Gonzalez and Wu, 1999]. When we analyzed calibration via reliability, we looked at the relationship between judgments xj and observed relative frequencies Pobs (yj = 1 | xj ). The LLO model imposes a particular functional form to the same general relationship. As the name suggests, the LLO model consists of a linear relation between the log-odds of a binary ground truth outcome P (yj = 1) and the logit of the subjective probability judgment xj :logit (PLLO (yj = 1 | xj )) = αlogit (xj ) + β (1) The parameters α, β ∈ (−∞ , ∞) capture the systematic calibration errors described above. α captures over-/under-extremity : α > 1 indicates under-extremity, where participants express less certainty than warranted; α < 1 indicates over-extremity, where they express more certainty than warranted; and α = 1 indicates no extremity bias. The limiting cases as α → 0 and below 0 are described in the Supplement. The parameter β captures over-/under-prediction , with values of β > 0 indicating a tendency to under-predict the probability that a cell is blast, and β < 0 indicating a tendency to over-predict the probability that a cell is blast. An absence of over-/under-prediction bias would be reflected by β = 0 .The LLO model can be understood as a model-based calibration curve: solving Equation (1) for PLLO (yj = 1 | xj )

gives the probability that a cell is blast as a function of the subjective judgment (see Supplement). We also use this model to characterize the (mis)calibration of the original AI output and to construct the recalibrated AI output, as described above. Because the LLO model has the form of a logistic regression with the logit of the subjective probability as a predictor, we fit it as a Bayesian multilevel logistic regression with true cell type as the outcome and condition, phase, logit judgment, and their interactions as predictors, allowing α and β and their change across phases to vary by participant. The full model specification is given in the Supplement. 

4.3.4 Blackwell Informativeness of Recalibrated Subjective Probabilities 

Resolution summarizes the full distribution of observed relative frequencies by a single number, its spread. To evaluate that distribution as a whole, we employ the Blackwell informativeness framework. Blackwell [1951] shows that a decision-maker who maximizes expected utility, whose overall expected utility is therefore a convex function of beliefs, will always prefer well-calibrated judgments that are more dispersed—that is, a mean-preserving spread . The conceptual motivation and formal treatment are given in the Supplement. Our analysis is as follows: (a) recalibrate probability judgments for each individual in each phase, then (b) compare the full distributions to determine whether, and to what degree, Phase 3 recalibrated judgments are a mean-preserving spread of Phase 1 recalibrated judgments in each condition. If this is true, then the judgments at Phase 3 are said to be 

Blackwell more informative than the judgments at Phase 1, supporting better decisions for all payoff functions. If no such relationship holds, then there is no context-free ranking of judgments: some decision payoff structures may favor Phase 1 judgments, while others may favor Phase 3. We derive recalibrated judgments ˜xij for each stimulus j judged by participant i from the hierarchical LLO model predictions, ˜xij = PLLO (yj = 1 | i, x ij ), which provide a continuous rather than binned recalibration and reduce the influence of outliers through partial pooling. We then overlay the pooled distributions of MAP recalibrated judgments for Phases 1 and 3 in each condition, together with the recalibrated AI output for reference. As a more precise test, we plot the cumulative area under each distribution’s empirical CDF against ˜xij : Phase 3 judgments are a mean-preserving spread of Phase 1 judgments if and only if the two distributions share the same value at ˜xj = 1 and Phase 3 exceeds Phase 1 for all ˜xj ∈ (0 , 1) . ## S5 Extended Methods 

S5.1 Experimental Methods S5.1.1 Stimulus classification and typicality ratings 

For the full 549-image set, three hematopathology faculty from the Department of Pathology, Microbiology and Immunology at Vanderbilt University Medical Center independently classified each image as “blast” or “non-blast” and rated how typical each image was of its type on a scale from 1 to 5. The 549-image set includes only images for which all three experts agreed on the blast/non-blast classification. This consensus classification was used as the ground truth cell type. The average of the expert typicality ratings was used to assign images to five difficulty categories: very easy, easy, medium, hard, and very hard [Epping et al., 2026]. 

S5.1.2 Familiarization and task introduction 

Because participants were generally expected to be novices without previous experience with white blood cell morphology, they began by observing 40 examples of white blood cell images (20 blast, 20 non-blast) after an overview of the study. For each participant, examples were pseudo-randomly sampled without replacement in proportion to the overall distribution of difficulty, and were not seen again for the duration of the experiment. Participants saw 4 examples at a time, 2 non-blast on the left and 2 blast on the right, and could observe each set for as long as desired. Next, participants were introduced to the task of assigning subjective probability judgments to each cell, corresponding to their degree of certainty in the cell being blast or non-blast. At this time, we also qualitatively explained how their chance of winning the $4.00 bonus would be determined by performance in Phase 3, such that judgments with greater certainty would increase chances if they were correct, but would dramatically worsen chances if they were wrong. 

S5.1.3 Trial structure and instructions 

In each of the 100 Phase 1 trials, participants were shown the image of the cell in the center of the screen, below which they provided a number between 0% and 100% in a text box, answering the question “What is the probability the cell is a Blast?”. After responding, participants were shown the true cell type (e.g., “True cell type: Non-Blast ”), while simultaneously being shown their response and the cell image. There was no constraint on the time an individual could take to submit their response, or to observe the feedback screen. After clicking “Next”, the next trial began. After 50 trials, participants saw a break screen where they were informed of the probability they would have had to win the $4.00 if this had been Phase 3 (as determined by their average Brier score). This chance was recalculated for the second set of 50 trials and shown again at the end of Phase 1, providing a kind of aggregate performance feedback in addition to the trial-by-trial feedback. Before Phase 2, participants were introduced to the AI model, and told that the AI had been “trained to classify cells as blast or non-blast” and “has an accuracy of about 94% on images of cells similar to the ones you will see”. We told the participants that after each judgment in Phase 2, they would see the AI’s predictions instead of the true cell type, suggesting (but not explicitly directing) “You may use this information to help as you learn to make better probability judgments of your own”. After making each judgment, participants were shown the image, their own judgment, and the AI prediction as either a probability rounded to the nearest percent (e.g., “AI prediction: 86% probability of being a Blast”) or a classification (e.g., “AI prediction: Blast”), depending on the condition. In Phase 3, participants were given a chance to take a break after every 45 trials, without receiving feedback on their chance to win the bonus. 

S5.1.4 Post-experiment survey 

After completing Phase 3, participants answered a survey about the perceptual attributes they used to make their judgments, the weight of these attributes in the judgment process, and whether the AI exposure helped them learn how to distinguish blast from non-blast. Some of these questions were open-ended, while others systematically investigated the use of specific morphological attributes defined in previous literature [Tsutsui et al., 2023]. Participants also answered questions about their everyday use of AI, and demographics. These questions were included for exploratory purposes and are not analyzed in this paper. Finally, participants were told whether they won the $4.00 bonus and given the opportunity to view the specifics of the bonus probability calculation via Brier score.

S5.2.2 Interpretation of reliability and resolution 

Perfect calibration would result in reliability equal to 0, achieved by an individual providing subjective probabilities which perfectly match the corresponding relative frequencies of blast cells among those judgments. Perfect reliability can arise in multiple ways, 

12 Miscalibrated AI improves human calibration PREPRINT 

including always giving correct 0% or 100% judgments, always responding 50% when blasts occur on 50% of trials, or providing well-calibrated probabilities between these extremes. A score of 1 is the worst possible value and occurs when judgments are completely certain but always wrong, such as assigning every blast cell a 0% probability of being a blast. For resolution, suppose that ¯y = .5 as it is in our experiment. Despite perfect calibration, an individual responding 50% for every cell earns a resolution of 0, the minimum possible value, reflecting that their judgments provide no useful information beyond the overall frequency. At best, resolution is equal to the uncertainty , or variance of the true cell type, which is .5(1 − .5) = .25 for our experiment. This is earned when blast and non-blast cells are judged with mutually exclusive probabilities. One could achieve this in conjunction with perfect reliability by assigning all blast cells 100% and all non-blast cells 0%. However, they could also achieve this by assigning every blast cell a probability > 50% and every non-blast cell a probability < 50% , or by answering every cell incorrectly but with complete confidence—though this comes at a cost to reliability. 

S5.2.3 Binarized accuracy: random-effects structure 

For the binarized accuracy model, we allowed intercepts and effects of phase, cell type, and their interaction to vary by participant, and for these effects to be correlated among participants. We also allowed intercepts and effects of condition, phase, and their interaction to vary by stimulus, and to be correlated among stimuli, capturing systematic differences in stimulus difficulty and learnability within different conditions. 

S5.2.4 LLO model: limiting values, calibration curve, and random effects 

As α increases above 1, probability judgments become increasingly under-extreme. As α → 0, judgments become increasingly over-extreme, until α = 0 where they become entirely undiagnostic of the true category of a cell, corresponding to resolution of 0. Below 0, α indicates that judgments are diagnostically inverted, with non-blast cells getting higher subjective probabilities of being blast, and vice versa. One way to think of the LLO is as a model-based calibration curve, a commonly used visualization of probability calibration errors. Solving the linear form for PLLO (yj = 1 | xj ) gives the probability that a cell is a blast as a function of subjective probability judgments: 

PLLO (yj = 1 | xj ) = 11 + e−(α logit (xj )+β) (S1) Plotting this function alongside actual probability judgments shows how the LLO model is capable of capturing systematic errors. For the LLO model, the value of β for each condition and phase is obtained from the overall intercept and the main effects of condition and phase along with their interaction, while the value of α is obtained from all terms interacting with the logit judgment predictor. We allowed baseline α and β and their change from Phase 1 to Phase 3 to vary among participants via by-participant varying intercepts, effects of phase and logit judgment, their interaction, and correlations among these effects. 

## S6 Recalibrated Elicited Beliefs and the Blackwell Ordering 

This section provides the conceptual background for the Blackwell informativeness analysis reported in the main text. We first motivate the distribution of recalibrated elicited beliefs as the object of interest, then show why the Brier resolution term alone can fail to capture decision-relevant differences between sets of judgments, and finally connect this reasoning to the Blackwell ordering. The formal treatment—including proofs, the Brier decomposition, and the procedure for constructing recalibrated beliefs from data—is given in the section titled “Formal Treatment of Recalibrated Elicited Beliefs, Resolution, and Decision Value”. 

S6.1 From Calibration to Recalibrated Elicited Beliefs 

The standard approach in the psychology of probability judgment evaluates elicited beliefs using proper scoring rules, especially the Brier score, and its decomposition into reliability, resolution, and uncertainty. This decomposition is useful because it separates miscalibration from the extent to which judgments distinguish cases with different outcome frequencies. In this paper, we build directly on that tradition, but use it to motivate a richer object of analysis: the distribution of recalibrated elicited beliefs .To see the idea, consider our medical image classification task. Let Y = 1 denote that an image contains a blast cell and Y = 0 

denote that it does not. A participant reports an elicited belief p that Y = 1 . Suppose reports are grouped into bins indexed by k. In bin k, let pk denote the participant’s reported probability, wk the fraction of observations in that bin, and qk the actual frequency of blast cells among images in that bin. Thus pk is what the participant says, while qk is what that report empirically means. The Brier decomposition already uses both objects. The reliability term compares pk to qk , measuring miscalibration. The resolution term measures how much the qk ’s vary around the base rate. Thus the standard decomposition implicitly replaces raw reports with their empirical meanings. We call these empirical meanings recalibrated elicited beliefs :

S6.3 Connection to Blackwell Comparisons 

The preceding example also clarifies the role of Blackwell comparisons. Blackwell’s criterion asks when one information structure is more valuable than another for every possible decision problem. In a two-state setting, this is equivalent to comparing the full distributions of posterior beliefs by convex order. The implication is straightforward. If one distribution of recalibrated elicited beliefs Blackwell-dominates another, then it supports weakly better decisions for all payoff functions. If no such dominance holds, then there is no context-free ranking: different decision problems may favor different systems. This is not a limitation of the approach. It is the correct conclusion. In medical classification, the practical value of learning depends on the intervention threshold. When the threshold changes, the relative value of different posterior distributions can change as well. The empirical task is therefore not to force all learning systems into a single scalar ranking, but to recover the distribution of recalibrated elicited beliefs and examine how that distribution supports decisions across relevant thresholds. The procedure implementing this comparison, and the resulting comparison of Phase 1 and Phase 3 judgments across the three conditions, are reported in the main text. The formal basis for the framework described above—including the calibration property of recalibrated beliefs, the Brier decomposition, the connection to threshold-based decisions and the Blackwell/convex order, and the procedure for constructing recalibrated beliefs from data—is given in the following section.
