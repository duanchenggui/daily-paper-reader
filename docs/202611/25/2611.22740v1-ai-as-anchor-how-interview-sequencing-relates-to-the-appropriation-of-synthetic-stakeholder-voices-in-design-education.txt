# AI as Anchor: How Interview Sequencing Relates to the Appropriation of Synthetic Stakeholder Voices in Design Education ∗

## OSNAT MOKRYN †, OSHRAT AYALON, and NOGA DINES, Information Systems, University of Haifa, Israel 

## ORIT SHAER, Computer Science, Wellesley College, USA 

Designers increasingly use large language models to simulate stakeholder conversations. Yet it remains unclear how AI Simulated Stakeholders (AIS) are taken up when used alongside human stakeholder engagement. We report a field study in a single-institution, single-semester design course in which 8 teams (N=21) constructed, evaluated, and peer-reviewed an initial AIS. Then, the teams interviewed both AIS and human stakeholders in different sequences: AIS-interview-first or human-interview-first. Drawing on interview transcripts, design artifacts, course-embedded reflections, and post-course qualitative sessions, we examine exploratory patterns associated with these sequencing conditions. We find that students did not describe AIS as a neutral addition to the process. Instead, the order in which AIS and human stakeholders were formally interviewed was associated with different ways of interpreting and using information from the two sources. When the AIS interview came first, it provided a broad but often general frame that students later reconsidered through human interviews. When the AIS interview followed human engagement, AIS was more often used to revisit and validate insights already grounded in lived experience. Across both interview orders, students described AIS as a point of reference, or “anchor,” but its reported role varied with its position in the formal interview sequence. In the final designs, a larger proportion of prominent human-stakeholder-derived concerns than AIS-derived concerns had corresponding design elements. We contribute: (1) a characterization of AIS as a reference point whose reported role varies with its position in the formal interview sequence, (2) exploratory empirical evidence that the implemented interview-order conditions were associated with distinct patterns of interpreting and using stakeholder input, and (3) implications for integrating AIS into design workflows in ways that preserve direct engagement with human stakeholders. 1 Introduction 

Sociotechnical systems represent complex configurations where technology, human actors, and social structures interact dynamically, creating emergent behaviors that cannot be predicted from analyzing individual components in isolation [5, 20]. Designing such systems requires engaging with the perspectives of those affected by them, raising questions about whose interests are represented and whose voices are heard. Participatory design addresses this by involving stakeholders directly in design processes, while value-sensitive design provides methods for eliciting and embedding stakeholder values into technical decisions [20, 47]. In practice, however, participatory approaches face constraints. Limited time, resources, and access often restrict designers to engaging only a small number of stakeholders, providing a partial and sometimes unbalanced view of the design space.

Large language models can generate interactive agents that simulate stakeholder dialogue. Prior work has examined such systems either as substitutes for human participants or as standalone design aids, with mixed findings regarding their realism, usefulness, and limitations [1, 3, 31, 50]. Less is known about how such agents function when used alongside authentic stakeholder engagement within an ongoing design process. A key question is therefore, not only whether AI-generated stakeholders are useful, but how their introduction into the process relates to the interpretation of subsequent input from authentic stakeholder. In this study, all teams constructed and evaluated an AIS before sequence assignment. Sequencing therefore refers specifically to the order of the two formal interviews, rather than to initial exposure versus non-exposure to AIS. When designers engage with different sources of information over time, interview order may be associated with what is noticed, trusted, and carried forward into design decisions. This relationship remains underexplored in hybrid human-AI stakeholder engagement. In this paper, we examine AI Simulated Stakeholders (AIS) , LLM-generated agents designed to emulate concrete stakeholder roles through conversational interaction. Rather than treating AIS as replacements for human stakeholder participation, we study how they are used alongside human stakeholders and how their position within the design process relates to designers’ interpretation and use of stakeholder input. We investigate this question in a naturalistic field study conducted in a mixed-level design course. Eight teams (N=21) constructed AIS before sequence assignment and then interviewed AIS and human stakeholders in one of two formal interview orders: AIS-interview-first or human-interview-first, as part of a semester-long project. This setting allows us to examine how students who are novice designers navigate synthetic and authentic voices over time and how concerns from each source are incorporated into evolving design work. Here, we use appropriation to refer to how students take up AIS in practice: constructing and adapting it, interpreting and evaluating its outputs, validating or challenging them, and integrating, repurposing, or rejecting them within the design process. We ask: 

RQ1 : How do novice designers appropriate AIS during early-stage design work? 

RQ2 : How does the order of the formal AIS and human stakeholder interviews relate to students’ expectations, interpretations, and interview practices? 

RQ3 : How are insights from AIS and human stakeholders reflected in the design decisions students produce? While the study is situated in a university design studio, we approach this setting as a field site for observing how novice designers engage with synthetic and authentic voices, rather than as an evaluation of educational outcomes.

1. AIS as a reference point: We characterize how students used AI Simulated Stakeholders as provisional or secondary reference points across two interview orders. 2. Interview order and interpretation: We provide exploratory evidence that the implemented sequencing conditions were associated with different reported ways of interpreting and using stakeholder input. 3. Implications for design practice: We outline considerations for integrating AIS into design workflows in ways that support engagement with human stakeholders. 2 Related Work 

We examine AI Simulated Stakeholders (AIS) as one instance of AI-mediated, synthetic stakeholder representations that are woven into early-stage design work, alongside traditional stakeholder engagement in sociotechnical systems. In here, we focus on how novice designers appropriate AIS in practice, how they navigate synthetic and authentic voices over time, and how these engagements relate to the design decisions they ultimately produce. This study examines how novice designers learn to engage stakeholder voices in sociotechnical design contexts where participatory methods are central to practice. We draw on research in design empathy and personas, emerging work on synthetic users and AI-generated personas, and studies of human–AI workflows in design education. 

2.1 Stakeholder Engagement in Sociotechnical Design Education 

Design students learning to work on sociotechnical systems are typically taught to engage diverse stakeholders directly, drawing on participatory design and value-sensitive design traditions. Participatory design emerged from Scandinavian workplace democracy movements, establishing the principle that those affected by design should participate in the design process [6, 15]. This tradition emphasizes direct involvement of the various stakeholders that represent various user groups needs, while making sure to also include underrepresented voices [47]. The disability rights movement’s principle of “Nothing About Us Without Us” [9] further reinforces that authentic participation cannot be substituted by designer imagination or proxies. Authentic stakeholder voices are central to participatory design because they surface lived experience, contextual nuance, and power asymmetries that are often flattened or abstracted in designer-generated representations [19, 32, 47]. Value-centered design examines how technologies shape and are shaped by values and moral concerns [20]. As moral concerns and values are embedded within the social aspects of organizations, they are often invisible to designers. Value design relies on direct engagement with affected communities to ground requirements, negotiate trade-offs, and justify design choices in lived experience rather than abstractions [19]. In practice, however, real-world projects operate under time and budget limitations that restrict the number of stakeholders who can be meaningfully engaged. Delgado et al. [14] found a tension between aspirations for empowerment and practical constraints such as resources, timelines, and expertise, leading to the adoption of algorithmic proxies that serve as user stand-ins. This is particularly problematic when stakeholder groups include marginalized or hard-to-reach populations who may be geographically dispersed, have limited availability (e.g., doctors, lawyers, etc.), or face barriers to participation [24, 36]. 

2.2 Design Empathy and Personas 

Personas are fictional characters that represent human stakeholder segments [10]. Personas help designers form a mental model of users, enabling them to anticipate behavior in scenarios while avoiding projecting their own needs onto the design [37]. Personas’ success lies in that people remember individuals better than abstractions, empathize with characters in stories, and adopt perspectives more readily when given a concrete, humanized anchor [22]. A key benefit is that personas enhance designer empathy and understanding of stakeholders [35, 40]. Grudin [22] describes design empathy as operating through sophisticated cognitive mechanisms rooted in the fundamental human ability to attribute mental states to others, Theory of mind. People “find it natural to create and use models of other people”, drawing on basic cognitive abilities to interact in complex social environments [22]. In design contexts, this translates into designers using personas to construct detailed mental models of user experiences, motivations, and 4 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer constraints. Empathy involves affective matching, self-oriented perspective-taking, and other-oriented perspective-taking [11]. Kouprie and Visser [32] distinguish levels of design empathy ranging from superficial understanding to embodied experience that might be achieved when the persona’s design is rich and detailed. Prior work has shown that personas, while widely used, often abstract away contextual nuance and can foreground designer assumptions rather than stakeholders’ lived experiences [33, 44, 48]. Personas are meant to represent users who cannot participate directly, yet the act of representation without partic-ipation risks epistemic injustice [17]. Personas, as representations of users, inevitably make value judgments about which characteristics matter and whose voices are centered. Hence, Costanza-Chock [12] argues that design justice requires moving beyond representation to actual participation by affected communities. 

2.3 Algorithmic Proxies and AI-Generated Stakeholder Representations 

As participatory design faces practical constraints [24], researchers and practitioners have increasingly turned to computational representations that stand in for human participants. These simulated proxies take several forms, each with distinct characteristics and applications. 

Algorithmic proxies broadly encompass computational representations that encode human needs or behaviors through data-driven models rather than direct engagement [25]. Delgado et al. [14] demonstrate that practitioners increasingly adopt proxy-based tactics as pragmatic compromises between participatory ideals and real-world constraints of time, resources, and stakeholder availability. Information-driven personas, termed “imaginary people representing real numbers” were created through computational analysis of online behavior patterns and social media mining [2, 28]. Salminen et al. [45], in their review of the use of algorithm proxies, show that data-driven personas have proliferated across domains from healthcare to urban planning as scalable alternatives to traditional ethnographic methods. Recent research has begun to examine the usability of AI-generated personas themselves. Kapania et al. [31] demonstrate that when large language models are used as research participant proxies, they create what they term “surrogate effects,” which raises epistemological concerns about whether these models can represent lived experience, social agency, or the dynamic and contested nature of stakeholder needs. Kaate et al. [29] compared chat-based and profile personas, identifying issues such as verbosity, information overload, and the so-called “fourth wall” effect, where users felt they were conversing with a description of a persona rather than the persona itself. Their work highlights how different presentation formats influence user perceptions and task performance. In contrast, our study investigates how and when to integrate the AI Simulated Stakeholders (AIS) in design processes. 

AI-generated personas represent a specific application where large language models are prompted to produce enhanced character profiles for design exploration. Goel et al. [21] examined how novice designers could use GPT-3 to assist in persona creation, finding that while AI-generated personas were comparable to manually created ones, they were rated lower on credibility, empathy, and memorability. Their work focused primarily on using AI to elaborate and refine static persona documents rather than enabling conversational interaction. 

LLM social simulations deploy multiple synthetic agents to replicate populations or communities for large-scale research purposes. Anthis et al. [3] systematically examine the fundamental challenges of using LLMs as human research proxies and identify five recurring issues: diversity (limited representation of demographic variety), bias 

(systematic distortions toward majority perspectives), sycophancy (tendency to agree with users), alienness (responses that feel artificial or non-human), and generalization (poor performance across different contexts). Smrke et al. [50] AI as Anchor 5compared LLM-generated personas with human-crafted personas in obesity research contexts, finding that LLM-generated personas were generally perceived as believable, relatable, and informative, with some even outperforming human-crafted personas on empathy and likability dimensions. 

AI Simulated Stakeholders (AIS) represent a distinct approach within this landscape. Unlike static AI-generated personas, AIS engage in real-time dialogue while maintaining specific stakeholder identities. Unlike population-level social simulations, AIS instantiate individual stakeholder roles within specific sociotechnical contexts, enabling designers to conduct one-on-one conversational interviews. Most critically, AIS are designed not as replacements for human participation, but as augmentation tools that can supplement limited stakeholder access while preparing designers for authentic engagement. The introduction of generative AI in design practice raises questions about whether AI should augment human capabilities or substitute for human participation entirely [42]. This distinction is particularly relevant when stakeholder access is constrained. Wright and McCarthy [54] emphasize the role of empathy in designer–user relations; it remains unclear whether AI-mediated interactions can cultivate similar levels of empathic understanding as traditional human-centered methods. Traditional personas build empathy through the research and synthesis process itself [22], where designers must actively construct mental models from user data. AIS bypass this constructive process, potentially limiting empathy development despite offering conversational interaction. In design education contexts, students face similar constraints to practitioners, such as limited time, access to stakeholders, and inexperience conducting stakeholder research. This makes design courses a particularly relevant site for examining how novice designers learn to navigate stakeholder engagement practices and appropriate new AI tools within those practices. 

2.4 Human-AI Workflows and Timing in Design Practice 

Recent work on human-AI collaboration in design has begun to treat AI tools not as isolated interventions, but as part of evolving workflows that unfold over time. Shin et al. [49] studied human-AI workflows for generating personas, and showed that how tasks are divided between humans and LLMs (e.g., who identifies key characteristics, who groups data, who generates the final persona) shapes both the quality of the output and the designer’s experience. This work frames persona generation as a collaborative process rather than a one-off AI output, which resonates with our interest in how students appropriate AIS over the course of a semester-long project. In design education, there is growing attention to how students learn to work with generative AI as a collaborator or “virtual colleague” in early design stages. Goel et al. [21] investigate how novice designers use AI text-generation tools to support persona creation in a course setting, finding that while AI-generated personas were comparable to manually created ones, they were rated lower on credibility, empathy, and memorability . Interestingly, Jung et al. [27] integrated LLMs with survey data analysis to generate personas that “humanize” data segments. Their PersonaCraft system scored highly on clarity, completeness, fluency, consistency, and credibility in multi-stakeholder evaluations. In design studies, Sattele and Ortiz [46] took a critical qualitative approach by conducting a Mexico City case study that revealed cultural biases and stereotyping risks in AI-generated personas, while proposing scalability benefits for idea generation. Gu et al. [23] found that designers who were using persona-based Synthetic Users during single-session ideation engaged in feedback-seeking, assumption-testing, and scenario role-playing types of interactions, that are impossible with static personas. Our naturalistic study extends this to AIS within complete semester-long design 6 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer processes, examining how timing shapes interaction patterns across expectations, interview practices, and design traceability. However, most of this work focuses on a single use of AI personas or synthetic users within a bounded activity (e.g., a workshop or a single assignment). There is less empirical work that follows novice designers over time in a naturalistic, course-embedded project, examining how the timing of AI use, when in the design process AIS are introduced, shapes students’ expectations, interview practices, and how insights from synthetic and authentic voices are reflected in their design decisions. Our study contributes to this emerging conversation by treating the sequence of AIS and human stakeholder interviews not as an experimental manipulation, but as a lens into how students navigate and differentiate between synthetic and authentic voices in early-stage design work. 

2.5 Research Gap 

While recent literature has identified fundamental limitations in LLM social simulations [3, 29, 50], little is known about how design students appropriate AIS as part of their learning process, especially when conducting stakeholder research for the first time in semester-long projects. Most prior work examines controlled tasks or single-session workshops, whereas our study observes how students navigate synthetic and authentic voices over time in a naturalistic classroom setting. In particular, few empirical studies have examined how the timing of AIS use shapes students’ expectations, interpre-tations, and interview practices in a real design course. It remains unclear how introducing AIS early versus later in the process affects how students prepare for and conduct interviews with human stakeholders, and how they interpret and integrate insights from each. Similarly, there is limited understanding of how insights derived from AIS and from human stakeholders are reflected in the design decisions students produce. Our study addresses this gap by presenting a naturalistic case study of how novice designers appropriate AIS during early-stage design work, how the timing of AIS use shapes their practices, and how insights from synthetic and authentic voices are traced into their design artifacts. 

3 Study Context and Methods 3.1 Course Setting and Pedagogical Structure 

The study was conducted as part of a semester-long university course on sociotechnical system design. Participants were undergraduate and graduate students with varied technical and design experience, representing multiple gender identities and diverse ethnic backgrounds. 

Course structure and timeline. The study was embedded in a 12-week flipped classroom course on ethical design of sociotechnical systems with generative AI. Each week typically combined three components: pre-class preparation, including an assigned reading and a short written response; an in-class discussion connecting the reading to sociotech-nical design; and a hands-on exercise or project activity. The semester-long group project developed incrementally alongside these activities, with students submitting intermediate milestones and receiving feedback throughout the course. Before any group could recruit or interact with human participants, all members were also required to complete human-subject research training. Weeks 1–3 introduced sociotechnical systems and stakeholder perspectives. Students first experimented with LLMs in short in-class exercises and discussed Berg’s [5] sociotechnical analysis of healthcare information systems, using it to examine how technical systems interact with organizational practices and human actors. The following week AI as Anchor 7focused explicitly on identifying stakeholders and understanding how different actors experience the same system, supported by Beede et al.’s [4] human-centered evaluation of an AI system deployed in clinical practice. During this period, students introduced themselves, formed groups of two to three, and began identifying possible project domains. Weeks 4–6 shifted toward generative AI, design cognition, and AI-supported persona creation. Students discussed the metacognitive demands of generative AI using Tankelevitch et al. [51] and practiced constructing more complex prompts using the Six Thinking Hats technique [13]. They then studied stakeholder persona construction, informed by Goel et al.’s [21] work on human-AI collaboration in persona creation, and participated in a guest lecture and hands-on persona-building exercise. By the end of this phase, groups had finalized their project ideas. Weeks 7–8 moved from project definition to stakeholder-engagement planning. Students examined an example of an HCI intervention and empirical evaluation [26], and then used Friedman and Hendry’s Envisioning Cards [18] to consider stakeholders, values, and possible downstream consequences of their proposed systems. During this period, groups planned their human and AIS interviews, finalized their stakeholder roles, and were assigned to the two interview sequences. Weeks 8–12 combined the interview and design work with broader ethical reflection. Class topics included dark patterns, values work and influence within organizations, and unintended consequences of technology. For example, students discussed Bongard-Blanchy et al.’s work on users’ experiences of dark patterns [7], Wong’s analysis [53] of how UX professionals engage in values work without formal authority, and Veinot et al.’s account of how well-intentioned informatics interventions can exacerbate inequality [52]. Alongside these discussions, groups worked on successive project milestones, received in-class feedback, synthesized their stakeholder interviews, developed their design interventions, and presented their final projects in the final week During the joint preparation phase, all eight groups constructed an initial AIS, evaluated and peer-reviewed it, and de-veloped interview protocols for both the AIS and human stakeholder. Every team had therefore already encountered and worked with AIS before the sequence assignment. After this shared preparation, groups were randomly assigned to one of two formal interview orders: four teams interviewed their AIS and then a human stakeholder, while four interviewed a human stakeholder and then their AIS. We refer to these conditions as AIS-interview-first and human-interview-first, respectively. Groups were permitted, but not required, to adapt their protocols or AIS between interviews. 3.2 Participants and Ethics 

Twenty-one students (8 groups) participated as part of their required coursework in a semester-long sociotechnical design course. Students provided written informed consent for participation and for the research use of their coursework materials. Data collection for research purposes was voluntary and did not affect course grades. Participants were 19 undergraduate and 2 graduate students enrolled in the course, working in 8 teams of two to three students. All students followed the same preparatory sequence before sequence assignment, including course readings on persona construction and evaluation, two short in-class AIS-creation exercises, a guest lecture, and the peer-review evaluation described in Section 4.1. These common activities provided a shared instructional context, but they do not establish equivalent prior experience, participant experience, or AIS quality across teams. A pre-course measure of prior experience with design methods, generative AI tools, personas, or stakeholder interviewing was not administered. After final grades were submitted, students were invited to participate in voluntary post-course qualitative sessions. These comprised one two-participant discussion and one individual interview, both facilitated by a researcher who had not been involved in course instruction. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at the authors’ university.

3.4.1 Pain point identification and classification. One of the authors, the primary coder, independently reviewed each group’s AIS and human stakeholder interview transcripts alongside their final design submissions, using the thematic AI as Anchor 11 analysis approach of Braun and Clarke [8]. The process began with a familiarization phase in which the coder read all final reports and design artifacts to develop initial observations about each group’s conceptual space before engaging with the transcripts, as the variety of topics and the different approaches were needed to receive enough context. The coder then identified the pain points raised in each interview and coded their perceived prominence. A pain point was considered prominent when it appeared repeatedly across responses or was expressed with clear intensity and treated as a central theme; it was considered mentioned when it appeared briefly or in passing without elaboration. This distinction reflects how salient each issue was within the interview itself. The source of each pain point, that is, AIS, human stakeholder, or both, was also recorded. The coding was then discussed with the other researchers, resulting in refinements to the primary coder’s scheme. 

Tracing pain points to design decisions. After identifying pain points in the interviews, the primary coder compared each pain point with the corresponding team’s reports and final design artifact. Each pain point was assigned to one of four categories that distinguished no identified design correspondence, contextual reference, researcher-inferred feature correspondence, and student-reported foundational influence. The third category records a correspondence identified by the researcher without establishing that the pain point caused the design component. The fourth category requires an explicit statement from the team that the pain point informed or drove the design decision.

4 AIS Creation Process 

Before examining patterns in how interview sequence related to design outcomes, we first describe the AIS creation process itself. 

4.1 Guidelines 

Students engaged with core readings on persona construction and evaluation, which were discussed in class to establish a shared vocabulary and quality standards [21, 43]. To situate the exercise in current practices, we hosted a guest lecture by the CEO of a startup that deploys a human resources AIS that supports managers in matching tasks to employees and in structuring team work. In addition, before starting the work on the course project, students tried two brief, informal in-class AIS-creation exercises to gain hands-on familiarity with the tools and vocabulary. These were exploratory rather than formally structured. 

Developing an AIS Evaluation Framework. We established a standardized set of AIS-evaluation criteria through structured class discussions informed by the literature [21, 43]. All student groups applied the same five dimensions: (1) credibility: believability and evidential grounding of user characteristics; (2) consistency: internal coherence across persona elements; (3) completeness: coverage of salient population attributes and identification of gaps, with sufficient detail for design decisions; (4) clarity: well-organized, comprehensible presentation; and (5) empathy/depth: 

motivations and constraints that foster user understanding. Applying the same evaluation dimensions gave teams a shared evaluative vocabulary. It did not establish equivalent AIS quality, prompting practices, prior experience, or participant experience across teams. Differences observed later in the course are therefore treated as exploratory patterns associated with the complete sequencing conditions as implemented. 

4.2 Initial creation process of the AIS 

Within this context, students proceeded to generate the AISs. Groups began by defining basic demographic and contextual parameters for their target users. The Teacher Management System group, for instance, started with specifications like “30-year-old female, mother of one, married for 5 years, 4 years of school experience, homeroom teacher for special education class, with additional responsibilities as science coordinator”. Several groups documented a struggle with their initial efforts of crafting prompts, with one group asking the LLM directly to give them advice on the prompt. Character consistency emerged as another problem. The Elderly Transportation group performed a simple test, asking their created AIS for its name. In response, the AIS answered with a new name that differed from the originally established one. 14 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer Once the students felt their AIS met the set criteria defined, the class entered a peer-review step, where groups evaluated other groups’ AISs, as follows. 

4.3 Peer Review Evaluation 

To evaluate the created AISs, students employed a peer-review interactive evaluation process using De Bono’s Six Thinking Hats methodology [13], with groups rotating through different evaluative perspectives and challenging each other’s creations. This peer review occurred during the joint preparation phase, before assignment to the two interview-order conditions. It provided a common review activity but did not ensure that the resulting AIS were equivalent in quality, specificity, refinement, or students’ experience of working with them. This structured peer review process revealed both the potential and limitations of AISs in ways that individual assessment might have missed. Students iteratively evaluated their AISs using multiple peer-review lenses, which surfaced recurring limitations of synthetic stakeholder representations. Fact-oriented review highlighted gaps in evidentiary grounding, prompting teams to distinguish assumption-based from evidence-based AIS characteristics and to supplement synthetic responses with quantitative data and real-world examples. Emotion-focused review revealed tensions between surface plausibility and emotional authenticity, leading students to adjust how AISs articulated motivations, frustrations, and values. Critical review proved particularly consequential, exposing patterns of overgeneralization and stereotyping; for example, teams were prompted to recognize diversity within stakeholder groups and to reconsider simplified or implausible narratives embedded in their AISs. Together, these evaluations foregrounded how synthetic stakeholder voices tend to abstract, generalize, or smooth over lived complexity, requiring deliberate interrogation to avoid misrepresenting stakeholder perspectives.

5.1 Differences in How AIS and Human Stakeholder Input Was Interpreted 

This section addresses the interpretation and evaluation components of RQ1 using individual reflections, the end-of-course class discussion, and post-course interviews. Because the post-course participants were all from AIS-interview-first teams, their accounts are used only to elaborate that condition. When students compared AIS interviews with interviews conducted with human stakeholders, they consistently described differences in how each source could be interpreted and used. These differences appeared across the qualitative corpus, including the written reflections, end-of-course class discussion, two-participant post-course discussion, and individual post-course interview, and concerned the level of specificity, the focus of the response, the expression of uncertainty and emotion, and the extent to which responses remained close to lived experience. AI as Anchor 15 

General framing. Students often described AIS as echoing common patterns while lacking the specificity, context, and nuance they encountered in human stakeholder interviews. Half of the groups referred to AIS responses as general, and sometimes stereotypical. As P16 from Group B3 put it in their reflection: “ It was very vanilla, not entirely accurate .” For two groups (B4, A2), this generality was closely tied to a lack of contextual background. Group B4, which examined gaming toxicity, noted that the AIS described toxic behavior in online games in broad terms, but did not distinguish between the different settings in which such behavior appears. The human stakeholder, by contrast, described these differences in a more situated way, explaining that non-competitive games were largely free of toxic behavior, while competitive environments could become overwhelming. The participant even referred to one specific game and said, “ Icouldn’t handle the toxicity there, they are especially toxic to newcomers .” Students also described AIS as producing the kinds of answers they expected in advance, rather than surfacing the unusual or revealing details they were hoping to uncover. Group B3 (P16) explained: “It was very stereotypical, giving us the kind of answer it assumed a typical person would say. But it didn’t behave like a real person overall. What interested us, though, were not the stereotypical things, but the edges, the differences, the pain points. ”Relatedly, some students suggested that AIS did not have enough background to provide contextually adequate responses. Referring to existing solutions in the CareTech domain, one group noted that the AIS appeared to respond from partial knowledge rather than from a situated understanding of the field. Taken together, these accounts suggest that AIS often provided a broad initial framing of the problem space, but one that students found difficult to prioritize or trust without the contextual grounding that emerged in human interviews. 

Focus and Depth. Three groups (B1, B2, B3) discussed AIS’s difficulty in aligning its focus with what mattered most. Two groups (B1, B2) explained that AIS sometimes emphasized peripheral issues while overlooking human stakeholders’ central concerns, or, as described by group B3, the AIS “decided” to focus on one aspect only. P16 from B3 described: “It took one aspect from the whole description of the cases and the problem, and focused only on that, without addressing anything else”. Continuing the stereotypical perspective mentioned earlier, here, Group B2 deepens their discussion on how AIS focuses entirely on specific aspects that are commonly discussed in the context of people with ADHD. In their reflection, P13 (B2) contrasted this with human interviews, where lived experience often made the central problem more concrete and actionable. “It saw the word ADHD and focused on how people with ADHD need help with concentration and maybe time management. It started talking about those things, and completely ignored the real struggles the [human] student described ”. For example, the stakeholder described great difficulties in communicating with the system and going through the process: “The process took me a year and a half and I had to chase after them, it’s hard to reach them and I didn’t know at all who I needed to talk to at first, and each time they referred me to someone else ”. While the AIS stated a preference: “I would also be happy if there was an option to do the process in a more smooth, flowing and fast way ”. 

Certainty. A smaller but important set of groups criticized AIS for sounding overly certain. Two groups (A1, A3) described the AIS as polished, structured, and confident, even when uncertainty would have been more appropriate. 16 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer During the end-of-course class discussion, Group A1 similarly described: “ The AIS always gave neat bullet-point answers, but real people spoke with hesitation and doubt .” In the food delivery project (A3), students felt that the AIS responded in an evasive, public-relations style that protected the organization rather than exposing the underlying privacy problem. P6 described it this way, as part of the personal reflection: “It was very political, giving answers in line of [describing AIS “internal perspective”]: ‘I don’t want to damage the company’s image, so I won’t say anything. In fact, I’ll say positive things instead’. But we were aiming to solve privacy problems in this company, and it responded: ‘Such problems do not exist, and everything is fine.’ ”By contrast, students described human stakeholders as more likely to hesitate, qualify their statements, or contradict themselves. These qualities were not treated as weaknesses. Rather, students often interpreted them as signs that the interview reflected lived experience and as markers of authenticity. 

Emotional intensity. Across all groups, the human interviews carried a stronger emotional impact, as stakeholders conveyed their pain points with evident emotional intensity. They employed explicitly emotional terms and, at times, repetitive language or fragmented speech, features that may signal stress. Stakeholders also openly shared their feelings. For instance, the CareTech participant (Group A1) emphasized that user feedback was crucial for her self-esteem and motivation to remain in the job; the participant with ADHD (Group B2) described the persistent emotional difficulty of interacting with the system; and the gamer (Group B4) reflected on his feelings during gameplay: “...ruin motivation. It’s a buzz-kill... makes me regret playing ”. The Educational communication System participant (P4, A2) noted during the two-participant post-course discussion: “The emotional aspect, which was missing in the AI-generated responses, became evident in the real interview, with the participant revealing frustration, exhaustion ”. For students, this emotional texture made human interviews harder to simplify, but also more memorable and more consequential for later design decisions. 

Solution-oriented. While primarily aiming to learn about pain points through the interviews, students noted that AISs did not merely recount pain points, but they reformulated them as problem–solution pairs. This sometimes resonated with students but in other cases misrepresented the raw experience. As noted by Group B2, the ADHD project, the AIS reframed frustration as a neat fix:“The AIS reframed the complaint into: ‘If the rights were explained clearly on the website, it would be much easier’.” Yet, human stakeholders expressed their confusion and exasperation more directly, which students found more relatable and easier to follow. The solution-oriented approach was also a possible source of delay, as in cases where the AIS’s suggested focus later proved to be misguided, as reflected by P6 (Group A3): “Unfortunately, in our case, it didn’t contribute to the solution at all and actually delayed us.” Taken together, these accounts suggest that AIS often helped students begin to frame a problem space, but did so in a smoother and more generalized way. Human interviews were narrower in scope, yet more grounded, more emotionally textured, and more likely to redirect students toward concerns they had not prioritized in advance.

5.3 Sequencing Patterns in Stakeholder Engagement 

This section primarily addresses RQ2 by comparing reported practices and interpretations across the four AIS-interview-first and four human-interview-first teams. The cross-condition comparison draws on the verified team-level course materials and end-of-course class discussion. The three post-course participants (all from AIS-interview-first teams) are used only to elaborate accounts within that condition. The team is the comparative unit, and the findings are exploratory patterns associated with the conditions as implemented. Students’ accounts revealed recurring differences in how AIS and human stakeholder interviews were interpreted and used, depending on the order in which they occurred. Students’ accounts differed across the conditions in their interview practices and in how they described the contribution of each source. Groups in the AIS-interview-first sequence (A1–A4) often found the AIS’s answers wrong or overly general, such as focusing on mental health difficulties that the human stakeholder did not confirm (group A1), or claiming there were no privacy problems in the company, which the stakeholder utterly contradicted (group A3). Group A2 summarized it as follows: “While the AIS captured broad themes, it lacked nuance and prioritization. The simulated responses tended to generalize issues rather than highlight the most pressing concerns, making it difficult to determine which pain points were most impactful. ”Still, the groups emphasized the preparatory value of the AIS: it showed whether their questions could elicit substantive answers, and sometimes introduced new perspectives. All AIS-interview-first groups described the AIS as a “good preliminary check” that helped them reshape questions. One student (group A4) explained that “it included several points we hadn’t thought of ourselves”. During the end-of-course class discussion, a student from B4 explained that using AIS creation in another course gave them a mental model of a store owner and “broke the fear” of the first interview. During the same class discussion, students from A2 and B3 described extending this use to job-interview preparation and simulations of workplace conversations. In these cases, AIS did not replace stakeholder engagement. Instead, it often served as an initial frame through which students tested questions, rehearsed interviews, and entered the human interview with a more concrete, if still provisional, sense of the domain. By contrast, groups who began with human interviews (B1–B4) described the AIS as primarily useful for validation. After speaking with human stakeholders, they turned to the AIS to check and repeat themes already observed. For instance, students in group B3 noted that the AIS could “validate themes that also appeared with the human”, while group B1 reflected that it helped them “think through possible problems in a systematic way”. In most cases, AISs seldom added novel information, functioning less as tools for discovery and more as predictable mechanisms for confirmation. A notable exception was B1, who reported that by deliberately contrasting stakeholder demographics in the AIS creation, they were able to identify an additional pain point that the human stakeholder had not raised. These observations highlight that the perceived role of AISs in the human-interview-first sequence was generally confirmatory, with occasional exceptions, and should be interpreted as impressionistic patterns rather than quantitative results. In these AI as Anchor 19 accounts, AIS rarely functioned as the primary source of discovery. More often, it was used after the human interview as a secondary source that helped students revisit themes, check whether concerns recurred, or think through possible extensions of what they had already heard. 

5.3.1 Patterns in How Students Interpreted and Used Stakeholder Input. The comparison identified recurring contrasts in how students described processing AIS and human-stakeholder input. 

Effort and filtering. Students in the AIS-interview-first sequence described the AIS interview as requiring substantial effort to make the responses usable. They reported having to narrow prompts, request specificity, and actively filter broad or verbose answers. As one student explained, they “had to constantly narrow and feed the AI specifics to get anything precise” (P2, A1). AIS responses were often experienced as covering a wide range of possible issues, but at a level of generality that required additional work to identify what was relevant. When these students later turned to human interviews, they described a different kind of effort. Human accounts were “less structured” and sometimes “messy” (A1–A4), yet they were also more emotionally engaging and more clearly organized around a small number of pressing concerns. Rather than filtering excess information, students described the effort as focusing on interpreting emotion, contradiction, and emphasis, and deciding how to carry these forward into their designs. Students in the human-interview-first sequence described a different progression. Groups that began with a human stakeholder described confronting contradiction, emotion, and contextual detail from the outset. Although challenging, this initial encounter helped them identify what mattered most in the problem space. When they later turned to AIS, they approached it more strategically, often using it to revisit or expand on concerns that had already become salient. In this sequence, Group B3 described the AIS as “an anchor” used for validation or comparison rather than as an initial starting point. Across both sequences, students described AIS as a reference point, but the role of that reference point differed with timing. In AIS-interview-first groups it often set an initial frame that was later revised through human input. In human-interview-first groups it more often supported comparison and validation of insights that were already grounded in stakeholder experience. 

Trust and credibility. Students often described AIS responses as requiring confirmation before they could be taken seriously. Several groups explicitly noted that they did not fully trust AI responses “until they’re corroborated by a real interview” (A2, A3). By contrast, human interviews were treated as immediately credible, even when they were incomplete, hesitant, or internally inconsistent. Students also reported that inconsistencies in AIS responses were harder to detect. Group A1, for example, described subtle contradictions in the AIS replies that were not immediately visible but became apparent only after closer inspection. This further reinforced a cautious stance toward AIS, where responses were treated as provisional unless they aligned with stakeholder accounts. These accounts also differed in how students reported using each source in their design process. Insights from human stakeholders were more readily carried forward into design decisions, while AIS responses were more often revisited, checked, or reformulated. Some human-interview-first teams reported adjusting subsequent AIS prompts or interview strategies to reflect concerns raised by the human stakeholder. Because refinement was discretionary, this is treated as a reported practice rather than a fixed feature of the condition. 

Challenging assumptions. Human interviews frequently surfaced issues that students had not prioritized in advance, including contradictions and non-obvious concerns that unsettled their assumptions. As several groups noted, 20 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer these interviews revealed “non-obvious and sometimes contradictory insights” that forced them to reconsider their initial framing. AIS, by contrast, was often described as returning expected or socially recognizable answers. Students across multiple groups noted that the AIS tended to “tell us what we wanted to hear” (A1–A4, B2–B4), reinforcing rather than challenging their existing assumptions. In this sense, AIS more often supported an initial frame, whereas human interviews were more likely to redirect it by introducing unexpected priorities or tensions. 

Emotional texture. Students repeatedly described human interviews as carrying what one group called “messy truths and emotion” (A3). Human stakeholders expressed frustration, exhaustion, hesitation, and repetition, and these qualities made the interviews harder to simplify into neat summaries. AIS responses, by contrast, were often described as “polite, non-committal” (A2, A3, B3), and as producing accounts that felt “not grounded in reality” (A1–A4, B2, B3, B4). This difference was reflected in how specific concerns were articulated. In the Medical Training project (A4), the AIS discussed general ethical considerations around medical data. The human stakeholder, however, repeatedly emphasized a specific concern about hallucinations: “This is the most important thing - if the system won’t be reliable and help me with correct material, giving correct answers to my questions, why do I need it at all? ”Students described this kind of expression as harder to ignore and more difficult to reduce to a general category. While AIS responses were often experienced as composed and abstract, human interviews were experienced as more situated and consequential for how students framed the problem. An exception was noted in Group B1, where the AIS expressed emotions such as fear, in contrast to a more authoritative human stakeholder. This suggests that while emotional expression can appear in AIS responses, students generally treated it differently from emotion grounded in lived experience. 

Cumulative sequence patterns. Over time, these differences accumulated into distinct patterns of use. In both sequences, students described AIS as an “anchor”, but they used this term differently. AIS-interview-first groups (A1–A4) generally treated AIS as an initial reference point that shaped their early understanding of the stakeholder domain. In these cases, AIS often provided a broad starting point that students later revised, narrowed, or corrected through human interviews. Human-interview-first groups (B1–B4), by contrast, approached AIS after an initial grounding in lived experience. In this sequence, AIS was more often used as a secondary anchor, supporting validation, comparison, or expansion of insights that had already been established. Students described using AIS to revisit themes, check whether concerns recurred, or explore variations on stakeholder-derived issues. Across both conditions, students described the AIS interview as a reference point, but its reported function varied with formal interview order. When the AIS interview preceded the human-stakeholder interview, it often served as a provisional frame that was later reconsidered. 6 Discussion 

This study examined how students used AIS alongside required human-stakeholder engagement, focusing on patterns associated with the order of the two formal interviews. All teams had constructed and evaluated an AIS before sequence assignment. In the AIS-interview-first condition, students often described the AIS interview as providing a provisional frame that was later reconsidered through the human-stakeholder interview. In the human-interview-first condition, students more often described the AIS interview as supporting comparison or validation of stakeholder-derived concerns. These findings concern the complete sequencing conditions as implemented, including shared pre-assignment AIS preparation and discretionary opportunities for adaptation. They do not isolate a causal effect of interview order. 

6.1 Reframing AI in Participatory Design: From Replacement to Augmentation 

Our findings speak directly to Delgado et al.’s identification of “algorithmic proxies” as an emerging trend in participatory AI design [14]. Prior work has raised concerns that such proxies may produce “stale modeling” and function as a technology of de-politicization when they substitute for stakeholder participation. In contrast, our study examines AIS in settings where they are used alongside, rather than in place of, authentic stakeholder engagement. Across groups, students used AIS alongside human-stakeholder interviews because both were required by the course. Every team had constructed, evaluated and peer-reviewed their AIS before the assignment. In the AIS-interview-first condition, the AIS interview often served as a provisional frame. In the human-interview-first condition, it more often served as a source of comparison or validation. This comparison concerns the order of the formal interviews and any discretionary adaptation between them. At the same time, our findings are consistent with broader concerns about AI mediation. Students frequently described AIS responses as smooth, generalized, and solution-oriented, in contrast to the uncertainty, contradiction, and emotional texture of human interviews. Similar observations have been reported in prior work. Kapania et al. [31] describe LLM outputs as lacking “palpability” and contextual depth, while Agnew et al. [1] characterize such systems as producing an “illusion of artificial inclusion.” Qian et al. [41] refer to this phenomenon as “fabricated reality,” where representations are technically functional but lack grounding in lived experience. In our study, this appeared in the form of responses that were coherent and usable, but often required additional interpretation or validation before they could be integrated into design decisions. These differences were also reflected in how students treated information from each source. AIS responses were often treated as provisional and subject to confirmation, while human interviews were more readily carried forward into design decisions. Rather than indicating a fixed hierarchy between sources, these patterns suggest that different types of input were treated as serving different roles within the design process. Platform variation offers an alternative explanation for the Teacher System (A2) and Medical Training (A4) exceptions: groups that used non-default LLMs or configurations may have produced AIS of different specificity or coherence, independent of sequence. We did not systematically log which LLM(s) each group used for their final AIS, so this explanation cannot be ruled out and is a priority for future replication with standardized platforms. Our findings also point to broader implications for participatory design practice. In our study, students worked with relatively accessible stakeholders, including peers and volunteers. In professional contexts, however, constraints of time, access, and resources may shape how designers choose between authentic engagement and AI-mediated representations. This raises a potential concern: AIS may be used more frequently for stakeholders who are harder to reach, including 22 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer marginalized or underrepresented groups. Prior work has warned that such substitutions risk reproducing epistemic injustice by limiting whose perspectives are directly represented in design processes [17, 24, 36]. While our study does not examine such deployments directly, the patterns we observed suggest the importance of considering how AIS is used in relation to stakeholder access. If AIS is used primarily where authentic engagement is difficult, it may inadvertently shift influence away from those stakeholders. Future work should examine how AIS is deployed in practice and how its use can be structured to support, rather than displace, engagement with underrepresented voices. 

6.2 How Students Described AIS and Empathic Engagement 

Traditional persona research positions empathy as a central mechanism in design, developed through engaging with stakeholder perspectives and synthesizing user data [22, 38]. Our findings point to a tension in how AIS contributes to this process. Students who interviewed AIS first often described using the AIS to test and reshape questions and to organize potential pain points before meeting human stakeholders. At the same time, they described AIS interactions as “not grounded in reality” and lacking the “messy truths and emotion” that characterized human interviews. This resonates with Kaate et al.’s observation of a “fourth wall” effect in chat-based personas, where users experience the interaction as a representation rather than as a person [29]. One possible explanation for this tension relates to how AIS was constructed and used. In traditional persona creation, designers build empathy through the process of synthesizing user data, actively constructing and refining mental models of stakeholders [22, 38]. In our study, students generated AIS through prompting, engaging primarily with the outputs rather than the full process of construction. This shift may help explain why AIS supported structured exploration of stakeholder issues while being experienced as less connected to lived experience. We do not claim that AIS reduces empathy, but that it appears to support a different mode of engagement, one that emphasizes coverage and organization over immersion in specific contexts. The selection dynamics described by Şengün et al. [48] provide a useful lens for interpreting how students worked with multiple sources. While their work focuses on how designers choose among personas, students in our study made similar judgments across sources: which pain points to prioritize, which perspectives to trust, and which insights to carry forward. Table 5 shows that a larger proportion of prominent human-stakeholder-derived pain points had a researcher-identified correspondence in the final designs than did prominent AIS-derived pain points. This difference concerns the frequency of coded correspondence. It does not establish the relative decision weight assigned to each source, because sources produced different numbers of concerns and the count treats an interface adjustment and a conceptual redesign as one unit each. Students’ accounts also highlight differences in how emotional content was encountered and used. Human interviews were described as involving contradiction, hesitation, and strong emotional expression, which students had to interpret and integrate. AIS responses, even when they included affective language, were more often described as composed and generalized. Prior work has similarly noted that AI-generated personas may lack the experiential grounding needed to convey context and emotional nuance [29, 34]. In our study, these differences were reflected in how students described the two sources rather than in direct measures of empathic engagement. These patterns connect to broader concerns in human–AI interaction. Qian et al. [41] describe how AI systems that “curate human experiences” may improve efficiency while altering users’ relationship to those experiences. In a similar way, AIS in our study appeared to support structured preparation and exploration, while human interviews introduced complexity, contradiction, and emotional weight. Rather than treating AIS as a substitute for stakeholder perspectives, AI as Anchor 23 these findings suggest that it may be more productive to consider how different forms of representation contribute differently to design reasoning. Finally, some students described the contrast between AIS and human stakeholders as clarifying rather than dimin-ishing the role of empathy. The relative simplicity and coherence of AIS responses made the complexity of human accounts more salient. 

6.3 Cognitive and Pedagogical Implications of Sequential Engagement 

Students’ reflections and interview practices revealed differences in the kinds of effort and interpretation required when engaging with AIS versus human stakeholders. In particular, students described AIS interactions as requiring additional effort to extract usable insights, noting they “had to constantly narrow and feed the AI specifics to get anything precise.” Prior work has similarly observed that AI-generated personas may produce verbose or overly polished responses that require users to filter and interpret output carefully [29]. In students’ accounts, this process involved narrowing questions, filtering AIS output, and systematically exploring potential issues before the human interview, but was also experienced as less connected to the situated and emotional aspects of stakeholder experience. Trust also differed in students’ accounts of the two sources. Several groups described not trusting AI responses “until they’re corroborated by a real interview”, while human interviews were described as immediately credible. These differences were reflected in how insights were carried forward into design decisions. Pooled across the eight groups, a larger proportion of prominent human-stakeholder-derived pain points had identified final-design correspondences than did prominent AIS-derived pain points (39/43, 90.7%, versus 22/32, 68.8%). At the team level, the human-derived rate was higher in four teams, equal in three, and lower in one team (A3). Because 12 prominent pain points were raised by both sources and therefore appear in both source totals, these source-specific descriptive rates overlap. They should not be interpreted as independent comparisons or as evidence of source superiority, decision weight, or causal influence. Rather than indicating a fixed hierarchy, these patterns suggest that students treated different sources as serving different roles, with human input more often grounding final decisions. This is consistent with prior work showing that limitations in AI-generated personas can affect perceived credibility and require additional validation [30], and with the emphasis in participatory design on engagement with authentic stakeholder voices [47]. These findings also have implications for design education. Beyond teaching students how to create and interview stakeholders, there is a need to make explicit how decisions are made about which voices are prioritized in the design process. Şengün et al. [48] show that persona selection is influenced by factors such as identification and perceived relevance, which can lead designers to privilege familiar perspectives. In our setting, similar dynamics appeared in how students chose which pain points to carry forward into their designs. Instructional approaches could therefore make these choices more explicit, for example by requiring students to document selection rationales, justify which perspectives are included or excluded, and reflect on how different sources influenced their final designs. Such practices align with design justice principles [12] by foregrounding the question of whose experiences are treated as design-relevant. Finally, the patterns observed here suggest that sequencing may play a role in how students develop strategies for working with AI-generated input. Students who encountered AIS early often described learning how to manage its outputs, treating them as provisional reference points while awaiting human validation. This resonates with Anthis et al.’s characterization of LLM simulations as “silicon samples” that can support early exploration but require critical evaluation [3]. Our findings extend this perspective by showing how such strategies emerge through practice when students engage with both AIS and human stakeholders over time. 24 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer 

6.4 Methodological Contributions and Theoretical Implications 

Methodologically, this study contributes by examining AIS within an ongoing design process rather than through isolated or one-time evaluations. Prior work has often compared AI- and human-generated personas in more bounded settings [21, 50], whereas our naturalistic approach makes it possible to examine how AIS is taken up over time alongside authentic stakeholder engagement. In particular, the study highlights sequencing as an important dimension of hybrid human–AI design workflows. By following how students engaged with AIS and human stakeholders across a semester-long project, we were able to trace how different sources of input were interpreted, revised, and incorporated into design decisions. Theoretically, our findings suggest that AIS is not well described only as an “algorithmic proxy” [14]. In our study, AIS more often functioned as a reference point within the design process: sometimes as an initial frame, and sometimes as a secondary source used for validation, comparison, or extension. This shifts the emphasis from whether AIS stands in for a stakeholder to how its position within the implemented workflow is associated with students’ interpretation of other stakeholder input over time. This perspective also speaks to broader concerns in participatory design and design justice. Costanza-Chock argues that tools designed around efficiency can reproduce extractive dynamics when they displace meaningful engagement with affected communities [12]. Our findings do not show such displacement directly, since students in this study engaged with both AIS and human stakeholders. However, they do suggest that AIS and human input were treated differently: AIS responses were more often experienced as coherent and generalized, while human interviews were treated as more grounded and more consequential for final design decisions. These patterns underscore the importance of examining not only whether AI-generated stakeholder representations are used, but how they are positioned relative to authentic voices in the design process. 

6.5 Design Implications for Conversational AIS 

Our findings suggest several directions for the design of conversational AIS as tools for participatory design. The recommendations below vary in evidentiary grounding: some follow directly from patterns we observed (e.g., students’ need to validate AIS before trusting it), while others are speculative directions motivated by, but not directly tested in, our data (e.g., productive-friction features). 

Clarifying function and boundaries. Prior work on algorithmic proxies has raised epistemological concerns about con-flating synthetic outputs with lived experience [29, 31]. In our study, students often treated AIS responses as provisional and sought confirmation through human interviews. This suggests a need for AIS to make their representational limits more visible. Rather than presenting as authentic stakeholder voices, conversational AIS may benefit from explicitly communicating their role as exploratory or provisional representations. This could take the form of system-level transparency features such as: (a) uncertainty markers (e.g., hedging language, confidence indicators, or statements such as “this is a generalization, not based on lived experience”); (b) scope disclaimers (e.g., notes that the AIS reflects patterns from available data rather than a specific individual or community); and (c) indicators when responses rely on common or stereotypical patterns. Such features may help designers interpret AIS outputs as starting points that require validation through stakeholder engagement. 

Designing for productive friction. Unlike static personas, which require designers to engage in synthesis and interpre-tation [22, 33], conversational AIS can present coherent and readily usable narratives. In our study, this sometimes reduced the need to work through ambiguity. One possible design direction is to introduce forms of “productive friction,” AI as Anchor 25 such as hesitation, partial answers, or conflicting perspectives. These features would not aim to simulate authenticity, but to make visible the limits of the representation and encourage further questioning. This aligns with Anthis et al.’s argument that simulations are most useful when they surface possibilities rather than provide definitive answers [3]. 

Adaptive support across use. The differences between AIS-interview-first and human-interview-first sequences suggest that AIS may play different roles over time. Early interactions could support exploration, for example by helping designers generate questions or map a broad set of potential concerns. Later interactions could foreground limitations by surfacing uncertainty, gaps, or inconsistencies. Such a shift may help direct designers back toward authentic stakeholder engagement rather than allowing AIS to stand in for it. This perspective resonates with Kaate et al.’s observation [29] that different persona formats involve different trade-offs in use. 

Integration into participatory workflows. Finally, AIS design may benefit from extending beyond one-on-one interaction to support integration with broader participatory processes. For example, systems could support comparison between AIS responses and stakeholder data, or help teams track where synthetic and human inputs converge or diverge. Such features could position AIS more clearly as part of a larger design workflow, supporting augmentation rather than substitution. 

Summary. Taken together, these implications highlight a core design challenge: conversational AIS need not aim to replicate the authenticity of lived voices, but rather to make their synthetic character visible and usable. Designing AIS that balances transparency, friction, and evolving support may help them complement, rather than compete with, stakeholder participation. 

6.6 Implications for Education 

Building on Goel et al.’s call to integrate AI persona creation into curricula to prepare future UX designers [21], we consider what our findings suggest for design education. Students in our study encountered both the affordances and limitations of AI Simulated Stakeholders (AIS), navigating the tension between polished but generalized synthetic responses and the more complex, sometimes contradictory accounts provided by human stakeholders. These experiences point to several pedagogical opportunities. First, AIS can serve as low-stakes practice environments. Interviewing an AIS allowed students to rehearse questioning techniques, refine interview protocols, and reduce the anxiety associated with initial stakeholder engagement. In this sense, AIS functioned less as stand-ins for authentic stakeholders and more as tools for developing confidence and procedural fluency. Second, the contrast between AIS responses and human accounts was repeatedly described by students as informative. The generality and solution-oriented nature of AIS responses made the specificity and emotional expression of human interviews more visible. Learning activities that intentionally juxtapose synthetic and authentic perspectives may therefore help students attend more closely to nuance, context, and variation in stakeholder accounts. Third, our findings highlight the importance of integrating structured reflection into the use of AIS. Without explicit prompts to question the limits of AIS representations, some students reported a tendency to treat responses as sufficiently informative or to rely on their convenience. Educational approaches could therefore include activities that require students to reflect on issues such as bias, representational limits, and the distinction between augmentation and substitution. Taken together, these observations suggest that AIS can function as provisional reference points in the learning process, shaping how students prepare for and reflect on engagement with human stakeholders. Integrating AIS into 26 Osnat Mokryn, Oshrat Ayalon, Noga Dines, and Orit Shaer design curricula may support the development of interviewing skills, analytic practices, and critical reflection, while reinforcing the role of authentic stakeholder voices in participatory design. 

6.7 Limitations and Future Directions 

This study was designed as an ecologically grounded investigation of how students incorporated AI Simulated Stake-holders (AIS) into authentic project work. Its ecological validity is important because actual uses of generative AI involve variation in project domains, available systems, prompting strategies, and opportunities for revision. Allowing teams to make these choices provided insight into how AIS may be appropriated in practice. At the same time, this naturalistic design places limitations on the interpretation of the findings. First, the study involved 21 novice designers, 19 undergraduate and two graduate students, in a single academic course. The findings are therefore most directly applicable to similar educational settings, and their transferability to professional or other educational contexts requires further examination. While all students completed the same preparatory activities, we did not administer a pre-course measure of their prior experience with design methods, generative AI, personas, or stakeholder interviews. We therefore cannot determine how differences in prior experience may have influenced their construction, evaluation, or use of AIS. Second, the study followed eight teams during one course project. It does not establish whether the practices students developed persisted over time or transferred to later design work. The number of participants and teams was also appropriate for examining qualitative patterns but not for making statistical population-level claims. Third, sequence was intertwined with opportunities for AIS refinement. Human-interview-first teams could incorporate insights from human stakeholders when refining their AIS. This reflects how sequencing may operate in practice and is therefore substantively relevant. However, it also means that the comparison captures both interview order and the information available during AIS development. Similarly, the common AIS-evaluation framework described in Section 4.1 provided teams with a shared vocabulary but did not ensure equivalent AIS quality, prompting practices, participant experience, or prior expertise. The observed differences should therefore be interpreted as patterns associated with the complete sequencing conditions rather than effects of interview order alone. All teams had constructed, evaluated, and peer-reviewed AIS before sequence assignment. The study therefore does not compare initial exposure to AIS with introduction after human engagement. It compares the order of the two formal interviews after shared AIS preparation. That shared preparation may already have influenced how teams understood their stakeholders in both conditions. Fourth, teams adapted AIS to their projects rather than following a fully standardized implementation. Most used ChatGPT, generally through its free web interface, while some used or tested other large language models. Prompt content, background information, interaction histories, and revision practices also varied. This variation strengthens the study’s account of how students appropriate AIS under realistic conditions, but it limits our ability to attribute particular findings to a specific model or interaction feature. In several teams, the AIS and human interviewees occupied related but not identical roles or experience profiles. Observed source differences may therefore partly reflect differences in stakeholder role, specificity, or perspective rather than source type alone. In addition, prominence was inferred from repetition or expressed intensity. Source-specific communication styles, such as repetitive AIS output or emotionally emphatic human accounts, may therefore have affected which concerns were classified as prominent. Fifth, teams selected different project domains and stakeholder groups (Table 1). This diversity allowed the study to examine AIS across varied design situations, but differences in emotional sensitivity, stakeholder expertise, technical complexity, and stakeholder access may also have shaped teams’ experiences. These domain characteristics cannot be separated fully from the sequencing conditions in the present design. Sixth, the qualitative analysis has limitations. Coding was conducted with knowledge of the completed project materials, creating AI as Anchor 27 some possibility of hindsight bias. Nevertheless, the analysis identified multiple pain points that did not subsequently appear in teams’ reports or designs, suggesting that the coding was not reconstructed solely from final outcomes. The post-course evidence was also selective. Only three of the 21 students participated approximately four months after the course, and all three represented AIS-interview-first teams. Their accounts may be affected by selective participation and delayed recall and cannot support cross-condition conclusions. Findings based on the broader course-embedded corpus are therefore distinguished from observations supported only by the post-course discussion or individual interview. Finally, the analysis focused on how students described their cognitive work and relational considerations when engaging with AIS and human stakeholders. It did not independently assess downstream design quality, implementation success, or stakeholder satisfaction. The findings therefore illuminate students’ processes and interpretations but do not establish the comparative effectiveness of the resulting designs. These limitations point to opportunities for complementary research. Longitudinal and multi-site studies could examine whether AIS-related practices persist beyond a single project and extend to professional or community-based settings. More controlled studies could separately examine interview order, AIS refinement, prior experience, model choice, and project domain. Such studies would complement the ecological perspective developed here. Future evaluations should also examine design quality and stakeholder experience directly, particularly in work involving underrepresented or difficult-to-reach communities, where substitution or misrepresentation may have especially serious consequences. 

7 Conclusions 

This paper examined how students combined AIS and human-stakeholder input when both forms of engagement were required. All teams constructed and evaluated AIS before assignment and then conducted the two formal interviews in different orders. Students in the AIS-interview-first condition often described the AIS interview as a provisional frame that was later reconsidered through human engagement. Students in the human-interview-first condition more often described the AIS interview as supporting comparison or validation. These are exploratory patterns associated with the complete conditions as implemented, not isolated effects of interview order. By situating AIS within related approaches such as algorithmic proxies, personas, and LLM-based simulations, this paper contributes both conceptual clarification and empirical observations. The findings suggest that AIS can support preparation and procedural skill development, while also tending to omit the hesitation, contradiction, and affect that characterized human stakeholder accounts. These differences help explain how students positioned AIS and human inputs within their design process. The study does not establish whether designers would retain human participation if it were optional, particularly under constraints of time, resources, or stakeholder access. It shows instead how students combined AIS and human stakeholder input when both were required. Within that setting, AIS supported activities such as rehearsal, provisional framing, comparison, and validation, while human interviews provided situated and emotionally textured accounts. These findings motivate treating AIS as provisional complements within educational workflows. Whether AIS supplements or displaces human participation in unconstrained practice remains an open empirical question.

B Case Study: Elderly Transportation Technology Group (B1) 

To illustrate our analytic process and the kinds of patterns it revealed, we present one project in detail: Elderly Transportation Technology . Group B1 focused on redesigning a public transportation application for elderly users, drawing on both a human stakeholder interview and an AIS interview. One component - audio alerts for route changes - had an identified correspondence only with a AI as Anchor 33 concern raised in the AIS interview. C Protocol for the post-course discussion and individual interview Opening and Consent 

Hello, and thank you for coming. I am [anonymized], a faculty member in the department. In this discussion, we will talk about the teaching processes that incorporate GenAI in the systems design course. During this conversation, there are no right or wrong answers. Our aim is to help us refine the insights that emerged in the discussion held during the course. We will not discuss personal or sensitive topics. Even so, if at any point you feel uncomfortable, simply tell us—we can skip any of the questions, or end the conversation altogether. Everything we talk about today is confidential, and your names will remain confidential. May I have your permission to record this interview? The recording will be stored confidentially and used for research purposes only. Before we begin, do you have any questions? When you answer during the conversation, please state your name. (3) Did the process of creating the persona contribute to your understanding of the project? In what way? (4) As part of the creation process, you wrote several prompts before arriving at the final one. How did you decide to stop? How did you decide it was good enough? 

Part 3: Interviewing the Persona 

Now let us look more closely at the part in which you interviewed the persona. (5) Did the interview itself contribute to you? In what way? (6) What was the advantage of the persona, from your point of view? (7) Was there a case in which you disagreed with the persona? What did you do? (10) Did you change your design for the system following the conversation with the persona? (11) Did you feel that your own ability to create a persona affected how much you trusted it? 

Part 4: Back to the Creation Process 

(12) Returning to the process of creating the persona: did you use questions from the interview you had planned in order to assess whether the persona was good enough? 

Part 5: Closing 

(13) In the end, would you say this contributes to the design process? If so, how would you recommend integrating it into the design process—should the interview with the persona come before or after the interview with a human stakeholder?
