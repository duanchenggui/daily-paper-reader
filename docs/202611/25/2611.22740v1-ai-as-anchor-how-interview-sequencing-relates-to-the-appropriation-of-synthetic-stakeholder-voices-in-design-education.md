## 摘要
设计师越来越多地使用大语言模型来模拟利益相关者对话。然而，当AI模拟利益相关者（AIS）与人类利益相关者参与一起使用时，其如何被采用仍不清楚。我们报告了一项在单机构、单学期设计课程中开展的实地研究，其中8个团队（N=21）构建、评估并同行评审了一个初始AIS。随后，这些团队以两种顺序分别访谈AIS和人类利益相关者：AIS优先或人类优先。基于访谈转录、设计制品、课程嵌入式反思以及课后定性讨论环节，我们考察了与这些顺序条件相关的探索性模式。我们发现，学生并未将AIS描述为对过程的中性补充。相反，AIS与人类利益相关者被正式访谈的顺序，与解读和使用这两类来源信息的不同方式相关。当先进行AIS访谈时，它提供了一个宽泛但往往笼统的框架，学生随后通过人类访谈重新审视该框架。当AIS访谈发生在人类参与之后时，AIS更常用于重新审视和验证已扎根于亲身经验的洞见。学生将AIS描述为“锚点”，但其报告的作用随其在正式访谈顺序中的位置而变化。在最终设计中，相较于源自AIS的关切，更高比例的源自人类利益相关者的突出关切具有对应的设计元素。我们的贡献包括：将AIS刻画为一个参照点，其报告的作用随其在正式访谈顺序中的位置而变化；提供探索性实证证据，表明所实施的访谈顺序条件与解读和使用利益相关者输入的不同模式相关；以及对如何将AIS整合到设计工作流程中、同时保留与人类利益相关者的直接互动提出启示。

## Abstract
Designers increasingly use large language models to simulate stakeholder conversations. Yet it remains unclear how AI-Simulated Stakeholders (AIS) are taken up when used alongside human stakeholder engagement. We report a field study in a single-institution, single-semester design course in which 8 teams (N=21) constructed, evaluated, and peer-reviewed an initial AIS. Then, the teams interviewed both AIS and human stakeholders in two sequences: AIS-first or human-first. Drawing on interview transcripts, design artifacts, course-embedded reflections, and post-course qualitative sessions, we examine exploratory patterns associated with these sequencing conditions. We find that students did not describe AIS as a neutral addition to the process. Instead, the order in which AIS and human stakeholders were formally interviewed was associated with different ways of interpreting and using information from the two sources. When the AIS interview came first, it provided a broad but often general frame that students later reconsidered through human interviews. When the AIS interview followed human engagement, AIS was more often used to revisit and validate insights already grounded in lived experience. Students described AIS as an "anchor," but its reported role varied with its position in the formal interview sequence. In the final designs, a larger proportion of prominent human-stakeholder-derived concerns than AIS-derived concerns had corresponding design elements. We contribute: characterization of AIS as a reference point whose reported role varies with its position in the formal interview sequence; exploratory empirical evidence that the implemented interview-order conditions were associated with distinct patterns of interpreting and using stakeholder input; and implications for integrating AIS into design workflows in ways that preserve direct engagement with human stakeholders
