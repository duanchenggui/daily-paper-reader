## Abstract 

Children using Scratch to learn programming can struggle with text-heavy, lengthy explanations and hints during debugging. Ver-bal interactions based on screen sharing or camera data may be an interesting alternative for supporting learners. We therefore explore the quality, use, and perceptions of fifth- and sixth-graders’ verbal interactions with a GenAI tutor. The goal is to investigate the quality and use of the multimodal GenAI tutor in the given context. First of all, a pilot study of eight tasks conducted without students helped inform the process of the tutor’s classroom evalua-tion. The classroom study was realized after fifth- and sixth-graders had participated in six Scratch classroom lessons. Then 23 students were introduced to the tutor, worked on the eight tasks with the GenAI tutor, and completed a questionnaire. The analysis of the pilot and classroom study shows that the tutor’s responses were intelligible and actionable, and no single turn revealed a complete solution. However, the tutor missed existing errors in 61.5% of 603 error-related classroom turns, compared to 15.7% in the pilot study. Children’s voice prompts were brief and mostly responded to the tutor. Although a video had explained that responses could be incor-rect, 39.1% of the children trusted the tutor’s answers to always be correct. These findings have implications for educators and learners using multimodal GenAI tutors, as they need to be aware that an intelligible and human-like voice does not ensure correct support. For developers, our results suggest that screen sharing alone does not ensure responses that match the program state.

## 1 Introduction 

In Scratch, children can learn to program by assembling visual blocks and creating animations, games, and stories [35]. When a program behaves unexpectedly, learners need to connect that behavior to the block(s) that produced it. In the classroom, providing individual debugging support is difficult when many learners need help at the same time. Teachers in lower secondary school describe this situation as the “hands-up problem” [11]. GenAI can offer on-demand assistance for programming [33], but research in higher education also identifies risks involving incorrect information and over-reliance [2, 32, 33]. At the same time, scoping reviews of GenAI in computing education report a research gap at the secondary level [1, 31, 46], and an overly positive narrative [23]. Research on young Scratch learners (aged 6-12) has explored hints [15] and GenAI-based guidance [5, 9]. However, text-based conversations with GenAI require learners to read explanations and type questions, while working with task instructions and block labels. This sometimes leads to children not reading hints precisely or not at all [16]. These findings lead us to consider alternatives to text-heavy assistance. Verbal interactions may be an alternative to reduce literacy barriers [5] (i.e., reduce reading and writing demands so pre- and early readers can succeed [7]). Multimodal generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) models can combine verbal and visual context from screen sharing or cameras and respond with text or audio with low latency [14]. These models could support learning across subjects and digital or physical settings without requiring integration into a specific learning application. Our goal is to investigate the quality and use of a GenAI tutor with screen sharing during Scratch lessons in grades 5 and 6. We use the term GenAI tutor to refer to Gemini 2.5 Flash Native Audio Preview in Google AI Studio, instructed through a system prompt to support children with Scratch tasks without revealing the solution. Children interact with the tutor by voice while sharing their Scratch browser tab. Our exploratory study combines an expert pilot with an analysis of classroom recordings and questionnaire responses from 23 children. We address the following research questions: RQ1 How can the GenAI tutor’s verbal responses be characterized while supporting a child with a Scratch task? 

RQ2 How do fifth- and sixth-graders interact with the GenAI tutor while working on Scratch tasks? 

RQ3 How do children perceive the GenAI tutor as a dialogue partner in terms of instructional support, trust in its responses, and verbal communication? 

The present study contributes (1) an evaluation scheme adapted to verbal interactions with screen-sharing data as context, (2) a classroom analysis of tutor responses and children’s interactions, and (3) insights into children’s experiences and perspectives. arXiv:2611.21306v1 [cs.AI] 16 Nov 2026 WiPSCE ’27, February 03 – 05, 2027, Ljubljana, Slovenia Jacobs, Berning, Kiesler 

## 2 Related Work 

Program Analysis and Feedback in Scratch. Related research on Scratch programs analyzes learners’ Scratch programs and uses the resulting data to inform automatically provided feedback. For example, LitterBox detects well-known bug patterns and explains them to learners [10]. Whisker tests program behavior against spec-ifications [45], and NuzzleBug [6], as another example, serves as an interactive debugger. Even though these and other tools may help identify problems in a program, their educational value depends (among other things) on how the resulting feedback guides learners’ next steps. Using LitterBox [10] and Whisker [45] for Scratchlog analysis, Greifenstein et al. [15] investigated the content of hints (as a form of feedback) with 36 children (aged 7-12) from 10 pro-gramming courses. The children were asked to debug six Scratch programs using different types of hints. The study found that pro-cedural hints naming the bug’s location led to the most successful program repairs. Likewise, children rated the bug location as the most helpful information within the hint. In other related work, Greifenstein et al. [16] found that children attending a block-based robot-programming course valued having the choice between text and image hints. Some of them even preferred pictures, as they could avoid reading. Greifenstein et al. [16] note that textual hints, unlike pictures, can easily be delivered as verbal, i.e., audio output. Scratch Copilot [9] supports children’s ideation, implementation, and debugging through a text chat. In a study with 18 children (aged 7-12), it answered an estimated 70% of queries successfully. Ten children wished it could observe their screen to better understand their questions or to nudge them when they were doing something wrong. Druga and Ko [9] conclude that such assistants need better context awareness and recommend flexible multimodal inputs and outputs. Addressing this need for context, Si et al. [43] introduce VisionScratch, which combines Scratch project code with videos of program execution to diagnose bugs and generate repairs. Because some issues manifest only in the final rendered gameplay video, the videos serve as additional context for a GenAI model. The system applies targeted code changes and checks them by executing the modified project. Its evaluation on ten faulty projects focuses on correct bug identification and valid fixes. Children’s interactions with such tutoring support were not evaluated by Si et al. [43]. Overall, these studies motivate exploring alternatives to textual presentation (e.g., video and audio) of information and interactions in learning environments involving Scratch. 

Multimodal Interaction in Programming Education. Ever since the advent of GenAI, there are several new ways to assist learners [33], for example, through chatbots [25, 38, 40] and feedback tools [3, 20, 21] using text. Multimodal interactions with GenAI have only recently been explored in programming education and learn-ing environments. Riegel et al. [36] studied text and speech-to-text input in the Prompt Programming web platform [30]. University students solved Prompt Problems by prompting GPT-4o-mini via voice or text to generate code, which they could run against prede-fined tests. Spoken prompts were automatically transcribed. Most students used and preferred text. One reason may have been the brevity of the prompts, which university students could write down fast. Another reason may have been due to speech recognition issues with the speech-to-text model used. In another study with elementary school students (grades 1-2), speech recognition also remained error-prone in noisy conditions even after fine-tuning the AI model on children’s voices [26]. Related work [22] further examined how novice programmers (aged 14-15) interacted via voiced prompts with a real-time GenAI voice tutor during programming tasks. They provided the model (gpt-4o-realtime-preview-2024-10-01 ) with current code and compiler output as context. Students primarily used the tutor for debugging. However, 28.6% (119/416) of the feedback outputs were incorrect, especially when the tutor attempted to verbalize code [22]. Similarly, Hou et al. [17] studied novices in higher education solv-ing a Parsons puzzle and a programming task using a platform with live screen sharing and audio interaction. They used the Google AI Studio platform with a multimodal model ( gemini-2.5-flash-preview-native-audio-dialog ). Participants valued the contin-uous availability of context. Yet, they also wanted time to formulate questions, push-to-talk input, and the possibility to interrupt the tutor as additional features [17]. Context seems to matter for voice agents even beyond program-ming. In cooking tasks, an AI agent without context produced irrel-evant responses and misinterpreted requests, whereas a context-aware agent led to more fluent interaction with more complex requests [18]. Si et al. [43] argue similarly for programs in Scratch: As correctness in Scratch is visually displayed, a video stream is required as context. 

Multimodal Tutoring for Scratch. Within Scratch, ChatScratch [5] provides verbal interaction through a cascaded pipeline of speech recognition (Whisper), a large language model (GPT-4), and speech synthesis. The assistant answers children’s spoken questions by highlighting block categories and inserting block templates into the editor. Meowcrophone [12] lets children with motor disabilities edit Scratch programs through voice commands. Beyond these two systems, we could not find work that offers audio-based interac-tion within Scratch. At the same time, neither of these systems is a tutor with access to the child’s current workspace and stage. VisionScratch [43] and EcoScratch [44] use the visual program state, but without any verbal interactions. In contrast to cascaded pipelines, native-audio models process spoken input and generate spoken output within a single model [37]. Via Google’s Live API, Gemini 2.5 Flash Native Audio additionally accepts screen frames, and users can interrupt it at any time [14]. Such models have rarely been studied as real-time voice tutors for Python [22] and with screen sharing in higher education [17], but not yet in Scratch or younger learners. Therefore, the present work explores the response quality of a native-audio model with access to children’s Scratch screens, as well as the interactions and experiences of children in grades 5 and 6. 

## 3 Methodology 

We conducted an exploratory study of a multimodal GenAI tutor comprising a pilot and a classroom evaluation. In the pilot evalua-tion, we examined tutor responses to typical programming errors in Scratch projects. The findings informed the classroom evaluation. In the classroom evaluation, fifth- and sixth-graders worked indi-vidually on Scratch tasks with the tutor using speech and screen How Young Learners Interact with Multimodal GenAI While Solving Scratch Tasks WiPSCE ’27, February 03 – 05, 2027, Ljubljana, Slovenia 

sharing. In the following, we will introduce the tutor’s setup before elaborating on the pilot and classroom study. Specifically, to address RQ1, we used qualitative content analysis to study the characteristics of tutor responses from both phases, considering the surrounding dialogue and Scratch screen recording. For RQ2, we analyzed the classroom recordings to characterize children’s voiced prompts. To address RQ3, we calculated counts and percentages from a questionnaire on perceived instructional support, trust in the tutor’s responses, and spoken communication. For the classroom evaluation, we followed the local regulatory re-quirements for conducting research in schools, which are as follows. School leadership provided written informed permission. Parents gave written informed consent to participation and could withdraw consent at any time. Consent covered audio and screen record-ings, transmission of speech and screen content to the Google service, and analysis and publication of pseudonymized data. We also asked all children for their consent and allowed them to stop participating at any time. The recordings contain only participating children and the teacher (who is also the second author), with no non-participants present.

## 3.2 Pilot Study 

Before giving children access to the GenAI tool, we systematically tested it with eight tasks including subtasks. Four computer science teachers at the given school reviewed the tasks and provided verbal feedback. Based on that, we used multiple subtasks (e.g., 2.1, 2.2, and 2.3) instead of a single task. Based on their suggestions, we also moved the fourth task (originally the second) to the end. Teach-ers expected most children to complete Task 1 and parts of Task 2 within one lesson. Together, the designed worksheet included eight (sub-)tasks. All complete task descriptions and links to the original AppCamps material are available in our supplementary data repository [19]. Task 1 asked children to make the Scratch cat walk a square, pause after each movement, and return to its initial position and direction.

The analysis of the pilot data followed the same method as in the classroom study, so the method is reported once in the meth-ods section. The pilot’s evaluation is reported in detail in Table 1, along with the classroom study’s results. The findings show that the tone was never offensive and never revealed the full answer. Nevertheless, some results were concerning. For example, in 15.3% (19/124) of turns, the tutor made a factual error. In 15.7% (8/51) of error-related responses, the tutor did not address an existing error, and in 4.8% (6/124) of responses, it diagnosed a non-existent error. These results led us to carefully reflect on our responsibility towards school children, and whether it was responsible to deploy the GenAI tutor at all. Our primary concern was the children’s wellbeing: a tutor that overlooks errors, invents non-existent ones, or points to the wrong location could confuse or discourage young learners. After weighing these risks, we decided to proceed only with safeguards. The tutor was used for a limited period (approx. 45 minutes), under continuous teacher supervision, and only after the children watched a video explaining the tutor can make mistakes. 

## 3.3 Data Collection: Classroom Evaluation 

In the following, we describe the data collection as part of the classroom evaluation. We introduce the overall process before and during the data collection and present the questionnaire used to gather children’s perceptions. We used the same Scratch tasks as in the pilot. Both the tasks and questionnaire are available in our supplementary data repository [19]. Consent was provided by all required stakeholders, as described above. 

3.3.1 Preparation and Sampling. In preparation for the classroom study, each class participated in six 60-minute Scratch lessons taught in a team consisting of the second author and another Com-puter Science (CS) teacher. For both the grade 5 and grade 6 classes, these six Scratch lessons were the first programming lessons. All previous CS lessons had been unplugged (without computers) and covered topics other than programming. These six Scratch lessons were based on the Scratch intro and AppCamps materials, in which the children always had access to a reference solution, and most exercises were solved together. Children were then assessed via questions about these Scratch projects. We provide more details about these six lessons in our supplementary data repository [19]. Twenty-three children attending Realschule, a lower secondary school type in Germany’s tiered school system (grades 5–10, ISCED level 2), participated: six children from each of two grade 5 classes and two grade 6 classes. 12 identified as boys and 11 as girls, aged 9 to 13 years (mean age, 𝑀 = 11 .3). Participation was voluntary. The number of participants was also limited by the available lap-tops, the school’s internet connection, and the overall noise during data collection. When more than six children were present, we ran-domly selected six. The four classroom sessions took place during December 2025. Children interacted with the tutor by voice while sharing the Scratch browser tab on 14-inch laptops we provided. They wore headsets (Logitech 960) with microphones and sat at distributed positions around the classroom to reduce interference between conversations. We recorded the screen, audio input, and output with Open Broadcaster Software (OBS). 

3.3.2 Classroom Protocol. The second author and teacher con-ducted all four sessions during 60-minute computer science lessons (one for each class, six children at a time). A different teacher taught the non-participating children in a different room. That means only the six students and the instructing teacher were together in one and the same room during the study. The procedure was as follows: (1) Introduction (10 minutes): Children received an explanation of Google AI Studio and watched a video, which demonstrated the tutor’s operation. It explained that responses could be incorrect, as observed in the pilot. The video and English subtitles are available in our supplementary data repository [19]. The teacher also encouraged children to question uncertain responses and to raise a hand when they had problems. (2) Task phase (45 minutes): Children worked individually on the worksheet with the tutor (see 3.1). The teacher restarted the tutor when necessary and occasionally provided content-related help. Children rarely talked to each other. (3) Questionnaire (5 minutes) 

We recorded the laptop screens and the children’s and tutor’s speech with the Open Broadcaster Software (OBS). Classroom recordings lasted 38 to 53 minutes. We transcribed the recordings locally using the open-weight whisper-large-v3-turbo speech recog-nition model [34] and synchronized the speaker-labeled, times-tamped transcripts with the screen recordings in MAXQDA [24]. We checked and corrected every transcript against the recordings, retaining fillers, repetitions, and incomplete utterances. 

3.3.3 Questionnaire. To address RQ3, we developed a study-specific questionnaire informed by the Partner Modelling Questionnaire (PMQ) [8, 42]. For our young participants, we created a shorter, statement-based format with simple wording and symbolic smileys. The reduced length, simplified wording, and symbols were intended to limit reading and response demands for fifth- and sixth-graders. Figure 5 shows all questions (Q1–Q10) and response options. The questionnaire comprised ten German-language statements rated on a five-category scale with labels and smileys: not at all true 

(1, very sad smiley), rather not true (2, sad smiley), in between (3, neutral smiley), rather true (4, happy smiley), and exactly true (5, very happy smiley). The questionnaire overlaps conceptually with selected aspects of the PMQ but does not reproduce its item content or three-factor structure. The full questionnaire is available in our supplementary data repository [19]. 

## 3.4 Data Analysis 

We conducted a qualitative content analysis with a deductive coding framework [28]. We adapted the framework from prior evaluation schemes and refined it by coding the pilot and classroom recordings. We applied the revised coding rules to both datasets. The initial coding scheme combined categories from Maurya et al. [27] for the GenAI tutor turns (RQ1) and categories from Scholl et al. [39] (with the voice adaptation by Jacobs and Kiesler [22]) for children’s verbalized prompts (RQ2). The final scheme comprised twelve tutor-related response categories (see Table 1) and codes on children’s prompts (see Table 2). The first and second authors met twelve times for about two hours between February and June 2026 to jointly code approx. 20% of the material (app. 3,300 out of 16,656 code assignments, which corresponds to approx. 600 out of 2989 coding How Young Learners Interact with Multimodal GenAI While Solving Scratch Tasks WiPSCE ’27, February 03 – 05, 2027, Ljubljana, Slovenia 

units). We also compared assignments, resolved disagreements by consensus, and refined the coding scheme. The second author applied the final coding scheme to the full sample. We treated one turn within the dialogue between child and GenAI tutor as coding unit, which was assigned multiple codes. Because a turn can only be assessed in relation to what it responds to (e.g., an answer in relation to the preceding question), the con-text unit always included the immediately preceding turn. When this was insufficient to make an unambiguous coding decision, we consulted earlier turns in the same dialogue and the corresponding sections of the screen recording as additional context. We also counted the number of words in each coded segment in the corrected German transcripts, excluding speaker labels and including fillers and repetitions. 

3.4.1 Tutor Responses (RQ1). A tutor turn is defined by the tutor starting to “speak” until it ends or is interrupted. Within the 1,506 tutor segments, we identified four non-overlapping groups: 1,170 task-related content turns, 181 early-interrupted turns, 139 small-talk turns, and 16 other turns. Each of the 1,170 task-related content turns was assigned one label for each of the twelve dimensions (see Table 1), resulting in multiple labels per turn. We did not fur-ther code the 181 early-interrupted turns because the tutor was interrupted too early to make any sense of its answer. It means the tutor’s intent was not understandable because the turn was too short (i.e., interrupted too early). For the 1,170 task-related turns, we retained Maurya et al.’s [27] eight dimension concepts but adapted ( †) and expanded ( ‡) their categories. We operationalized all definitions for Scratch and spo-ken interaction. For example, mistake identification and coherence 

incorporate the visible program state. We added subcategories for false-positive diagnoses, false locations, and turns without an error under discussion. Unlike the original partial-guidance category, which includes incorrect or incomplete guidance, our scheme sepa-rates incorrect guidance from incomplete or irrelevant guidance. It also distinguishes correct guidance with and without a next-step hint. Human-likeness asks whether the response sounds natural rather than robotic or artificial. In addition, we added four dimen-sions: factual correctness , elicitation of student input , auditory quality ,and overall impression informed by related work [22]. 

3.4.2 Children’s Interactions (RQ2). We adapted the categories from related work [22, 39] to describe children’s turns, i.e., their voiced prompts, in the dialog with the GenAI tutor (see Table 2). Voice prompt categories distinguish standalone queries (STA), follow-ups to the child’s own prompt (PRE), responses to the tutor (RES), and corrections (COR). Categories reflecting issues comprise de-bugging (DE), block selection and assembly (CG, originally code generation [39]), next steps (PP, originally pair programming [22]), 

problem understanding (PU), and conceptual understanding (CU). We counted solution requests (SR), requests to work alone or pause 

(WAF), and small talk (ST) separately. In addition, we coded whether children were talking in parallel with the tutor or interrupted the tutor. We also coded unintended voice inputs (e.g., thinking aloud or background noise). To report which task the students worked on, we added codes for each task, i.e., task markers. We calculated how many turns overlap with each task marker and how many students worked on each task. 

3.4.3 Questionnaire (RQ3). For the paper questionnaires, we digi-tized all responses and calculated counts and percentages per item. 

## 4 Results 

Although 24 students participated in the classroom evaluation, we excluded one fifth-grade child’s session (recordings and question-naire) because it contained only non-task-related interactions with the tutor. We thus analyzed 23 recordings and the corresponding 23 questionnaires. All data were pseudonymized using class and laptop number. Task markers indicated when students worked on each task: Task 1 was processed in 23 sessions, Task 2.1 in 19 sessions, and Task 2.2 in 1 session. No session progressed beyond Task 2.2. Task 1 accounted for 62.6% (732/1,170) of content turns, and subtask 2.1 for 35.5% (415/1,170). In the following, we present the analysis of the tutor’s responses, children’s interactions, and questionnaire responses to answer the three research questions. 

## 4.1 RQ1: Characteristics of Tutor Responses 

To address RQ1, we analyzed 1,170 GenAI tutor turns from the classroom study across twelve dimensions. Table 1 reports the complete category definitions, anchor examples, and frequency distributions. It further reports the analysis of 124 GenAI tutor turns from the pilot study. 

4.1.1 Mistake Identification and Location. We report the Yes , To some extent , and No /No location subcategories of the mistake iden-tification and mistake location categories related to the 603 error-related tutor turns in the classroom, excluding the 567 content turns labeled No mistake present . The excluded category covers turns without an error present or currently discussed. Within these error-related turns, full identification accounted for 25.0% (151/603), whereas missed errors accounted for 61.5% (371/603). Missed errors occurred in all but one session. Per session, their share of error-related turns ranged from 0.0% (0/15) to 82.4% (42/51) ( 𝑀 = 55 .2%, 

𝑆𝐷 = 21 .8) and was at least 50% in 15 of 23 sessions. The session with no errors missed contained 11 false positives instead. The tutor named a specific error location in 21.1% (127/603) and a false location in 13.6% (82/603). Across all content turns in the classroom, diagnoses of nonexistent errors, coded as False positive , accounted for 4.1% (48/1,170). As shown in Figure 4, the GenAI tutor repeat-edly does not recognize an error. At the same time, these turns can be factually correct. For example, the tutor may just focus on something else (see Figure 4, turn 7). 

4.1.2 Guidance and Revealing of the Answer. For providing guidance ,60.0% (702/1,170) of classroom turns offered correct, relevant sup-port. Incorrect instructions occurred in 19.7% (231/1,170), compared to 7.3% (9/124) in the pilot. Correct guidance included a next-step hint in 28.5% (334/1,170) of classroom turns. This differs from reveal-ing of the answer , which denotes a complete task solution within a turn. Although this never occurred, it does not mean the tutor did not provide a complete solution over a series of turns. 

4.1.3 Actionability, Coherence, and Elicitation. For actionability ,95.8% (1,121/1,170) of classroom turns referred to a next action or reflection. Coherence with the preceding utterance or action was rated positively in 80.3% (939/1,170), compared with 100.0% (124/124) in the pilot. ## 5 Discussion 

Intelligible, but Unreliable Tutor. The tutor missed an existing error in 61.5% (371/603) of error-related turns in the classroom study (compared to 15.7% (8/51) in the pilot). At the same time, 95.8% (1,121/1,170) of its turns were actionable, and no single turn revealed a complete solution. The GenAI tutor named a specific error location in only 21.1% (127/603) of error-related turns and a false location in 13.6% (82/603). This seems unfortunate, given that children in a Scratch debugging study identified the bug location as crucial for helpful hints [15]. Figure 4 also shows how the GenAI tutor can fail: after the child answers “yes”, the tutor confirms a nesting that is missing on the screen. Nonetheless, 98.4% (1,151/1,170) of the tutor’s turns were intelligible, and only one child could not clearly understand the voice. Yet, this is a precondition for using speech to lower reading and writing demands [5, 16]. Only 30.4% (7/23) of children agreed that the tutor helped them understand an error, and 53.2% (622/1,170) of turns were judged helpful (overall impression). Although a video had explained that responses could be incorrect, 39.1% (9/23) of the children trusted the tutor’s answers to always be correct, and only 30.4% (7/23) reported noticing mistakes. Similarly, ninth graders rated a voice tutor relatively high on competence, although 28.6% (119/416) of its feedback was incorrect [22]. 

Short Voiced Prompts from Young Learners. Children’s verbal-ized prompts were brief: 29.2% (355/1,216) comprised at most three words, and “yes” was the most frequent one (RQ2). Most prompts, 90.8% (1,104/1,216), were responses (RES) to the tutor, and only 4.3% (52/1,216) were standalone queries (STA). One reason may be the GenAI tutor, which included an open question in 54.9% (642/1,170) of its responses or a simple closed question in 17.5% (205/1,170) of its responses. An additional factor could be the children’s ability to put what they need into words. The six preparatory Scratch lessons were their first programming lessons, so their CS vocabulary for naming blocks or describing errors was likely limited. Age may also contribute, although we did not assess language skills. In her review of help-seeking in children, Nelson-Le Gall [29] notes that young children often indicate verbally that they have understood when they have not. Instead, they may express their lack of understanding nonverbally. The GenAI tutor used in this study does not capture nonverbal expressions. Therefore, some “yes” responses may not indicate understanding or may be misleading (as in Figure 4). Children also have to learn which context a GenAI tool needs. In the text chat of Scratch Copilot, the assistant often misunderstood vague questions such as “Make it bigger?” about an on-screen character [9]. Similarly, children aged 7-12 learned over time how to phrase questions to a Google Home device [4]. Screen sharing could make such references resolvable, but only if the tutor correctly perceives the program state. 

Screen Sharing Without Program State. The gap between pilot and classroom has several possible explanations. In the pilot, an adult described one inserted error at a time through a studio micro-phone (subsection 3.1). In the classroom, children answered briefly, interrupted the tutor, and the microphone captured unintended speech, conditions that impaired speech recognition in another spoken tutor for children [26]. Because the model is a black box, we cannot determine whether its errors originated from the screen frames, the children’s speech, or something else. 

## 6 Threats to Validity 

Some limitations should be noted when interpreting our results. For example, the study involved children from four classes at one school, using German and one tutor setup. Voluntary participation and space limitations may have biased the sample. Moreover, prior Scratch instruction does not establish equal individual knowledge or competency. Teacher assistance and peer conversations may also have affected the interactions. Another aspect is that the main coder (and second author) taught the classes, conducted the pilot, and ran the classroom sessions. These overlapping roles may have influenced judgments and led to bias. The first author independently coded four sessions and reviewed the remaining assignments, and both authors resolved disagreements by consensus. However, no inter-rater agreement estimate is available. It should further be noted that the analysis describes turns nested within sessions rather than independent observations of distinct errors, i.e., several ratings can concern one and the same unresolved error (e.g., Figure 4). Finally, the questionnaire only captured retrospective experiences of the lesson, including technical difficulties. 

## 7 Conclusions 

We studied a multimodal GenAI tutor (Gemini 2.5 Flash Native Audio Preview in Google AI Studio) with screen sharing in Scratch lessons of grades 5 and 6, combining an expert pilot, a content anal-ysis of 1,170 tutor turns and 1,216 voice prompts from 23 children in a classroom, and a questionnaire. Regarding RQ1, the tutor’s responses were intelligible and actionable, and no single turn re-vealed a complete solution, but they missed existing errors in 61.5% (371/603) of error-related turns, far more often than in the pilot, and often did not locate them. For RQ2, children’s voice prompts were brief and mostly responded to the tutor. Children regulated the pace through pause requests and interruptions. Concerning RQ3, children rated the voice more favorably than the content, and many trusted answers that were not always correct. The present study contributes (1) an evaluation scheme adapted to verbal interactions with screen-sharing data as context, (2) a classroom analysis show-ing that screen access did not ensure tutor responses matching the program state in our setting, and (3) insights into children’s experiences and perspectives. Future work should develop custom multimodal GenAI tutor variants with access to the project state and benchmark multimodal GenAI under authentic classroom con-ditions.
