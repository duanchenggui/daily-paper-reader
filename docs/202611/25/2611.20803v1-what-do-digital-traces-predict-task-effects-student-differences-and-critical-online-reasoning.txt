# What Do Digital Traces Predict? Task Effects, Student Differences, and Critical Online Reasoning 

## Maxim Konca * Alexander Mehler 

Institute of Informatics and Mathematics, Goethe University, Frankfurt am Main, Germany Manuscript submitted for publication 

Abstract 

Digital assessment traces are increasingly used to predict student performance, often evaluated by correlating predicted and observed scores pooled across tasks. However, such correlations may reflect between-task differences rather than performance differences among students within tasks. To investigate this, we analyzed up to 527 browsing sessions from 424 university students completing open-Internet Critical Online Reasoning tasks and assessed the cross-task stability of their ratings. We evaluated the same out-of-fold predictions within and across tasks, compared three validation tiers up to leave-one-task-out, and tested incremental predictive contributions of consultation order and relational structure against matched permutation and randomization controls. None of the twelve prespecified primary tests was significant: on held-out tasks, consulted-page features showed no significant association with relative student performance, nor did observed order or relational structure add predictive value. Pooling observations across tasks substantially increased correlations, particularly for factual correctness, whose scores showed limited evidence of stable between-student differences across tasks. However, sentence embeddings yielded modest positive correlations with generic response quality on held-out tasks, without establishing added predictive value from observed order or relational structure. Our findings show that pooled predictive correlations may not reflect within-task student differences, highlighting the need for within-task evaluation and criterion generalizability audits. 

Keywords: Critical Online Reasoning, trace data, predictive modeling, model evaluation, generalizability, task specificity. 

# 1 Introduction 

Digital assessment environments (see Katz, 2007, for an early example; Sparks et al., 2016, and Van Damme & Zahner, 2022, for reviews; and Tenison & Sparks, 2023, for a recent study) capture both student outcomes, such as written responses, and process data, including search queries in internet-based information problem-solving tasks, accessed resources, and interaction sequences. The competence assessed here is broadly critical thinking, understood as purposeful, self-regulatory judgment involving interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, and justification of the grounds for judgment (Facione, 1990, p. 3). Applied to online information literacy, critical thinking becomes Critical Online Reasoning (COR): searching, selecting, evaluating, and reasoning from online information to reach justified conclusions (Molerov et al., 2020). Critical thinking, as addressed by COR research, is typically measured as a latent construct inferred from process observables (e.g., page visits, dwell times, scrolling, and page transitions) and product observables (e.g., linguistic features of written responses) collected during assessment tasks. To infer COR from product variables, this approach uses rater scores of written responses as indicators of task-specific performance, which serves as an intermediary construct related to COR. The problem is that a data-driven predictor may correlate highly with these scores by exploiting task-specific rather than task-independent information. The challenge is therefore to demonstrate generalization first to unseen but related tasks and ultimately to unrelated tasks, while establishing construct validity. This article addresses the first challenge by modeling COR-related task-specific performance across related tasks using both process and product observables. In doing so, it revisits a longstanding problem in performance assessment research. Generalizability Theory conceptualizes observed performance scores as arising from samples of tasks, occasions, raters, and measurement methods, with the intended scope of generalization determining which sources of variation constitute measurement error (Brennan, 2001; Cronbach et al., 1972). In a study of elementary-level mathematics and science performance assessments, Shavelson et al. (1993) found that task-sampling variability contributed substantially more to measurement error than rater-sampling variability. This finding highlights the importance of accounting for task-specific variation when predicting

In particular, student-by-task interaction variance constitutes measurement error when generalizing student performance across tasks. A parallel problem arises in predictive learning analytics. Across nine university courses, Gaˇ sevi´ c et al. (2016) found that predictive performance and predictor relevance varied between course-specific and generalized models, demonstrating that ignoring instructional contexts can distort estimated associations between learning-management-system features and academic success. Similarly, the present study examines predictive generalization across assessment tasks using both process and product observables. Specifically, it investigates whether these observables contain task-independent predictive information. These two research traditions motivate a more stringent question than whether process or product observables correlate with observed rater scores. Predictive modeling must distinguish task-context information from information that differentiates students performing the same task (within-task predictive validity). It must also establish the incremental predictive value of each observable type and clarify what these observables represent. Crucially, it must determine whether within-task predictive relationships generalize to unseen tasks (cross-task predictive generalizability). More specifically, outcome scores derive from ratings of written responses, while both process- and product-related observables serve as predictors. We audit the performance criteria and evaluate predictive generalization at three levels: observation, student, and task. For process-related observables, we focus on consulted content pages, excluding search-results pages, which constitute approximately one-third of the captured pages; a sensitivity analysis includes the full browsing record. Using written-response features as the baseline, we assess the incremental predictive value of consulted-page content, order-invariant features, consultation order, and graph-based page relations. All models use matched observations for comparability. Permutation and randomization controls, along with neural ablations, test whether temporal and relational structures add predictive information beyond page content. In sum, our contribution is not to demonstrate task-sampling variability or limited predictive generalizability, both of which are already recognized challenges. Rather, we connect these problems in COR-related performance assessment by testing whether models based on process observables and product observables differentiate students within tasks (within-task predictive performance) and generalize to unseen tasks (cross-task predictive generalizability), while examining how much pooled associations reflect task differences. We further test whether structured process representations add predictive value beyond content representations ( C ), using matched null representations as controls. The study addresses the following research questions on within-task prediction and cross-task generalizability: RQ1 Product: To what extent do linguistic features of written responses, not explicitly included in the rating criteria, predict COR-related performance as measured by rater scores? RQ2 Process content: To what extent do linguistic features of consulted documents predict COR-related performance? RQ3 Process sequence: Does consultation order add predictive information beyond the unordered set of consulted documents? RQ4 Process network: Do graph-based representations of information-seeking trajectories add predictive information beyond document content and consultation order? RQ5 Generalizability: To what extent do models evaluated under RQ1–RQ4 transfer to unseen tasks? 

# 2 Related work 

The assessed construct. COR was conceptualized as a construct and assessment framework for university students (Molerov et al., 2020). Generic COR tasks were subsequently validated using several methods, including generalizability theory (Nagel & Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia, 2025; Nagel et al., 2022). Nagel et al. (2022) found that person and person-by-task variance dominated task and rater effects, interpreting both as meaningful performance variation consistent with the assessment design. Because variance components depend on the sampled students, tasks, raters, and scoring procedures, we re-estimate them here. Student-by-task interaction variance may reflect meaningful task-specific performance differences but limits the interpretation of scores or predictions as indicators of task-general competence. Hartig et al. (2026) document the rating procedure for generic COR tasks involving online information acquisition, evidence-based reasoning, and synthesis. We use existing aggregate ratings and audit their underlying criteria rather than developing new ones. 

Comprehension across multiple documents. Reading across multiple sources to answer questions is conceptualized as an interaction among reader, task, and texts rather than a reader attribute alone (List & Alexander, 2019; Rouet & Britt, 2011). The MD-TRACE model reflects this perspective and is particularly relevant to COR assessment because it treats task-model construction and evaluation of the resulting product as core processes (Rouet & Britt, 2011). COR-related performance scores 2are expected to be task dependent because tasks differ in their requirements, students’ interpretations and responses, and the criteria used to evaluate performance. The variance decomposition in Section 4.2 quantifies this variation without necessarily implying deficiencies in the rating rubric. 

Process observables as performance predictors. Predicting performance from process observables is well established in learning analytics, with growing emphasis on interaction sequences rather than isolated events. Yu et al. (2025) mined event sequences from 49 undergraduates completing a flipped-learning mathematics task, identifying six patterns correlated with post-test performance ( |r| ≥ 0.2), five involving multiple events and three negatively correlated. However, these predictors were selected and evaluated on the same sample, without permutation controls. Thus, the findings link sequence features to performance but do not establish the predictive contribution of event order itself. Our study tests this contribution. In the COR setting of Molerov et al. (2020), Schmidt et al. (2020) collected eye-tracking and event-log data from 32 undergraduates completing a single COR task. They used process mining to characterize information seeking and latent class analysis to identify two student profiles differing in process characteristics and rated task performance. We likewise examine information seeking but focus on content-document visits and transitions, incorporating a content model absent from Schmidt et al. (2020). 

Aggregation and transfer across tasks. Pooled task correlations may differ from within-task correlations because they also reflect between-task variation. Thus, a high pooled correlation may reflect within-task differences, between-task differences, or both, without reliably distinguishing students performing the same task. This distinction also matters for prediction from process observables. Gaˇ sevi´ c et al. (2016) found that the same LMS feature could correlate positively or negatively with success depending on the course, and that significance differed between pooled and within-course models. These findings show that pooled predictive performance does not necessarily identify features predictive within or across contexts. A complementary methodological concern is the statistical reliability of predictive model comparisons. Reviewing 87 predictive modeling experiments in MOOCs, Gardner and Brooks (2018) found that 52 lacked significance tests for model comparisons and none corrected for multiple testing. A later review of 39 studies and 82 models of course-specific academic success found that performance was most often evaluated using accuracy alone (Arizmendi et al., 2023). Neither review examines the distinction between within-task and between-task predictive associations central to this study. The portability of predictive models across educational contexts remains a concern in learning analytics. A review of 70 studies using log data from open-ended science and engineering environments identified task design, construct definition, feature extraction, and model validation as barriers to generalizability (Wang et al., 2023). More recently, Xu et al. (2026) trained models at one institution and tested them at another using standardized behavioral features from a shared online textbook, keeping the learning platform, content, and feature definitions relatively stable while varying the institutional context. Our study examines complementary generalization: the assessment instrument and feature definitions remain fixed, while each of four tasks and its students are excluded from training in turn. This design tests whether predictive performance persists when the evaluation task and its students are excluded from training. 

# 3 Materials and Methods 

## 3.1 Data reconstruction and analysis populations 

We analyze student performance on four Critical Online Reasoning (COR) assessment tasks requiring students to consult online information and prepare written responses. Each student-task observation comprises a written response and process observables recorded during task completion. The four tasks, drawn from Hartig et al. (2026), examine health risks from cell-phone towers (FM), mosquito-bite treatment with heat sticks (HS), Tetra Pak recyclability (TP), and poisoning from a traditional German dish (GS). Page visits were reconstructed from page-opening logs and assigned to individual students and tasks. We extracted linguistic features from process and product texts using Coh-Metrix (Graesser et al., 2004; McNamara et al., 2014). We chose Coh-Metrix for its established use in linguistic analysis, cross-linguistic applicability, and standardized feature set, avoiding an exhaustive comparison of alternative models. As the tasks and most texts were in German, we computed the Coh-Metrix features using German-language text processing, excluding non-German texts and those shorter than 50 words under an inherited minimum-content rule before constructing either feature representation. Reconstruction rules, URL matching, classifiers, item mapping, model settings, and ablation designs are documented in the Supplement. 

Page sets. Visited pages were classified by URL as either search engine results pages (SERPs) or content pages, the latter being a pragmatic rather than a comprehensive genre category. Page set A = C ∪ S comprises all pages retained after preprocessing, partitioned into content pages ( C) and SERPs ( S), with C ∩ S = /0. Page sets determine which pages are 3considered, whereas analysis populations comprise the student-task observations eligible for analysis within each page set. 1

A student-task observation is eligible for a given page set if at least two pages meet the feature-extraction requirements. As RQ2–RQ4 focus on consulted documents, C was designated the primary page set if two prespecified criteria were met: at least 30 eligible observations per task and at most 25% two-page trajectories. Section 4.1 reports whether these criteria were met. 

Ratings and annotations. The study uses observed rater scores assigned by trained student raters using the generic-COR rating procedure (Hartig et al., 2026). The primary analyses use the supplied criterion-level aggregate scores; neither the rating procedure nor the original aggregation was modified. Topic, genre, and emotion labels were assigned during data preparation using the classifiers documented in the supplement. 

## 3.2 Performance outcomes and criterion audit 

The four tasks share a rating rubric with generic criteria worded identically across tasks and task-specific factual-correctness criteria. After rescaling the selected criteria to [0, 1], we formed three unweighted composites: Domain-Independent Response Quality (DIRQ ; general linguistic and formal quality), the mean of response fulfillment, source referencing, and COHerence 

(COH ; semantic connectedness and conceptual consistency of the response); Task-Specific Factual Correctness (TaSFC ; factual correctness relative to task requirements), the mean of the task’s factual-correctness criteria; and Overall , the mean of all selected criteria. Reliability and agreement coefficients describe the ratings but do not weight the composites. The primary outcomes are DIRQ , TaSFC , and COH . COH , the only single criterion among them, is analyzed separately although it enters 

DIRQ , so the two analyses are not independent. Response fulfillment and source referencing are analyzed as exploratory components of DIRQ .Secondary outcomes include the unstandardized Overall composite and within-task standardized DIRQ , TaSFC , and Overall scores. Standardized scores are defined as zit = ( xit − μt )/σt , with mean μt and standard deviation σt computed from all valid ratings for task t, independently of the modeling cohort. Retrospective standardization equalizes task means and standard deviations but does not ensure equivalent score interpretations across tasks. The rating-only audit uses the 644 of 664 rated responses that have valid DIRQ , TaSFC , and COH scores to assess cross-task rank-order consistency and estimate variance components attributable to students, student-by-task interactions, raters, and rater-by-task interactions. Of the 144 students observed on two tasks, 136 had valid scores for both. Each variance component is tested using a likelihood-ratio test comparing the fitted model with a reduced model omitting that component. Projected dependability coefficients are reported without confidence intervals and interpreted solely as guidance for assessment design.

## 3.4 Estimators, validation tiers, and scoring 

For fixed-length features, we use random forest regression (Breiman, 2001) as the primary estimator, with radial basis function support vector regression and partial least squares regression for sensitivity analyses, all with fixed hyperparameters and without tuning. As exploratory RQ3 and RQ4 benchmarks, we fit a single-layer gated recurrent unit (GRU) (Cho et al., 2014) and a message-passing graph network (Gilmer et al., 2017), with matched order and relation ablations. We fit neural models to predict DIRQ , TaSFC , and COH from sequences or graphs of page vectors xU

> i j

, using three random seeds. All predictor transformations, including imputation, scaling, principal components, annotation levels, and representation adapters, are fitted only on the respective training partition. Task standardization transforms targets, not predictors, and its constants include evaluation data, so standardized outcomes are retrospective targets unavailable in prospective prediction. We use three evaluation tiers (T1–T3) with increasingly restrictive train-test separation and fixed split manifests based on page set A, shared across outcomes, representations, estimators, and feature sources. 

Tier T1, observation-level. We partition student-task observations into five task-stratified folds and repeat this procedure five times. Observations from the same students and tasks may occur in both training and test sets. 

Tier T2, student-grouped. We use five-fold, task-stratified, student-grouped cross-validation with five repetitions, ensuring student-disjoint train-test splits while retaining all tasks in training. 

Tier T3, task holdout. We hold out each task in turn, excluding all observations from its students from training, thereby evaluating transfer to an unseen task with student-disjoint splits. T2 prevents student overlap between training and test sets, following blocked cross-validation principles for hierarchical and repeated-measures data (Roberts et al., 2017). T3 retains student-level separation while training on three tasks and testing exclusively on the fourth. Treating each task as a distinct domain, the training and test sets are domain-disjoint, corresponding to the domain-generalization setting described by Koh et al. (2021). As all four tasks belong to the same study, T3 provides retrospective leave-one-task-out transfer evaluation (RQ5) rather than prospective or independent external validation. Inference. We define a specification contrast as the difference in mean within-task Spearman correlations between predictor specifications evaluated on identical held-out observations. Confidence intervals use a student-cluster percentile bootstrap (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993); contrasts are tested by paired permutations within students, and individual correlations by permuting ratings within tasks. For RQ3 and RQ4, we use conjunction tests (logical AND tests): the null hypothesis states that at least one of the two component effects is non-positive, whereas the alternative requires both to be positive. The first component tests incremental predictive value relative to the preceding baseline. The second tests whether the observed representation outperforms its matched permutation or randomization control, distinguishing structural contributions from gains due merely to additional features. Logged events do not explain the processes generating them, nor does predictive value establish their interpretation. We therefore do not interpret order and relational features as specific cognitive strategies but test whether observed structural information adds predictive value beyond matched controls. The conjunction p-value is the larger component p-value if both estimated effects are positive, and 1 otherwise (Berger, 1982). RQ2 uses separate tests because its two quantities address distinct estimands: whether consulted-page content predicts performance and whether it adds predictive information beyond the written response. Neither claim is conditional on the other. We use paired student-cluster bootstraps to assess differences between evaluation tiers and the pooling gap , defined as the difference between pooled and within-task Spearman correlations. # 4 Results 

## 4.1 Analysis sample and process-data coverage 

Figure 1 shows the selection of student-task observations. Of 664 rated responses, 544 from 435 students had at least one recorded page visit; 532 had at least two analyzable pages. Page set C yields 527 eligible observations from 424 students at three German universities; 514 have valid scores on all three primary outcomes, with at least 86 scored observations per task. The prespecified criteria for C were met: the smallest task had 92 eligible observations, and 474 of 527 trajectories (90%) contained at least three pages. Two-page trajectories, with structurally zero relational contrast, comprised 10.1% of eligible observations in C and 1.9% in A.Of the sources students named in their responses, 76% matched recorded visits with analyzable text, whereas 15% came from sessions without recorded visits.

Stronger evidence of TaSFC ’s lower cross-task consistency comes from the variance decomposition below: at least half the variance reflects student-by-task interactions, while stable between-student variance is not significantly different from zero. The variance decomposition helps explain these differences (Table 1). With only 136 students scored on two tasks, separating stable student from student-by-task variance is particularly uncertain. Despite the uncertainty, TaSFC shows a pronounced difference between its student-by-task interaction variance (64%) and student variance (8%), suggesting that TaSFC scores depend substantially more on the particular student-task combination than on stable between-student differences. By contrast, DIRQ and COH have similar estimated student variance shares (23% and 19%, respectively). Because each student completed each task only once, the student-by-task interaction component confounds task-specific and occasion-specific variation. This student-by-task/occasion variation indicates limited cross-task consistency in 

TaSFC , without distinguishing task-sampling from occasion effects. Stable between-student variation is established for DIRQ 

and COH , potentially supporting cross-task transfer, but not for TaSFC . However, the stable components cannot be ranked across outcomes: TaSFC ’s interval reaches 20%, near DIRQ ’s estimate of 23%. Rater components were the least precise estimates; among the primary outcomes, only COH showed a significant rater main effect (19%, p = 0.001). A COR validation study likewise found substantial person-by-task variance and small rater effects (Nagel et al., 2022), while task-sampling effects are documented in other performance assessments (Shavelson et al., 1993). Our findings agree for TaSFC and DIRQ , whose rater main effects are not significant, but differ for COH , whose effect is significant. This required within-task prediction, transfer to unseen tasks, and, for structural features, gains over matched permutation controls. Each analysis yielded a distinct finding. Across 576 configurations at Tiers 1 and 2, pooled correlations exceeded within-task correlations by a median of 0 .169. The criterion audit qualified null findings: TaSFC ’s stable between-student variance was only 8% and not distinguishable from zero, making null results less informative than for DIRQ or 

COH . Finally, permutation controls tested whether predictive gains reflected observed order or relational structure rather than additional features. The prespecified results provide little support for incremental predictive contributions. At Tier 3, Coh-Metrix features of consulted-page content showed no significant within-task association with performance, and neither structural conjunction test was significant. ## 5.2 What the criterion audit implies 

Non-significant predictions may reflect weak predictors, an unstable criterion, or both, particularly for TaSFC . Student-by-task variation accounted for 64% of TaSFC variance (95% profile-likelihood interval: 53%–69%), without distinguishing task from occasion effects. Stable between-student variance was 8% (95% interval: 0%–20%), and relative generalizability for one task rated by three raters was 0 .10. 

TaSFC may still be predictable on a held-out task, but the audit limits its interpretation as a stable, task-general attribute. Its four non-significant tests are therefore less informative about task-general predictive validity than those for DIRQ and COH .Stable between-student variance was 23% for DIRQ and 19% for COH , with single-task generalizability coefficients of 0 .39 and 0.40, respectively. Both components differed significantly from zero, but their magnitudes cannot be compared precisely given the confidence intervals and few repeated students. For DIRQ , student-by-task variance was also 23%, leaving the relative contributions uncertain. Thus, non-significant DIRQ and COH results more strongly, though still cautiously, constrain the task-general predictive value of the tested trace representations. Rater effects warrant similar caution. Estimated rater variance was 2% for TaSFC and 19% for COH , but both estimates are imprecise given six raters with unequal workloads. Systematic rater severity differences were established for COH , without evidence that the effects differ significantly between outcomes. 

## 5.3 Where positive associations appeared 

Sentence embeddings yielded the strongest trace-based associations with performance. At Tier 3, the full fixed-vector specification correlated with DIRQ at ρ = 0.150, significant after correction; seven of eight neural specifications were also significant. However, five were unordered baselines or randomized controls, some outperforming their observed counterparts. No matched fixed-vector, graph, or sequence comparison established significant gains from observed order or relational structure. Thus, embeddings predicted DIRQ without identifying which trace aspects contributed. Comparable evidence was absent for TaSFC and COH .Written-response features showed the strongest within-task association with DIRQ (ρ = 0.320). Adding Coh-Metrix page features did not improve prediction beyond the written response alone. Because features and ratings derived from the same written text, this association does not establish predictive value for the preceding browsing process. The rated response contained performance-related information beyond the seventeen Coh-Metrix indices. Within tasks, response length correlated with DIRQ (ρ = 0.391) and COH (ρ = 0.338), remaining nearly unchanged after controlling for these indices (0 .393 and 0 .337). Length was not among the indices. These associations do not establish that length contributed 14 to the response model’s predictive performance. 

## 5.4 Implications for trace-based assessment 

The findings support five recommendations for evaluating trace-based assessments. First, report pooled and within-task performance from identical out-of-fold predictions when multiple tasks are involved. Their difference indicates the contribution of between-task variation without constituting an additive decomposition. This extends concerns about aggregated metrics in learning analytics (Arizmendi et al., 2023; Gardner & Brooks, 2018) by highlighting task boundaries as an evaluation choice. Second, claims of transfer to unseen tasks require evaluation on tasks excluded from training. Our study tests transfer across tasks within one multisite assessment, whereas earlier work examined transfer across institutions using a shared instrument (Xu et al., 2026). These designs address distinct generalization boundaries. Positive embedding-based associations with DIRQ 

indicate cross-task predictive transfer, but matched comparisons established no additional contribution from observed order or relational structure. Third, claims about order and relational structure require controls that reorganize the same events while preserving content and model class. Sequence-performance associations alone do not establish order-specific predictive information (Yu et al., 2025). In the present study, several observed-order models correlated positively with DIRQ , but shuffled or randomized controls performed similarly, with no significant structural advantage after correction. Fourth, assessment tasks and session contents must be documented to interpret browsing traces as evidence about students. Task prompts constrain consulted pages, written responses, and evaluation criteria. Search-results pages, content pages, and responses capture distinct aspects of this process. Trace interpretations must therefore account for both the task and the underlying page set. Finally, model evaluation cannot compensate for a criterion lacking task-general validity. Task-general interpretation requires stable between-student differences, which the criterion audit did not establish for TaSFC . Single-task prediction therefore cannot validate TaSFC as a task-general attribute, and averaging across tasks improves generalizability only if such stability exists. 

## 5.5 Limitations and future directions 

This retrospective study evaluates one multisite assessment without prospective or external validation. The final plan was fixed before modeling corrected data but informed by earlier analyses, precluding independent preregistration. Tier 3 holds out each of four tasks in turn, the smallest containing 86 scored observations. Moreover, the inferential intervals resample students rather than tasks and therefore do not quantify uncertainty arising from the selection of these four tasks. Holding out different tasks leaves 337–441 scored training observations, confounding task identity with training-set size. Thus, transfer findings apply to the studied tasks, not an unrestricted population of future tasks. The primary analysis includes only student-task observations with at least two analyzable content pages. Students consulting fewer than two pages may differ in information seeking or performance but lacked eligible browsing traces. Ratings, however, did not differ systematically between responses with and without at least two analyzable pages (mean DIRQ 0.609 vs. 0.594, 

TaSFC 0.457 vs. 0.469, COH 0.655 vs. 0.649). None of the twelve task-wise comparisons approached significance (all 

p ≥ 0.135). Of eligible traces, 10% contained exactly two pages, for which RQ3 captures only visit direction and the RQ4 observed-versus-randomized contrast is structurally zero. Thus, RQ3 and RQ4 estimate average increments including traces with limited or no structural variation. URL-based page classification makes content pages a proxy for documents. Coh-Metrix indices use a German adaptation; topic, genre, and emotion classifiers were not validated on these pages. The representations omit queries, dwell times, within-page reading, source credibility, and task-specific relevance. Thus, non-significant findings concern the tested representations and effect sizes, not browsing traces generally. The criterion audit includes only 136 students assessed once on each of two tasks, limiting evidence of stable between-student variance, particularly for TaSFC .The study covers four tasks, one assessment framework and construct, three universities, and one national context. This reflects the context dependence reported by Gaˇ sevi´ c et al. (2016), who found varying trace-feature contributions across nine courses. Predictive value depends on recorded behavior, performance measures, and training contexts. Replication should therefore vary tasks, frameworks, institutions, and populations, not merely algorithms. A key objective for future research is to develop representations that generalize across tasks while remaining sensitive to meaningful differences between students. Future studies could compare selected with available sources or model alignment between consulted content and task demands, capturing information seeking on a common, task-relative scale. However, 15 features must retain consistent meanings and comparable predictive relationships across tasks. This requires diverse tasks, repeated student observations, and explicit tests of cross-task comparability and transfer. 

## 5.6 Conclusion 

Our study shows that high correlations between browsing traces and performance may largely reflect differences between tasks rather than between students within tasks. Across 576 configurations, pooled correlations exceeded within-task correlations by a median of 0 .169. This suggests models captured task-related differences without necessarily recognizing task identity. On held-out tasks, Coh-Metrix page features showed no reliable within-task prediction, nor did observed order or relational structure add significant predictive value. Observed-order features improved TaSFC prediction over the unordered baseline but not over permuted order, precluding attribution to the actual consultation sequence. Sentence embeddings yielded modest correlations with DIRQ (approximately 0 .15), unlike Coh-Metrix features. However, unordered and randomized controls showed similar associations, with no significant structural advantage. Thus, traces predicted DIRQ modestly, but the responsible features remain unidentified. In the primary analysis, written-response features showed the strongest within-task association with DIRQ (ρ = 0.32). Because predictors and ratings derive from the same text, this does not independently implicate browsing behavior. Non-significant results do not rule out predictive value in browsing traces or their structure. Power for small associations was limited, stable between-student variance was not established for TaSFC , short traces constrained structural comparisons, and transfer involved only four tasks. Three evaluation checks distinguished apparent performance from generalizable predictive information. Comparing pooled and within-task correlations revealed the contribution of task differences. Criterion audits checked whether stable between-student variation supported task-general interpretations. Matched controls tested whether observed order or relational structure added predictive value beyond reorganized traces. These findings have implications beyond the present assessment: predicting ratings, distinguishing students within tasks, generalizing to unseen tasks, and identifying the predictive contribution of specific trace properties are distinct achievements that require distinct evidence. 

# Ethical Considerations 

This study uses students’ browsing histories and written responses to model performance on online critical reasoning tasks. Because these data may reveal sensitive information about students’ behavior and achievement, their collection and use require informed consent, privacy safeguards, and secure data handling. We also note that models trained on such data may reproduce existing biases and may not generalize across prompts, student populations, or educational settings. Accordingly, we do not consider these models appropriate for high-stakes decisions about individual students, and any educational use should remain low-stakes and subject to human oversight. Finally, because browsing behavior is only an indirect proxy for reading and reasoning, model outputs should not be interpreted as direct evidence of students’ comprehension strategies or cognitive states.

## Data reconstruction and trace audit 

During task completion, page openings were logged and the text of each opened page was captured. The capture could store several versions of the text associated with one visit. Each visit received the largest usable version, and the latest version when several were equally large, because late captures were sometimes partial loads or consent overlays. Pages captured without a separate opening event were inserted at their first capture time. Assessment-platform pages, browser-internal and non-web addresses, and search-engine click-through redirects were removed. Successive openings of the same URL within two seconds were treated as one load, whereas later returns were retained as revisits; dwell time was not used. The 50-word minimum for analyzable texts (Section 3.1) is not required by Coh-Metrix; in our data, shorter captures often consisted of navigation fragments or isolated sentences, for which adjacent-sentence indices are undefined (Graesser et al., 2004). Named-source URLs were matched to visits at page or site level after resolving search-engine redirects, removing protocols, leading www. or m., query strings, and trailing slashes, and lower-casing the host and path. Topic, genre, and emotion labels were assigned with classla/multilingual-IPTC-news-topic-classifier , classla/xlm-roberta-base-multilingual-text-genre-classifier , and MilaNLProc/xlm-emo-t , respectively. 

Audit procedures. The audit tracked student-task observations at successive stages of data processing, from rater scores and process observables to eligibility and feature extraction, while examining page coverage and named-source recovery. A named source was classified as opened if its corresponding page appeared in the same student-task session, and as analyzed if its textual content was included in the analysis. The audit used outcome data solely to determine the availability of rater scores for each observation. Among pages with recorded visits and captured text, 94% were in German, compared with 89% of content pages with captured text. Content pages were longer than SERPs (median: 759 vs. 220 words; Table S8). Most sessions with recorded visits had at least three content pages with analyzable text (474 of 544 sessions; median 5). We also assessed whether sources cited in students’ responses matched recorded page visits. Of these sources, 76% matched recorded visits with analyzable text and 8% matched visits without it. Fewer than 2% lacked matching visits despite available traces, while 15% came from sessions without recorded visits (percentages rounded; Figure S1).

## Criterion audit: further results 

For the rater-sensitivity analysis only, criterion scores were recomputed twice: once using all raters and once excluding an unusually severe COH rater. No correction for rater severity was applied. The composites are not rated directly. Instead, raters score individual criteria, from which composite scores are calculated separately for each rater according to Section 3.2, yielding one composite score per response and rater. The variance decomposition is based on these rater-specific composite scores. Cross-task consistency is estimated using data from the 136 students with valid scores on both tasks. Each score is first converted to its rank among all valid responses to the same task, thereby removing task-specific differences in location and scale. Each student then contributes one pair of ranks, with the two tasks ordered alphabetically rather than by completion time. A single Spearman correlation ρ is computed across these pairs, pooling students from all six possible task combinations. A two-sided test assesses the null hypothesis of zero Spearman correlation, with confidence intervals obtained using an approximate Fisher transformation and student-level bootstrapping. The generalizability coefficient expresses the ratio of stable between-student variance to expected observed-score variance and, under the assumptions of Generalizability Theory, corresponds to the expected squared correlation between a student’s observed score and their universe score, averaged over all admissible tasks (Cronbach et al., 1972). For a single task rated by three raters, the most common configuration in our sample, the estimated coefficients are 0 .39 for DIRQ , 0 .40 for COH ,and 0 .10 for TaSFC . These estimates characterize the outcomes as operationalized in Section 3.2, rather than the rubric itself: COH is an individual rating criterion, whereas DIRQ and TaSFC are composites constructed from multiple criteria. 

TaSFC averages between 5–9 binary items, depending on the task, so differences in measurement length may contribute to the estimated student-by-task variance rather than reflecting task-dependent performance alone. The remaining coefficients are projections for measurement designs not observed in this study: averaging scores across four tasks per student would yield an estimated generalizability coefficient of 0 .70 for DIRQ and COH , whereas TaSFC would require approximately 22 tasks to reach the same level. Under the fitted variance-component model, the main constraint on generalizability is therefore the number of tasks completed per student, rather than the number of raters per task. All these coefficients presuppose a measurement model in which a task-general student standing can be defined and its generalizability estimated. For DIRQ and 

COH , the stable between-student component is statistically distinguishable from zero, supporting the prospect of increasingly reliable task-general rankings as more tasks are sampled. For TaSFC , no such component is established; if its true value is zero, averaging over additional tasks cannot produce a reliable task-general ranking, regardless of how many tasks are administered. The single-task generalizability coefficients largely reflect the cross-task consistency patterns already reported in Section 4.2 rather than providing independent evidence. The projections may also be optimistic if person-by-rater variation is absorbed into the residual and persists across tasks, since such variation would not diminish through task averaging. The clearest descriptive pattern is that the shortest responses received lower and more variable COH scores. The design cannot distinguish between two potentially overlapping explanations. Response length may be associated with competence, as maintaining coherence across more material is demanding, but raters may also be influenced by fluency and response length independently of coherence. The measured text properties did not explain the association: after controlling for the seventeen response-level Coh-Metrix indices, the partial correlations of response length with COH and DIRQ were 0 .337 and 0 .393, respectively, compared with unadjusted correlations of 0 .338 and 0 .391. Within each task, response length, the outcome, and the seventeen indices were rank-transformed. Response length and the outcome were then separately residualized against the indices, and the resulting residuals were correlated. Task-specific correlations were averaged with equal weight. Two additional findings suggest that the association cannot be attributed solely to raters’ perceptions of response length. Rater disagreement about COH was not appreciably associated with response length ( ρ = −0.05), while the partial correlation between response length and factual correctness increased from 0 .11 to 0 .16 after adjustment for the text indices. The latter pattern is consistent with longer responses covering more factual-correctness checklist items. Associations were robust to score construction: omitting codebook-range scaling barely changed DIRQ ’s length correlation (0 .391 to 0 .398), while treating a missing bonus item as missing rather than zero left TaSFC ’s unchanged (0 .103). 

## Inference details 

Because some students completed two tasks, bootstrap resampling and paired permutation tests use students as sampling units to preserve within-student dependence. By contrast, permutation tests for individual correlations shuffle ratings independently within tasks, breaking cross-task correspondence between observations from the same student. We construct 95% confidence intervals using student-cluster percentile bootstrapping with 2 000 resamples (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993). We obtain two-sided 

p-values for specification contrasts from 5 000 paired permutations, swapping the predictions of the two specifications within students. We compute correlations within task, repeat, and neural-seed blocks, applying each student’s swap consistently across all blocks containing their observations. For individual correlations, we use the same bootstrap procedure but obtain one-sided 

p-values by permuting ratings within tasks. An intersection–union test rejects the global null only when both component null hypotheses are rejected; its p-value is therefore the maximum of the two valid component p-values (Berger, 1982). Our component tests are two-sided, so small 

p-values can arise from effects in either direction. To enforce the directional alternative, we set the conjunction p-value to the 25 larger component p-value if both estimated effects are positive, and to 1 otherwise. The task-wise permutation scheme is our declared procedure for testing individual correlations. Because it does not preserve cross-task dependence among observations from the same student, we additionally evaluate the three RQ2 correlation tests against a student-block permutation null and report both results. Under this alternative scheme, students are treated as exchangeable blocks: their rating vectors are permuted only among students who completed the same set of tasks, preserving both task membership and within-student dependence. This follows the multi-level block permutation framework of Winkler et al. (2015), with students as whole-block exchangeable units within strata defined by their completed task sets. The 417 students with scored observations form ten such strata; for students observed on only one task, the procedure coincides with the declared task-wise permutation scheme. For ρ(C C), the declared p-values of 0 .959, 0 .718, and 0 .788 for DIRQ , TaSFC , and 

COH change to 0 .966, 0 .709, and 0 .802, respectively, under student-block permutation. The maximum absolute difference is 0.014, and both procedures yield the same inferential conclusions. The conjunction rule of Section 3.5, which takes the larger component p-value when both estimates are positive and 1 otherwise, is conservative relative to a conjunction test based on valid one-sided component p-values, provided the two-sided tests appropriately control both tails. Although step-down procedures control the family-wise error rate regardless of family size, their statistical power may decrease as the number of tests increases (Holm, 1979). For large-scale model comparisons involving thousands of tests, Gardner and Brooks (2018) discuss Bayesian alternatives. Failure to reject the null hypothesis does not establish the absence of an effect. The upper confidence bound nevertheless indicates how large a positive effect remains compatible with the data. For the six individual primary contrasts, point estimates are close to zero, and their confidence intervals constrain the magnitude of positive effects compatible with the observed data. We use the upper endpoint of the two-sided 90% confidence interval as a one-sided 95% upper confidence bound, corresponding to a significance level of 5%. This differs from the two-sided 95% confidence intervals reported elsewhere. All six RQ2 estimates lie within 0 .08 of zero. The one-sided 95% upper confidence bounds are 0 .16, 0 .11, and 0 .11 for the correlations between consulted-page content and DIRQ , TaSFC , and COH , respectively, and 0 .09, 0 .13, and 0 .05 for the corresponding incremental contributions beyond written-response features. Across the twelve component contrasts underlying the order and relation conjunction tests, the one-sided 95% upper confidence bounds range from 0 .05 to 0 .16. The largest upper bound concerns the TaSFC comparison between observed order and the order-invariant baseline, for which the estimated improvement was 0 .098 and the confidence interval excluded zero. The conjunction nevertheless failed because observed order did not outperform its permutation control, rather than because both component contrasts were negligible. Under the assumed effect sizes and variability, a replication of this design would have approximately 80% power to detect a correlation of 0 .13 or an incremental correlation of 0 .08. These estimates apply to individual, unadjusted tests, not to the Holm-adjusted test families or conjunction tests. None of these analyses was prespecified: the analysis plan included no equivalence tests, and recomputing confidence bounds from the previously established intervals verifies their numerical consistency without making the resulting bounds part of the prespecified analysis. 

## Student-stable variance in the predictors 

The criterion audit decomposes the variance of the rated outcomes. The same question can be put to the predictors: how much of a feature is a stable property of the student across the tasks they completed? Identification rests on the 106 students whose two sessions were both eligible in page set C, so the observation-level value of each feature is decomposed into a student component and a residual that confounds the student-by-task interaction with error, each student-task cell holding one session. Fits use statsmodels REML (restricted maximum likelihood, REML) with profile-likelihood intervals, as in the criterion audit. Because a model uses a whole block rather than one feature, and because the coordinates of a sentence embedding carry no interpretation and its basis is arbitrary, the block-level quantity is also reported: the largest between-student share attainable by a linear combination, which is invariant to rotation of the feature space. Estimated in sample this is meaningless, since some direction separates the students whenever the block is wide, so it is cross-validated — the combination is estimated from one set of students and the share measured on a disjoint set — and compared with a null in which student identity is permuted within task. The cross-validated block estimates rest on 103 of these students, since a block requires a complete vector in both sessions. The embedding block is reduced by principal components to the width of the Coh-Metrix block so that the two are compared at equal dimension. This analysis is post hoc; the declaration names no such test. A post-hoc analysis assessed cross-session predictor stability for 103 students with two eligible sessions (Table S7), without prespecified testing or multiplicity correction. Consulted-page content showed no significant cross-session stability with Coh-Metrix or embeddings, unlike visit descriptors (share 0 .262, uncorrected p = 0.013), although the responsible descriptors remain unidentified. These engagement-related descriptors (Arizmendi et al., 2023; Gaˇ sevi´ c et al., 2016) suggest that browsing behavior may be more stable across tasks than consulted content.
