# The Linguistic Knowledge Benchmark: Do Language Models Know the Foundations of Early-Grade Literacy? 

Maxime Lelièvre Alasdair Mackintosh Dominic Andrews Tobias Murray Moritz Boos Oliver G. B. Garrod Fab AI 

Abstract 

Many languages of instruction in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are poorly represented in the data used to train language models. Before these models can support early-grade literacy in such languages, we need to know whether they hold the linguistic knowledge of each language that literacy teaching relies on. To test this, we present the Linguistic Knowledge Benchmark and the reproducible, largely automated pipeline that turns curriculum documents, teacher guides and pupil books into a vetted question set organised on a fixed grid of nine linguistic domains and seven grade levels. The pipeline combines LLM-driven source survey, feature consolidation, item generation and quality control with an item-selection step inspired by item response theory and a final expert review. We instantiate it for Kiswahili (Kenya; 135 items) and English (United Kingdom; 137 items) and evaluate 121 and 119 models respectively. Frontier models exceed 90% accuracy in both languages, but accuracy falls steeply for smaller and cheaper models, and more so in Kiswahili. The two best models under 10 billion parameters reach 91.7% and 73.0% in English but only 51.9% and 40.7% in Kiswahili, and the mean accuracy of models deployable on mobile and tablet hardware is 47% in English against 24% in Kiswahili. The models most practical for low-resource classrooms are therefore the least capable in the languages they would serve. We release a 20-item sample per language, all pipeline prompts, open-source code, and a public leaderboard. This is the first rung of a three-level evaluation ladder for measuring and generating decodable text to support foundational literacy. 

# 1 Introduction 

Every language is different. In Kiswahili, grammatical number (singular/plural) is often marked through changes to the start of nouns, whereas in English plurals are usually marked with changes to the end of nouns. Pronunciation, word order and tense all vary across languages. How well language models understand and generate text in a language depends in part on how much exposure, and what kind of exposure, they have had to that language. Many languages used in LMICs are poorly represented in AI training data, giving language models fewer opportunities to learn their linguistic structures and patterns. Common Crawl, a public archive of web pages widely used to train AI models, shows this imbalance: African languages account for well below one per cent of its identified content (Common Crawl, 2026). If AI is going to help solve the learning crisis, developers and users need to know whether it works in the languages used in LMIC contexts, where mother-tongue instruction is known to have large benefits for foundational literacy (Alvarez-Marinelli et al., 2025). Much of what teachers need in early-grade literacy is level-specific: practice readers, targeted support and assessment passages, each restricted to the letter–sounds already taught. Such material is called 

decodable text . Decodable material is scarce in Kiswahili and many other LMIC languages, and AI could help close that gap. 1arXiv:2611.34823v1 [cs.CL] 25 Nov 2026 Our end goal is to evaluate whether AI models can generate decodable text. A single end-to-end score would show whether models succeed, but not why they fail, and therefore not where to intervene. We therefore built a three-rung benchmark ladder that separates the capabilities decodable-text generation depends on. Linguistic Knowledge tests what a model knows about a language’s key features. Decodability Assessment tests whether a model can identify words a child cannot yet decode, given a specified instructional sequence. Decodable Text Generation tests whether a model can write text that is decodable. This paper concerns the first rung. Our contributions are as follows: • A reproducible, language-agnostic pipeline that turns linguistic knowledge gathered from primary sources into a vetted evaluation benchmark, with automated stages for source survey, consolidation, item generation and quality control, an item-selection step inspired by item response theory, and a final expert review (Section 2). • Two instantiations of the pipeline: a Kiswahili benchmark built from Kenya-focused resources (135 items) and an English benchmark built from UK-focused resources (137 items), each validated at the item-selection, expert-review and scoring levels. • An evaluation of 121 models in Kiswahili and 119 in English, showing that frontier models perform very well in both languages while the smaller models that are more practical for on-device and resource-constrained deployment struggle, with a consistently larger deficit in Kiswahili (Section 3). 

# 2 Methods 

2.1 Pipeline 

We designed the Linguistic Knowledge Benchmark as a reproducible pipeline so that it can be adapted and produced for additional languages. The pipeline turns source documents (curricula, reference grammars, teacher guides and pupil books) into a vetted question set that assesses how well a model can answer questions about language features relevant to foundational literacy. Every question sits on a fixed grid of nine linguistic domains and school grade levels (from pre-primary to Grade 5 and beyond) that stays the same across languages (Table A1). The grade tag records where the content is first introduced in the curriculum, not how hard the question is. Each item also carries two framework tags: the strand of the Simple View of Reading (SVR; Gough and Tunmer, 1986) that the tested knowledge serves (decoding or language comprehension), and the core instructional component identified by the Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel (GEEAP; Alvarez-Marinelli et al., 2025) that an error in the item would corrupt (Table A5). The methodology and linguistic domains were reviewed with sector pedagogy experts. Importantly, the benchmark targets formal linguistic competence (getting the form of a language right) rather than functional competence (using the language to accomplish goals in the world), the two being distinct cognitive skills (Mahowald et al., 2024). The two upper rungs, Decodability Assessment and Decodable Text Generation, test functional competence and are currently under development. 

2.2 Design rationale 

The pipeline was built following three guiding principles. • Mirror the expert workflow. The pipeline keeps the main stages of benchmark construc-tion separate: reviewing the source documents, inventorying testable facts, consolidating them into a unified map of the language, drafting questions against that map, quality-checking 2them, and selecting a balanced final set. Keeping each stage separate creates a traceable process, making it easier for experts to identify where quality degrades. • Use experts selectively. The pipeline automates source-document analysis, extraction, question drafting and initial quality control, and uses domain experts for the final review. This reduces the amount of expert time required per benchmark while retaining human judgement. • Reuse across languages. Every stage uses a language-agnostic prompt, so adapting the benchmark to a new language only requires swapping the source documents and revising the language-specific content of the prompts (Table A2). 

2.3 Orchestration 

The benchmark development team inputs a curated set of resources (typically 6 to 11) covering the standard early-literacy topics in the target language: curriculum documents, teacher guides and pupil-facing books. The resources used for each language are listed in Tables A6 and A7. The pipeline classifies each source into one of four tiers: curriculum, teacher material, student material, and other (e.g. a grammar book). Figure 1 summarises the end-to-end process. The pipeline then proceeds as follows. 1. Survey the resources (Prompt A1). One document at a time, the pipeline compiles a list of linguistic facts. For each fact it records a verbatim example, a document location (file, page, heading), the domain, and the grade at which the content is first introduced. Working per document means sources can be processed in parallel or added later. 2. Merge the extracted facts (Prompt A2). All facts are consolidated into a single feature map, grouped by domain and ordered by grade. Duplicates are merged into one feature listing all of its contributing sources. Where two sources disagree on a grade, the highest-tier source wins, according to a manual hierarchy given in the prompt. 3. Generate question items (Prompt A3). Items are either multiple choice or short answer, depending on the nature of the fact: contrasts and judgements are tested through multiple-choice items, while facts about producing a form use short-answer items. Using both formats lets us test unaided recall as well as the ability to distinguish correct forms from plausible, diagnostic errors. This stage is run once per subset of sources small enough to fit in the context window. It deliberately oversamples, generating several candidates per feature and making no global decisions about counts or balance. 4. Quality-check the items (Prompt A4). All candidates are pooled and each is checked against explicit anti-gaming and shape rules. For instance, the answer must not be given away in the stem, options must be length-parallel and functionally plausible, and the item must not be solvable by general reasoning alone. Duplicates are grouped and ranked rather than deleted, so nothing is discarded without a record. 5. Compile a working dataset. Items are selected using an approach inspired by item response theory (IRT; Lord, 1980), a statistical framework for judging how well each question separates stronger from weaker performers. Using the responses of a panel of 10 models spanning different providers and a wide performance range (Table A3), we select a trimmed set that (a) avoids saturation by dropping items every model gets right, (b) maximises differentiation by favouring high-discrimination items, and (c) keeps a balanced domain ×

grade spread. Items that no model answers correctly are deliberately kept and flagged for the human expert to review. A linguist or first-language speaker then reviews every question, adapting or discarding any items with remaining issues to create the final benchmark dataset. 2.4 Pipeline validation 

We validated the pipeline at two levels: the effect of item selection and the outcome of expert review. 

Item selection. For Kiswahili, item selection reduces the pool from 307 to 150 items, meeting the target of 150 set in the selection code. Of the 157 items removed, 85 were answered correctly by all ten models and therefore carry no discriminating power. The remainder were trimmed on difficulty, discrimination, and spread across linguistic domains and grade levels. For English, the same procedure reduces 377 items to 140, marginally below target, as 217 items were answered correctly by every model. Figure 2 reports domain × grade coverage for Kiswahili before and after selection, together with the proportion of items dropped within each domain and each grade. The reduction is broadly uniform: most domains and grades lose between 40% and 60% of their items, so no single stratum absorbs a disproportionate share of the cut. Two strata depart from this pattern, both of which were sparsely populated in the full bank: D7 (Pragmatics and Discourse), which loses 80% of its items, and Grade 0, which is dropped in full and is therefore not represented in the released set. Expert review. Every candidate item passing the selection step was read by a human expert before entering the final set. Reviewers worked from a fixed rubric separating two judgements per item: whether the item itself is sound (accept as is, edit, or reject when the flaw was too large to repair), and, independently, whether its metadata is correct (domain, grade, SVR strand, GEEAP component; Table A5). Kiswahili was reviewed twice, sequentially. A pedagogy expert 5reviewed all 150 candidates, dropping 12 and correcting 9. The resulting 138 items then went to an early-literacy expert and native Kiswahili speaker, who dropped a further 3 and edited 10, yielding the final 135. English was reviewed once, by a pedagogy expert and native English speaker, over 140 candidates: 3 were dropped and 7 rewritten, yielding the final 137. Taken together, the experts’ decisions to edit or drop items followed recurring patterns. Rejections traced to problems with how the items were generated, and the same problems came up in both languages. Redundancy dominated in Kiswahili, where most first-pass drops duplicated a linguistic feature covered elsewhere; answer leakage (the target answer recoverable from the stem) accounted for a smaller share. The remainder in both languages were items with more than one defensible answer, and items that measured the wrong thing (testing linguistics expertise rather than teacher knowledge, or covering content not taught in schools). Edits diverged by language. English corrections were almost entirely to metadata, most often reassigning an SVR strand or GEEAP component. Kiswahili second-pass edits mainly expanded an item’s set of accepted answers ( expected_answers , Table A5) with valid responses the generation stage had missed. 

2.5 Scoring 

We use regular-expression parsing for multiple-choice items and LLM-as-a-judge for short-answer items. The prompts used for question answering and judging are reproduced in Prompts A5 and A6. For short answers, we run three judges, one each from three different providers, and take the majority verdict, to avoid the bias and prompt-sensitivity of any single judge (Verga et al., 2024): Claude Sonnet 5 (Anthropic), GPT-5.6 Terra (OpenAI) and Gemini-3.6 Flash (Google). The judge prompt is strict: it accepts only differences of capitalisation, hyphenation and spacing, never synonyms or paraphrases (Prompt A6). Chain-of-thought blocks from a model’s reasoning are stripped from the text sent to the judge, so that a long reasoning trace cannot dwarf the actual answer. Each judge replies in a structured format with two fields: a boolean verdict and a written justification. The verdict is used directly to compute accuracy, while the justification serves two purposes. It requires the judge to ground its verdict in verifiable facts, and it makes it possible to inspect a judge’s reasoning and disagreements between judges. For example, during development we noticed that some short-answer items were missing valid answers from their 

expected_answers list (Table A5), because many models consistently answered with a valid synonym that was not listed. For multiple choice, six regular-expression patterns cover the common response formats, including answers wrapped in parentheses and answers that follow a closed reasoning block. When a model’s response matches none of the patterns, we run a fallback pass that sends only those unparseable rows to a single LLM judge, asking whether the response indicates the correct letter (Prompt A6). Any multiple-choice answer that cannot be parsed is either resolved by the fallback judge or excluded from the accuracy denominator; it is never counted as wrong. Each scored row records which method produced its verdict ( regex , llm_judge , llm_fallback or 

unscored ), so that strict-format scores remain distinguishable from rescued ones. We report accuracy for each model computed over scored rows only, with the unscored count reported alongside. 

2.6 Scoring validation 

The scoring layer was validated alongside the items, with each check summarised below. 

Judge agreement. Agreement between the three LLM judges on short-answer responses is reported as the raw pairwise rate and as Cohen’s κ, which corrects for the inflation that raw 6agreement exhibits when most responses are correct. Pairwise agreement was higher in Kiswahili (97.5–98.7%, κ 0.944–0.970) than in English (92.8–96.4%, κ 0.773–0.897), with full three-way consensus on 97.0% of judged short-answer responses in Kiswahili and 92.1% in English. The remainder were 2-of-3 splits resolved by majority vote. 

Self-preference bias. LLM judges can favour their own outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024), so we checked whether any judge favoured responses from models of its own provider. We compared each judge’s leniency toward responses from its own provider with its leniency toward other providers’ responses, where leniency denotes marking an item correct that the other two judges mark incorrect (rows where the other two disagree are excluded). We found no meaningful self-preference effect on scoring in either language. 

Fallback rate. A single LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 5) re-scored multiple-choice rows where parsing failed, using Prompt A6. Fallback was never invoked for 90% of models in English and 91% in Kiswahili. Among models that required it, the higher-scoring models showed either an increase in accuracy or no change. Claude Sonnet 4.5 went from 92.2% (95/103) to 94.2% (129/137) in English, and from 78.2% (79/101) to 81.5% (110/135) in Kiswahili; Kimi K2.5 went from 97.0% (127/131) to 97.1% (133/137) in English. Their recovered answers were correct about as often as the answers that parsed normally. The smallest models did not gain, and some lost accuracy: Qwen3-1.7B went from 53.6% (52/97) to 48.9% (67/137) in English, and Llama-3.2-1B from 27.3% (36/132) to 27.0% (37/137). Their recovered answers were mostly wrong. 

Model-family consistency. We checked consistency across six model families (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Gemma, Llama, Qwen) in both languages. Accuracy respects model size and tier overall, with only isolated inversions among same-generation, same-tier variants (e.g. within GPT and Qwen). This is consistent with the benchmark measuring a capability that scales with model size (Figures A2 and A3). 

# 3 Results 

Accuracy spans nearly the full available range in both languages, from 99% for the highest-scoring model to 10% for the lowest. Frontier models cluster above 90%, while accuracy declines steeply among mid-sized and smaller models (Figures 3 and 4). The two languages are not equivalent. English exhibits a longer plateau above 90% and a shallower tail, whereas the Kiswahili distribution begins to fall away earlier and extends lower (Figure 4). Consequently, the set of models meeting any given accuracy threshold is substantially smaller in Kiswahili than in English. Figures 3 and 4 show the subset of models pre-selected for the public leaderboard; accuracies for all evaluated models are reported in Tables A8 and A9. These results have a direct consequence for deployment. The models that perform strongly on the benchmark are the largest and most costly to serve, whereas the affordable, offline-capable models (those that could plausibly operate in a low-resource classroom) are the least able to hold the linguistic knowledge a foundational-literacy application would depend on. At or below $0.10 per million tokens (assuming a 3:1 ratio of input to output tokens), the best model available at the time of writing is Qwen3.7 Flash, which reaches 96.4% in English but 69.6% in Kiswahili. Among models small enough to run on a phone or tablet (below 10 billion parameters), the two best are Qwen3.5 9B and Gemma-4 E2B, at 91.7% and 73.0% in English but 51.9% and 40.7% in Kiswahili. We grouped models by hardware tier so that accuracy could be compared across the classes of device available in LMIC classrooms, where memory and connectivity determine which models can be run locally. Each model is assigned the smallest tier on which it could run as the primary foreground workload. Closed-weight models are placed in the cloud tier first, since their weights cannot be obtained at any size. The remainder are assigned on total parameter count against a per-tier memory budget, defined as the device’s total RAM multiplied by the share a model can claim, at the precision assumed for that tier (4-bit on mobile and tablet, 8-bit at workstation class). The full methodology, assumptions and caveats are given in Appendix D. Accuracy declines monotonically as the memory budget decreases, in both languages (Fig-ure 6). Mean accuracy in the cloud tier is 91% for English and 79% for Kiswahili, falling to 61% and 41% at workstation class and to 47% and 24% on mobile and tablet hardware. The Kiswahili deficit persists at every tier and widens steadily as hardware shrinks, from 12 percentage points in the cloud tier to 20 at workstation class and 23 on mobile and tablet. The models most likely to be deployed in a low-resource classroom are thus the least capable in the language they would be deployed to serve. The workstation tier rests on 4 models and the mobile and tablet tier on 11, however, so their means should be read as indicative. We did not run a formal human baseline, but our discussions with experts suggest that a trained Kiswahili lower-primary teacher would score roughly 80–90% on these items. # 4 Limitations 

We identify three limitations of the present study, concerning the medium of the items, the scope of the construct, and the inference conditions. 

Medium of the items. Questions are written in English and ask about the target language, which isolates linguistic knowledge from reading comprehension and instruction-following in the same language. Asking about a language in English is an easier task than operating in it, so this construction is likely to overstate what a model would achieve on the same content presented in the target language, and the near-ceiling scores we observe for frontier models may partly reflect the English medium. Results should be read as an upper bound. A version with items written in the target language would plausibly lower scores across the board, and the option of translating items programmatically remains open. 

Scope of the construct. The benchmark is a retrieval test by design: it establishes whether a model holds a linguistic fact, not whether it can apply that fact in the situations a classroom tool would demand. This motivates its position as the first of three benchmarks in our suite: Linguistic Knowledge, Decodability Assessment, and Decodable Text Generation. The latter two test whether models can put that knowledge to work, by identifying words a learner cannot yet decode and by writing text within a given instructional sequence. A model can pass this benchmark while failing either. Retrieval is a necessary condition for decodable-text generation, not a sufficient one, and the near-ceiling frontier scores reported here should not be read as evidence that the end goal is met. 

Inference conditions. All models are run under a single fixed configuration, and we did not explore the decoding and reasoning parameters that would plausibly change the ranking. Sampling settings such as temperature and other decoding controls were left at their defaults, which is likely to matter most for the smaller models, where answer stability is more sensitive to 10 configuration. We inspected outputs to confirm that low scores were not artefacts of parsing or answer-extraction failure, so the gap we report is not a formatting artefact, but we cannot rule out that different decoding settings would raise smaller-model accuracy. Similarly, we did not vary reasoning effort or extended-thinking budgets for the larger models that support them, so their reported accuracy reflects one operating point rather than the frontier of what they can achieve. The reported figures should therefore be read as accuracy under one specific configuration, not as an upper bound on model capability. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced the Linguistic Knowledge Benchmark, the first rung of a three-level evaluation ladder for decodable-text generation, and a reproducible pipeline that builds such a benchmark from a language’s own curriculum and teaching materials with expert effort concentrated on the final review. Applied to Kiswahili and English, the benchmark separates models across nearly the full accuracy range. Frontier models answer almost all items correctly in both languages, but the cheaper and smaller models that could realistically run in low-resource classrooms lose much more ground in Kiswahili than in English, and this gap widens as hardware shrinks. For developers, the practical implication is that model choice for LMIC literacy tools cannot be made from English results alone. For the field, the results show where intervention is needed: smaller, deployable models need better coverage of the languages they would serve. Because the benchmark is a retrieval test posed in English, it is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for producing decodable text. Next steps are versions with items written in the target language, extension of the pipeline to further LMIC languages, and the two upper rungs of the ladder, Decodability Assessment and Decodable Text Generation, which test whether models can apply this knowledge.

28 D Hardware Tier Assignment 

We grouped the models by the smallest class of device on which they can run locally, so that accuracy can be compared within each deployment class. For each model, we use the total number of parameters as a proxy for the memory needed at inference. We did not run any model on the devices described here, so all the requirements given below are estimates. The tiers describe deployability at an acceptable quality level, not only whether the weights can fit in memory. Two assumptions limit this further. First, we assume that the model is the main task running in the foreground and that other applications are closed; the tiers therefore do not describe normal use with several applications open at the same time. Second, the tier does not correspond to the configuration we benchmarked: all evaluations were run at the best precision available for each model, while the tiers assume a quantised deployment. 

How requirements were estimated. The assignment uses three gates, applied in order. The first gate is deployment access . We resolve it before any memory calculation, because whether a user can obtain the weights is a different question from whether their hardware can hold them. Closed-weight models are therefore assigned to the cloud tier whatever their size, because they are served only through their vendor’s API. The second gate is data sufficiency : open-weight models whose parameter count is not published are excluded from the comparison rather than assigned to a default tier. The third gate is hardware capacity , described below. We estimate the memory required as the total number of parameters, multiplied by the number of bytes per parameter at the precision assumed for that tier, plus 25%. This allowance covers runtime overhead, activations, and a key-value cache for a short or medium context. The key-value cache grows with context length and the number of layers, not with the number of parameters, so the allowance is tight for the smallest models and generous for the largest. The memory available to the model is only a fraction of the device’s total RAM, and this fraction differs by tier, depending on whether memory is shared with an operating system or dedicated to an accelerator. On mobile and tablet hardware we allow the model 50% of the total, which is a maximum for a well-behaved application rather than a typical value. At workstation level the model is placed in the GPU’s dedicated memory, so we allow 85%. Precision is defined per tier. On mobile and tablet we assume 4 bits, because memory is limited on these devices and 4-bit quantisation is the usual way of deploying a model on them. On a workstation, memory is no longer the limiting factor and precision becomes a real choice, so we assume 8 bits and treat this as a minimum quality level. In the cloud, memory is less of a constraint, so precision never changes the tier a model is assigned to. Storage is not used in the assignment. With each tier’s quantisation, the model’s size on disk stays well below the free space available on that class of device: 1.6 GB at the top of the mobile tier, 4.8 GB at the top of the tablet tier, and 21.8 GB at the top of the workstation tier. 

Tier boundaries. We define four tiers, set out in Table A10. The RAM values are device totals as they appear on a specification sheet: 4 GB corresponds to an entry-level Android phone, 12 GB to a premium phone or a tablet, and 32 GB to the memory of a high-end consumer graphics card. The usable share is the part of this total the model can use once the operating system and other processes have taken what they need, and the model budget is the product of the two. The maximum number of parameters is the largest model this budget can hold at the tier’s precision, after applying the 25% allowance. Since memory is the only real constraint, the assignment reduces to a threshold on parameter count. In Figure 6, the mobile and tablet tiers are presented together.
