## Abstract 

LLM-based interactive learner simulators are increasingly used in educational applications, including teacher training and tutoring system development. A criti-cal requirement for those simulations is cognitive fidelity: the simulated learner should maintain the assigned knowledge, misconceptions, and reasoning proce-dures throughout interaction. However, LLMs may express knowledge or problem-solving skills beyond the assigned learner’s cognitive status, a phenomenon we refer to as competence leakage. In this exploratory study, we examine 3,339 sim-ulated learner utterances from 130 teacher–learner simulator transcripts across three learning scenarios and identify over-competence as a prominent direction of cognitive-profile misalignment during interaction. We then investigate the recur-rence of previously observed competence leakage cases under four matched-trigger evaluation protocols, ranging from isolated questioning to multi-turn interactions, across five model configurations. Overall, we observe higher violation recurrence under multi-turn protocols than under isolated question answering. More capable models generally show lower recurrence, whereas increased reasoning effort does not consistently improve cognitive fidelity and is even associated with slightly higher recurrence under multi-turn protocols in our matched-model comparison. These findings provide exploratory evidence of competence leakage phenomena in interactive learner simulation and suggest that single-turn question-answering type of evaluations alone may be insufficient, motivating the development of more systematic and reliable multi-turn protocols for evaluating cognitive fidelity in learner simulation. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate learners in educational applications, including teacher training, tutoring-system development, and synthetic data generation [Mikeska et al., 2025b, Wu et al., 2025, Yuan et al., 2026]. These applications often require simulators to represent learners with specific knowledge profiles, including their knowledge, misconceptions, and reasoning processes. A central requirement is therefore cognitive fidelity : the extent to which simulated learners behave consistently with their assigned knowledge profiles throughout interactions. However, general-purpose LLMs are trained to provide helpful and accurate responses and possess knowledge and reasoning capabilities that often exceed those of the learners they simulate. This creates a potential tension when simulating novice learners with limited knowledge, naive reasoning

Consequently, LLMs may express knowledge or reasoning that is misaligned with their assigned knowledge profiles during interaction, a phenomenon we refer to as competence leakage , compromising cognitive fidelity even when their response is factually correct. This challenge relates to broader research on role-playing and instruction following, particularly whether LLMs can respect predefined knowledge and capability boundaries [Lu et al., 2025]. Prior studies of learner simulation have identified a related competence paradox and examined whether simulated misconceptions persist or change appropriately in response to feedback [Do et al., 2026, Yuan et al., 2026]. However, important questions remain regarding the prevalence and manifestations of competence leakage, the interactional conditions that may trigger it, and its associations with model capabilities and reasoning. In this study, we investigate competence leakage through exploratory failure analysis and case-based recurrence evaluation. We first analyze 3,339 simulated learner utterances from 130 human teacher– simulated learner interaction transcripts across three elementary-learning scenarios to examine patterns of cognitive-profile misalignment and the prevalence of competence leakage. We then reconstruct observed competence leakage cases under four matched-trigger evaluation protocols, spanning from single-turn questioning to multi-turn interactions, across five model configurations. Specifically, we address three research questions: RQ1: How does cognitive-profile misalignment manifest, and to what extent does over-competence predominate? RQ2: How does competence leakage recurrence vary across interaction contexts? RQ3: How does recurrence vary across model capabilities and reasoning configurations? Together, these analyses represent an initial step toward systematically understanding competence leakage, shedding light on how its recurrence differs across interaction settings and model configurations while underscoring the need for more comprehensive evaluations of cognitive fidelity in multi-turn interactions. 

## 2 Method 

2.1 Data and Learner Simulation Tasks 

Our data are drawn from LLM-based learner simulation systems developed to support pre-service and in-service teachers in practicing instructional skills [Mikeska et al., 2025b,a]. Each simulated learner is assigned an explicit knowledge profile specifying their prior knowledge, reasoning processes, and misconceptions or beliefs to maintain throughout interactions. 2We examine three elementary-learning scenarios: Grade 1 mathematics (Strategies for Adding), Grade 3 mathematics (Subtraction with Regrouping), and Grade 5 science (Mystery Substance). These scenarios require simulators to maintain distinct cognitive constraints, ranging from naive arithmetic procedures to misconceptions about scientific reasoning. Figure 1 summarizes the task contexts and example knowledge profiles, with full specifications provided in Appendix A. The original simulations were powered by GPT-4o for Strategies for Adding and GPT-5.3-chat for the other two scenarios. Our dataset comprises 130 human teacher–simulated learner interaction transcripts containing 3,339 simulated learner utterances, selected from the full system evaluation dataset based on the presence of human-annotated cognitive-profile violations. Each transcript contains at least one human-identified violation of the assigned knowledge profile. Additional details on the original knowledge profile alignment annotation procedure and inter-rater agreement are provided by [Mikeska et al., 2025b], and descriptive statistics are reported in Appendix B. 

2.2 Characterizing Cognitive-Fidelity Violation 

To characterize how simulated learners deviate from their assigned knowledge profiles (RQ1), we classify the human-identified violations according to the direction and nature of misalignment. We distinguish between over-competence , in which a simulator exhibits knowledge or reasoning beyond its assigned profile, and under-competence , in which it fails to demonstrate knowledge or abilities specified in the profile. Within over-competence, we further distinguish two forms of competence leakage: (1) Knowledge leakage , where the simulator demonstrates knowledge or problem-solving procedures beyond those permitted by its profile; and (2) Metacognition leakage , where the simulator exhibits inappropriate metacognitive awareness or knowledge updating, such as recognizing an assigned misconception or learning from an interaction despite being required to maintain its original cognitive state. Violations not captured by these categories are classified as other. Detailed definitions and illustrative examples are provided in Appendix C. We use four LLM judges (Claude Sonnet-4.6, GPT-5, GPT-5.5, and DeepSeek V4-Pro) to classify the violations. Each judge receives the assigned knowledge profile, the conversation transcript, and the classification codebook. Multiple labels are permitted when a violation response exhibits more than one pattern. At least three of the four judges agree in 78% of cases, and a human audit of 30 cases drawn from this subset yields 93% agreement with the majority LLM classification. We then retain the majority classifications for cases with at least three agreeing judges and manually classify the remaining 22% of cases. The classification rubric and judge prompts are provided in Appendix E. 

2.3 Competence Leakage Recurrence Evaluation 

To investigate the recurrence of competence leakage and its relationship with conversational con-text and model capabilities, we construct a matched-trigger evaluation using previously observed over-competence violations. Specifically, we examine whether situations that previously elicited competence leakage continue to produce cognitive-profile violations under different interaction conditions (RQ2), and how recurrence varies across models and reasoning configurations (RQ3). 

Case Construction. We select previously observed over-competence cases from the Subtraction with Regrouping and Mystery Substance scenarios, both originally simulated using GPT-5.3-chat to minimize differences attributable to the source model. For each case, we extract the assigned knowledge profile, the conversation history preceding the violation, and the teacher utterance imme-diately preceding the violating response, referred to as the “target trigger”. Each case is reconstructed under four interaction protocols and evaluated across five model configurations, enabling matched comparisons across conversational contexts and model capabilities. 

Interaction Protocols. To examine how interaction dynamics are associated with competence leakage recurrence (RQ2), we design four evaluation protocols for response generation in Figure 2 and more details in Appendix D. • P1: Single Question. Only the target trigger is presented to the candidate model. ## 3 Results 

Cognitive-Profile Violations Are Predominantly Toward Over-Competence. (RQ1) Across all three learner simulation scenarios, over-competence emerges as the predominant form of cognitive-profile misalignment (Figure 3), with knowledge leakage occurring more frequently than metacog-nition leakage. Specifically, knowledge leakage, characterized by simulators bypassing assigned misconceptions or naive reasoning procedures, accounts for 36.5%, 66.9%, and 55.4% of violations in Strategies for Adding, Subtraction with Regrouping, and Mystery Substance, respectively. In contrast, metacognition leakage accounts for only 7.7%, 12.4%, and 10.7% of violations across the corresponding scenarios.

More Capable Models Generally Show Lower Competence Leakage Recurrence, but Higher Reasoning Effort Does Not (RQ3) Overall, more capable models tended to exhibit lower com-petence leakage recurrence rates across evaluation protocols. To isolate the role of reasoning effort, we compared GPT-5.5 with and without high reasoning effort. Under the single-question evaluation protocol (P1), the high-reasoning setting exhibited slightly lower recurrence rates in both scenarios. However, this pattern reversed under multi-turn interaction evaluation protocols (P2–P4), where the high-reasoning setting is associated with higher recurrence rates than its non-reasoning counterpart. Our findings show that greater model capability is generally associated with lower competence leakage recurrence, whereas increased reasoning effort does not consistently improve cognitive fidelity. 

## 4 Discussion and Conclusion 

Recent studies have demonstrated the promise of LLM-based student simulation through carefully designed prompts, explicit behavioral constraints, and model fine-tuning [Duan et al., 2026a, Fer-nandez et al., 2026, Scarlatos et al., 2025, 2026, Yang et al., 2026]. Building on this progress, our study takes an exploratory failure-analysis perspective to investigate competence leakage as a remaining challenge to maintaining learner-specific cognitive constraints. From a cognitive science perspective, human learners’ behaviors emerge from information processing and reasoning grounded in their existing, often incomplete knowledge representations, schemas, and mental models [Chi, 2008, Vosniadou, 1994]. In contrast, LLM-based learner simulation relies on profile-conditioned role-playing by models with knowledge and reasoning capabilities beyond those of the target learn-ers. Achieving cognitive fidelity therefore requires an understanding of how a learner’s existing knowledge structures, misconceptions, and reasoning processes give rise to characteristic behavioral 5responses at a particular stage of learning. Our findings highlight competence leakage as a notable form of cognitive-profile misalignment, with identified violations predominantly skewing toward over-competence rather than under-competence, consistent with concerns about the competence paradox [Yuan et al., 2026] and over-knowledge in student simulation [Fukuhara and Domingue, 2026]. We also observed that knowledge leakage was more frequently identified than metacognitive leakage across all three scenarios. However, because we did not systematically examine or control for the distribution of teacher questions eliciting cognitive versus metacognitive behaviors, this difference should not be interpreted as evidence of their relative difficulty in maintaining fidelity. Nevertheless, understanding the distinct challenges of maintaining knowledge and metacognitive fidelity represents a valuable direction for future investigation. Our recurrence evaluation further examines the recurrence of previously identified competence leakage cases across interaction protocols and model configurations. We observed that these cases generally exhibited higher violation recurrence under protocols incorporating conversational history than under isolated questioning. One possible explanation is that multi-turn conversation introduces competing demands between maintaining assigned misconceptions and responding to teachers’ ques-tions, consistent with prior observations of misconception instability during instructional interactions [Do et al., 2026]. Meanwhile, more capable models generally showed better cognitive-profile adher-ence, whereas increased reasoning effort did not consistently reduce violations, particularly under multi-turn protocols. These observations are consistent with prior concerns about the relationship between general reasoning capabilities and learner-specific cognitive constraints [Yuan et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2026]. While our exploratory analysis does not establish causal relationships, these patterns motivate further investigation into how interaction conditions, model capabilities, and reasoning effort relate to competence leakage and cognitive fidelity. Together, these findings suggest several directions for advancing the evaluation of competence leakage and cognitive fidelity in interactive learner simulation. First, single-turn evaluation may be insufficient for assessing cognitive fidelity, particularly in interactive simulation contexts. Second, our findings motivate the development of reliable protocols for systematic evaluation of cognitive fidelity across interaction contexts, learner profiles, and model configurations. Finally, while our study focuses on adherence to fixed cognitive status interactive simulation, extending cognitive fidelity evaluation to dynamic learning contexts could introduce additional challenges, as simulated learners must appropriately maintain and update their cognitive states in response to instruction. Recent studies have begun examining selective misconception updating, guidance responsiveness, and history-aware student simulation [Do et al., 2026, Yang et al., 2026, Duan et al., 2026b]. Future work could further examine how knowledge and metacognitive fidelity can be maintained as learners’ cognitive states evolve through instruction. Together, these directions may support more systematic characterization of competence leakage and the development of more cognitively faithful interactive learner simulators. 

## Ethical Considerations 

This study involves secondary analysis of teacher–AI simulation transcripts collected as part of prior research conducted at Educational Testing Service (ETS) [Mikeska et al., 2025a,b]. The original studies were reviewed and approved by ETS’s Institutional Review Board, and participating pre-service and in-service teachers provided informed consent for the research use of their interaction data. Prior to being provided for the present analysis, the transcripts had been de-identified, with each participant assigned a unique ID in place of personally identifiable information to protect participant privacy. The present study involved no additional human participant recruitment or data collection.

## D Matched-Trigger Recurrence Evaluation: Implementation Details 

For each matched-trigger case, all four protocols preserve the assigned knowledge profile and target trigger while varying how the preceding conversation history is represented or generated. P1 and P4 additionally use a fixed GPT-5 adapter with minimal reasoning. The adapter is held constant across cases and is not an evaluated candidate model. 

D.1 P1: Single-Question Evaluation 

P1 removes the original conversation history and presents only the target trigger question. When the question cannot be understood independently, a fixed GPT-5 adapter minimally reformulates it or adds the necessary context to make it self-contained, while preserving its original instructional intent. The adapter prompt is reproduced below: 

> You create the shortest natural question-only version of a teacher trigger. First, minimally decontextualize the original trigger itself. You may remove discourse markers, acknowledgements, or continuation words such as "OK", "so", "then", or "and", and minimally clarify a reference when this does not change the question's substantive meaning, pressure, difficulty, or expected answer. If that minimally edited trigger is independently natural and interpretable, return no lead-in questions. This direct-trigger condition is strongly preferred. Example: Original: "Ok. So did the weight help you eliminate the options?" Adapted: "Did the weight help you eliminate the options?" Lead-in questions: [] Only if the adapted trigger still has an unresolved reference or indispensable presupposition, work backward to the nearest natural breakpoint and select one or, only when necessary, a few nearby source teacher questions. Hard constraint: every lead-in utterance must be a question. The teacher must not supply observations, measurements, comparison results, student claims, or answers. Those facts must come from the candidate's reply. Return one JSON object only: {"start_turn": ..., "selected_source_turns": [...], "lead_in_teacher_questions": [...],

11 "adapted_trigger_question": "...", "required_trigger_prerequisites": [...], "selection_reason": "..."} 

D.2 P2: Conversation in System Prompt 

P2 serializes the complete preceding conversation history in the system message after the assigned knowledge profile. The target trigger is then presented as the user message. 

D.3 P3: Original Conversation Flow 

P3 replays the same preceding conversation history in its original turn structure as alternating user and assistant messages. The target trigger is presented as the final user message. P2 and P3 therefore preserve the same source content while varying its message-role representation. 

D.4 P4: Dynamic Regeneration 

P4 asks the candidate model to generate its own responses turn-by-turn from the start of the conversa-tion, and the same GPT-5 adapter (minimal reasoning) steers the teacher side of the dialogue so that the original script’s teacher moves remain coherent given whatever the candidate model actually said (which will diverge from the original human transcript). The adapter prompt is reproduced below: 

> You are a tightly constrained teacher-script adapter for a benchmark. When a bridging move is required, the adapter may emit a short bridge_utterance to re-anchor the reference before delivering teacher_utterance ; the default is to emit the source teacher move unchanged ( bridge_utterance : null ).

There are two judging tasks: taxonomy labeling of already identified source violations, and perfor-mance evaluation of newly generated target responses.
