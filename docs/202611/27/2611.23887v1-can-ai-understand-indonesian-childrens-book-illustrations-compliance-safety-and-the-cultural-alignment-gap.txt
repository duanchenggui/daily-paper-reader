## Abstract 

Children’s book illustrations carry developmental, peda-gogical, and cultural responsibilities that are often for-mally codified in guidelines. As AI systems begin enter-ing children’s publishing pipelines, both as evaluators and as generators, a natural question arises: can they reliably meet the structural, safety, and cultural standards required? We investigate this across three criteria: guideline com-pliance, safety, and cultural alignment, using Indonesia’s Book Leveling Guideline as a government-issued evalua-tion framework with explicit visual criteria for four reading levels. To understand how well VLMs can identify and ap-ply these guideline criteria, we first evaluate them on real published children’s book pages and compare their assess-ments against a human baseline. To then examine whether AI-generated illustrations meet the same standards, we ap-ply the VLM judge to images produced by two generation models and cross-check them against a subset of human il-lustrator raters. We find that VLMs are reliable on explicit criteria such as compliance and safety, but fall short on cul-tural alignment, systematically missing content that human raters flag as culturally inappropriate. AI-generated illus-trations show the same blind spot: they satisfy structural and safety criteria but fail to capture implicit cultural de-tails that human raters consistently identify. 

## 1. Introduction 

Children’s book illustrations are not merely decorative. They carry developmental, pedagogical, and cultural re-sponsibilities: shaping early literacy, representing the child’s world, and reflecting the social norms of the commu-nities that produce them [8, 14]. In many countries, these responsibilities are formally codified. At the same time, AI systems are disrupting creative in-dustry workflows, including the children’s book creation process. Images generated by text-to-image models [3, 5, 9] are beginning to appear in illustrated books. Recent work has documented this shift at the family level: Jin and Yuan [7] found that children aged 4–8 rarely noticed or cared whether story images were AI-generated or human-created, and that parents generally accepted AI-generated illustrations provided they met perceived quality thresh-olds around visual consistency and emotional clarity. Yet that same study found safety to be a binary, non-negotiable threshold for parents, with content that fails it rejected out-right, regardless of other qualities. As AI-generated illustra-tions enter publishing pipelines for children, whether they meet domain-specific quality and safety standards is there-fore not just an academic question. This question is not triv-ial to answer, as most evaluations of text-to-image models rely on generic perceptual metrics, such as FID or CLIP-Score [6] or aesthetics and plausibility [10]. We use Indonesian children’s books as a case study for two reasons. First, the Book Leveling Guideline provides a concrete, written evaluation anchor. Its structural rules, cov-1arXiv:2611.23887v1 [cs.CV] 18 Nov 2026 ering illustration dominance to typography usage per read-ing level, are explicit and verifiable. This makes it possible to measure how well VLMs and generative models comply with the guideline without relying solely on subjective im-pressions. Second, Indonesia’s rich and distinct culture [1] introduces a genuine challenge for children’s book illustra-tion. As we can see in Figure 1, which shows two illustra-tions side by side: the left illustration was created by a hu-man, and the right was generated by AI. While, at a glance, the AI illustration quality is on par, the AI missed a small cultural detail: it is not common to use showers in Indone-sia. Capturing cultural specificity is a challenging task in AI research. Prior works have shown that systems trained predominantly on Western-sourced data underrepresent the cultural knowledge of other regions [2, 11, 12]. Cultural content rules are not written in the guidelines. What an In-donesian home, child, or outfit should look like exists as implicit knowledge, known by illustrators from their back-ground and training, and for illustrations targeting Indone-sian children, getting this right is far from trivial. This motivates two research questions. RQ1: How well do vision-language models (VLMs) align with human ex-pert raters when evaluating real, published Indonesian chil-dren’s book illustrations against the formal guideline crite-ria? RQ2: Where do text-to-image models comply with, and where do they diverge from, those criteria, particularly on dimensions the guideline leaves implicit, such as cul-tural representation and safety? To answer these questions, we conduct a two-task study. In Task 1, we measure VLM– human alignment on published book pages rated against the Guideline. In Task 2, we extend this evaluation to illustra-tions produced by two text-to-image models, using VLM scores cross-checked against human illustrator ratings on a subset. Both tasks share a common three-pillar frame-work: guideline compliance, cultural alignment, and safety, ordered from most explicit to most implicit in the guideline. 

## 2. Study Design

Indonesia’s Book Leveling Guideline [4] classifies books by reading level from emergent to advanced. 2.3. Task 1: Alignment on Published Pages 

Task 1 measures how well VLMs align with human raters when evaluating real, published Indonesian children’s book pages against the guidelines. We sample 100 pages from the dataset (25 per level, stratified across titles) and evalu-ate them with three VLMs: Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Commercial LLM), Qwen3-VL 8B [13] (open-weight general model), and SEA-LION-v4-8B-VL [11] (open-weight Qwen fine-tuned on South East Asian cultural data). Each model re-ceives the page image, the page text, and an instruction re-questing level prediction, guideline feature extraction, cul-tural alignment score, a safety flag, and overall age appro-priateness, with the full guideline appended as reference. For the human baseline, three professional annotators in-dependently evaluated a random sample of 20 pages drawn from the 100-page pool (5 per level), rating level classifica-tion, cultural alignment, and safety. 

2.4. Task 2: Image Generation and Evaluation 

Task 2 extends the measurement to AI-generated illustra-tions. We select 8 books (2 per level) and generate 3 pages per book using two text-to-image models: Imagen 4 [5] and 2FLUX.1 [dev] [9], yielding 24 images per generator (48 to-tal). Generation prompts are constructed using a round-trip strategy: Claude Sonnet 4.6 first produces a structured vi-sual description by reading the original published page im-age; then, the page text and level illustration requirements are combined with explicit guidance on the Indonesian cul-tural context to form the final prompt. This approach sepa-rates language understanding from image generation, pro-ducing richer, more guideline-aware prompts than direct page-to-image generation. The 48 generated images are then evaluated by Claude Sonnet 4.6 as a judge using a similar Task 1-structured prompt, enabling direct comparison of VLM scores across real and AI-generated pages. To cross-check VLM scores on a subset, three professional illustrators evaluated 12 gen-erated page-pairs from level A (2 books, 3 pages each, both generators). Raters scored each image on four criteria (vi-sual guidelines, cultural elements, age appropriateness, and logical coherence) on a 1–3 scale. 

## 3. Results and Discussion 

3.1. Task 1: Alignment on Published Pages 

Book level prediction. Addressing RQ1, Figure 3 shows confusion matrices for the N=20 human-annotated subset. Qwen and SEA-LION effectively collapse the four-level scale to two classes, correctly separating B1 while folding levels A and B2 into it. SEA-LION, a fine-tuned variant of Qwen with South East Asian cultural data, reproduces this failure identically, suggesting the fine-tuning did not intro-duce new visual discrimination capacity. Claude identifies level A more reliably but still collapses B2 into B1. Human raters perfectly separate A and B3 but show notable B1/B2 confusion, indicating this boundary is genuinely ambigu-ous even to experts. On off-by-one (OB1) accuracy, where a prediction is correct if it falls within one level of the true label, all VLMs approach human performance; the gap is concentrated in exact level separation, particularly at B2. 

Cultural Alignment and Safety. Table 2 shows results on the N=20 human subset. Safety false positives are zero for all VLMs. Cultural alignment averages are close across annotators, but the top-rating distribution reveals a system-atic divergence: human raters assign the highest cultural rat-ing in 23.3% of cases; Claude in 15.0%, and both Qwen and SEA-LION in 0.0%. VLMs can detect cultural absence but fail to recognize cultural richness , even when human raters converge on clear cultural presence. On the full N=100 evaluation, Claude flagged one safety concern (shown in Figure 4), depicting an adult aggressively knocking on a window toward a frightened child, which could be distressing for young readers. Although this is a false positive on a published book, it demonstrates that Claude has safety sensitivity.

Across all 48 gen-erated pages, VLM scores are consistently higher than hu-man scores on the overlapping Level A subset, but the rel-ative model rankings largely agree: Imagen scores above FLUX on visual guidelines (2.54 vs. 2.33) and logical co-herence (2.46 vs. 2.38), and both score equally on age-appropriateness (2.79), all matching human raters. The one exception is cultural elements, where the VLM ranks FLUX above Imagen (2.25 vs. 2.08) while humans rate it the op-posite way, repeating the same cultural gap observed in Task 1 where the VLM incorrectly rates cultural elements as present when human raters, who carry implicit cultural knowledge, judge them as lacking. 

3.3. Qualitative Limits: Expressiveness, Gesture, and the Cultural Gap 

Expressiveness and gesture as an unwritten criterion. 

The open-ended feedback from human illustrators in Task 2 reveals a dimension that neither the guideline nor the struc-tured rubric captures: expressive coherence. FLUX pages were repeatedly flagged for emotionally unconvincing char-acters, such as ”strange child emotions” or ”not engaging through gesture”, concerns that are orthogonal to guide-line compliance, cultural elements, or age-appropriateness as defined. Anatomy failures (hands, feet, limb count) ap-peared across both generators, as shown in Figure 5. These concerns point to another evaluation dimension beyond the current framework: whether illustrated characters feel emo-tionally and physically plausible. 

Cultural absence is detectable; cultural richness is not.

Aclear example is the shower scene (Figure 1): both models depict a bathroom with a shower fixture, while in Indone-sian homes bathing typically uses a dipper from a standing tub. A contrasting case appears in Figure 6, where Imagen 4 places batik patterns on the mat, clothing, and toys all at once with nearly identical repetitions. While batik is a genuine Indonesian cultural marker, applying it uniformly across every surface in a scene is not how it appears in everyday life. One annotator flagged this as visually un-realistic, pointing to a cultural overemphasis in which the model adds recognizable cultural signals without the im-plicit knowledge of how and where they actually appear. This reveals an asymmetric structure: cultural absence is reliably detectable, while recognizing cultural richness re-quires implicit knowledge that current models do not have.

## 4. Conclusion 

Both VLM evaluators and generative models handle ex-plicit, written rules well: level-prediction approaches match human OB1 accuracy, and safety flags align with human judgment. Implicit cultural knowledge is a different prob-lem. VLMs can detect cultural absence but fail to recognize cultural richness, and neither Imagen 4 nor FLUX.1 [dev] meets the implicit cultural expectations a professional illus-trator would apply. 4References
