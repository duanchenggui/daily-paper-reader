setting and learning goals (left), and GELLI generates situated scenes with a character to converse with, supported by translations,

Learning a second language requires extensive practice across situations personal to each learner, yet the most effective existing
approaches — narrative situations designed around specific learning goals and delivered through media — demand substantial authoring
effort, limiting practice to the scope of what was built. We present GELLI, a system that generates narrative practice worlds from a
learner’s goals: situated scenes with characters to converse with, support alongside the interaction, and reflection, all produced at
runtime with no pre authored content. In a within-subjects study with 40 learners of Japanese, GELLI produced greater vocabulary recall
and confidence than expert-authored videos targeting the same goals, with no significant difference in speaking performance. Learners
turned remembered or imagined situations into experiences they could shape and learn through. Support made experimentation feel
safe, while unexpected narrative developments and language suggestions gave them new possibilities to pursue beyond what they
initially intended to practice.

Introduction
Building proficiency in a second language can take hundreds to thousands of hours of sustained practice in a variety of
situations [30, 57]. Practice grounded in lived experience is especially valuable, as personally meaningful activity in
contextually-aligned language-learning environments supports comprehension, transfer, and long-term participation [41,
45]. However, relevant real life situations, in addition to often being unavailable, can also be too chaotic and intimidating
for much learning progress [26, 28]. Thus, a core challenge for language learning technology is to deliver experiences
that feel situated in relevant social and physical contexts while also providing the support, structure, and accessibility
that authentic situations often cannot.
Prior work on creating this type of practice has largely focused on designing narrative situations around specific
learning goals and delivering them through media [10, 14, 35, 50]. Narrative provides context that makes actions
meaningful and helps retention [7, 33, 52], the media establishes a sense of place and social presence that deepens
engagement [8, 21, 46], and alignment with learning goals ensures that the experience is not only engaging, but also
pedagogically productive [14, 50]. However, creating these experiences requires substantial authoring effort, limiting
practice to the predetermined situations and learning goals of the author’s design [10, 24, 35]. What remains missing in
previous work is a way to provide such practice for whatever goals a learner brings.
Apart from the effort of producing the representational media, much of the authoring burden in narrative-centered
learning comes from the top-down coordination of plot with pedagogy. Every scene must be scripted to advance both
the story and the learning goals, and the learner’s actions must be anticipated in advance [35, 50]. Emergent narrative [2]
offers a way around this constraint, through narrative designed to arise from the learner’s interactions rather than from
a scripted plot. But without a plot to organize the experience, something else must provide the necessary pedagogical
structure. Experiential learning theory [27] suggests what that structure should be: a recurring cycle of experimentation,
reflection, and abstraction around instances of concrete experience. We envision a system that generates narrative
situations to provide this concrete experience, constructs them around successive subsets of the learner’s goals, and
supports the learner through the full cycle without preauthored content.
Realizing this vision involves overcoming significant technical and design challenges. Narrative must coherently
emerge without authored plot, engagement must arise without authored environments, and pedagogical structure must
be achieved without constraining the learner’s actions. To navigate these challenges, we design and develop GELLI: a
Generator for Experiential Language Learning Interactions. Given a set of learning goals, GELLI selects a subset for
each scene and generates a situation that makes the communicative actions those goals require natural and necessary,
featuring a character for the learner to converse with. To provide immersion, GELLI generates 2D scene and character
imagery, pairs them with voiced narration and dialogue, and delivers them at room scale through a mixed reality
headset. Support windows help the learner act without interrupting the roleplay and provide corrective feedback. After
each scene concludes, the system prompts the learner to reflect on what happened and generates feedback grounded
in their reflection and the conversation transcript, then uses remaining goals to produce the next scene. In this way,
GELLI situates the learner in a generated narrative world that both responds to their actions and shapes what they
practice, structured around the experiential learning cycle.
To examine whether runtime-generated practice could support learning despite risks of incoherence, limited engage-
ment, and goal misalignment, we evaluated GELLI with 43 learners of Japanese, including a controlled within-subjects
comparison with 40 participants. We compared GELLI with expert-authored conversation practice videos targeting the

same learning goals. These videos provided a reference for pedagogical alignment: every target goal appeared in context
through voiced characters, illustrated scenes, and bilingual subtitles, delivered on the same mixed reality headset.
Learners recalled significantly more target vocabulary and reported significantly greater confidence after GELLI
than after the videos. Speaking performance improved in both conditions, with no significant between-condition
difference. Coding of session logs showed that generated scenes created opportunities for most assigned goals and that
learners attempted most available opportunities. Participants also reported significantly greater narrative presence,
social presence, spatial presence, and emotional engagement than with the videos. Their accounts linked immersion to
believable interaction, imagination, and consistency among generated elements and their expectations. Learners valued
authoring situations for preparation and play and described the unfolding narrative as something they helped create,
while identifying moments when steering toward learning goals interfered with the direction they wanted to pursue.
We contribute:
• (1) empirical evidence that runtime-generated narrative scenes can support learning and create opportunities
for targeted language practice while preserving conversational agency;
• (2) an account of how learners experience narrative immersion, authoring, and steering in generated worlds
without a pre-authored plot;
• (3) design implications for generated practice covering steering toward learning goals, consistency across
representations, and support for authoring and exploration.

Related Work
Below, we survey contributions from adjacent fields that ground our interdisciplinary approach.
2.1
Interactive, Adaptive, and AI-Driven Learning Systems
Intelligent tutoring systems demonstrate that learner modeling, adaptive sequencing, and timely feedback improve
learning [37, 59]. Large language models have since expanded what can be generated for learners. Generative systems
can now produce educational content in an unprecedented scope, from stories that embed target vocabulary [44]
to practice tasks and environments generated procedurally [48, 61]. In language learning specifically, LLM-based
roleplay tools such as Speak, ELSA AI, and TalkPal have made interactive conversational practice widely accessible
and increasingly incorporate scenario drafting, task tracking, and post-conversation feedback [18, 55, 56]. Research
systems like ChitterChatter align AI-generated conversation to instructor-defined curriculum goals [19], while Rehearsal
demonstrates targeted practice with feedback for conflict resolution scenarios [53]. However, work on AI roleplay
for language learning also surfaces persistent challenges: LLM dialog can drift from objectives, break character, or
fail to create opportunities for target forms [15, 20]. Most of these systems still treat the interaction primarily as a
single conversation with an agent, without the cognitive and emotional benefits of narrative design, or the reliable
pedagogical alignment that comes from designing scenarios around specific learning goals.
2.2
Narrative-based Learning Systems
Narrative-based learning uses storytelling structures to provide context for action and meaning. Stories provide schemas,
scripts, and causal links that aid comprehension and retention [7, 52, 58], while narrative transportation increases
engagement and supports attitude change [9, 22].

Interactive narrative environments organize learning tasks within storyworlds. Systems such as Crystal Island tie
learning supports to plot progression [49], while larger worlds like Quest Atlantis use story goals and consequential
choices to structure inquiry [4]. A key challenge in these systems is maintaining pedagogical alignment when learners
deviate from the intended path. Research in interactive digital narrative addresses this issue through drama management
and narrative planning [47], in which an experience manager coordinates narrative goals with pedagogical goals to
produce a coherent experience that is both engaging and educationally productive. Recent work such as Oak Story [14]
extends this approach by using LLMs to give learners greater agency within the narrative while still driving them
toward pre-authored narrative events and learning activities. Still, these approaches manage pre-authored assets and
organize scripted or pre-determined narrative events that the learner must progress through.
Emergent narrative approaches offer an alternative to plot-driven coordination. Rather than scripting narratives
top down, these systems allow stories to arise from autonomous character interaction, as demonstrated in educational
drama systems like FearNot! [3]. This approach trades in the coherence guaranties of scripted plots for greater learner
agency and spontaneity, a tradeoff we revisit in Section 3.
GELLI builds on this tradition but operates under a different constraint: rather than coordinating pre-authored
narrative and pedagogical events, it must generate both at runtime from the learner’s goals. This shifts the design
challenge from narrative management of authored content to narrative generation of goal- conditioned content, which
we address through emergent narrative and experiential learning theory.
2.3
Immersive Learning Environments
VR and XR research has shown that immersive environments can enhance educational experiences through presence
and embodiment, as well as support situated learning [16, 29, 32]. Foundational research shows that people apply social
and physical rules to digital media as if they were real, treating mediated interactions as genuine encounters [46]. This
helps explain why simulated experiences can evoke emotions and learning outcomes similar to lived events.
Within language learning, XR-assisted practice is associated with improved oral proficiency, greater motivation,
and lower anxiety [5, 12, 13, 29, 62]. However, the landscape is narrower than appears. Review articles in the literature
note that few XR language learning applications explore the real potential of VR for novel, immersive, or experiential
learning approaches [5, 21]. Most operate as highly immersive flashcard systems [34] or present fixed tourist-like
scenarios with limited scope and interactivity [21].
Research on ‘presence’ further complicates the picture. Although stronger feelings of presence often enhance
engagement, the evidence that full 3D immersion improves learning outcomes over lighter alternatives is mixed [23, 38].
Factors such as modality, previous experience, and cognitive load introduce nuance, and richer environments can
decrease learning when the medium overwhelms the message [1, 39].
3
Design Tensions and Goals
In many authored approaches, experts embed their pedagogical and narrative expertise into a unified design: they craft
narrative that organizes and contextualizes interaction, develop media that helps learners feel present in an imagined
social situation, and script dialogue aligned with specific learning goals. This process produces effective practice, but
it is complex and costly, limiting practice to the scope of what was built and to the interactions anticipated by the
designer [35]. To bring this kind of practice into line with whatever goals the learner brings, with the learner free to
say and do what they choose, the narrative, media, dialog, and pedagogical structure must all be generated at runtime.
This demand introduces design tensions to be resolved: how to organize narrative, achieve presence, and structure

learning when each must be generated rather than designed by hand. In the following, we discuss these tensions and
the theoretical lenses we used to navigate them, deriving design goals that guided our implementation decisions.
To ground these decisions in domain expertise and learner experience, we iteratively prototyped with two Japanese
language instructors and eight learners of Japanese. Three principles from this process informed our design: support
should preserve productive struggle, support should remain accessible without interrupting the roleplay, and learners
need control over goal specification and pacing.
3.1
Generating Narrative without Authored Plot
3.1.1
Tension. In authored narrative-centered learning environments, plot serves as both the narrative and pedagogical
backbone: it determines what the learner encounters, in what order, and with what dramatic arc [35, 50]. The coordination
of plot with pedagogy is what makes these systems effective, but it is also what makes each scenario expensive to
author and rigid to adapt.
3.1.2
Theoretical lens. Emergent narrative [2] points to an alternative: rather than scripting a plot, the system could
generate social situations and let narrative arise from the learner’s interactions with characters who behave according
to their roles, removing the need to author every path and giving the learner genuine conversational agency. However,
without a plot organizing the experience toward specific objectives, the interaction could be engaging but pedagogically
aimless. In a dynamically generated narrative, learning goals could serve as the organizing unit in place of plot, with
the scene as the atomic unit of both narrative and pedagogy.
DG1: Generate narrative scenes around subsets of learning goals. A scene should be a single situated interaction
between the learner and a generated character, organized around a subset of the learner’s goals that cohere within one
social encounter. Within the scene, the character should stay in role and create opportunities for the learner to practice
target forms, while responding to unexpected input as someone in that social role would, rather than breaking character
to enforce topic adherence. Pedagogical relevance should be maintained through the social logic of the situation, not
through scripted constraints on what the learner can say.
DG2: Produce narrative continuity from goal progression and conversation history. Successive scenes should
be shaped by the remaining goals and the consequences of what has already occurred, producing narrative continuity
without authored plot structure. For example, a learner whose goals include checking for items at a lost-and-found
venue and taking the train to a destination might first find themselves at the lost-and-found, explaining to an attendant
that they lost their wallet. In the next scene, the system might generate a situation where they must ask a stranger to
borrow money for a new transit card (narrative consequence), in order to take the train (remaining learning goal).
3.2
Representing the Narrative World
3.2.1
Tension. Authored approaches achieve a sense of presence and engagement through a wide range of media, and
the evidence on which medium best supports learning is mixed [23, 38, 39]. For runtime generation, we face three key
constraints that shape our selection of representational strategy. First, generating richer assets increases latency, cost,
and the risk of visual incoherence. Second, richer environments can be distracting and increase cognitive load without
improving learning. Third, fully-rendered environments reduce narrative flexibility since every narrated event must be
consistent with what is shown, constraining narrative to existing visuals or requiring mid-conversation regeneration.

Lighter representations leave room for narration and imagination to fill the gaps, making the generated narrative more
resilient to conversational contingencies.
3.2.2
Theoretical lens. Research on narratives in extended reality distinguishes between system immersion, the
technological envelopment of the user’s senses, and narrative immersion, the psychological engagement produced by
imagery, characters, and storytelling [8]. A sense of place and social connection can be evoked largely independently
of environmental fidelity [8, 51], as spatial immersion applies across narrative media and emotional engagement is
driven by characters and events rather than explorable environments. This motivates a representational strategy in
which imagery, narration, and responsive characters may be sufficient to produce presence and engagement for runtime
generation.
DG3: Target spatial and emotional immersion through generated imagery, narration, and voice. Representa-
tion should maximize narrative flexibility while minimizing latency. In addition, it should establish a sense of place
through visual context, a sense of social presence through a visible voiced character, and bridge representational gaps
through narration.
3.3
Structuring Learning Under Learner Agency
3.3.1
Tension. A third tension arises from the need to structure learning within an interaction in which the learner has
full conversational agency. In authored approaches, designers can calibrate exactly when and how to introduce support,
feedback, and reflection because they know what the learner will encounter. Under full generativity, the system cannot
predict what will happen, so pedagogical structure must emerge from the interaction itself.
3.3.2
Theoretical lens. Experiential learning theory [27] provides the framework we use to organize the explicit
pedagogical structure on top of the situational context. Kolb describes learning as a recurring cycle of concrete
experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. We treat each generated
scene as a concrete experience and organize support around the remaining phases of the cycle.
DG4: Align support with experiential learning cycle. In-scene support should facilitate active experimentation
and abstract conceptualization during the concrete experience, while between scene reflection should facilitate reflective
observation and connect the learner’s experience to broader principles.
DG5: Preserve learner agency and productive struggle. The learner should control what they say, when they
speak, and when they move on. Support should preserve learner agency in producing their own responses. Support
should not interrupt the roleplay. We derived this goal in part from our iterative design process with teachers and
learners, described above.

GELLI: A Generator for Experiential Language Learning Interactions
GELLI implements the design goals described above through a coordinated multi-module architecture (see Figure 2 for
the interface and Figure 3 for the system data flow). The learner specifies a setting and learning goals, then enters a
series of interactive scenes where they converse with a generated character, receive off stage support, and complete a
reflection activity after each scene. In the following, we describe each stage of the interaction and the architecture that
coordinates them, as well as the design goal (DG) of Section 3 which informed its design.

4.1
Goal Specification
The session begins with a menu where the learner specifies what to practice. Separate input fields accept a setting
description, task goals, grammar goals, and vocabulary goals. The learner can fill in any subset of these and have the
system generate the rest: a learner who specifies only a setting receives AI-generated tasks, grammar, and vocabulary
aligned to that context. Learners can also generate goals first and have the system suggest a setting. Once at least
one learning goal is present, the learner generates a preview describing their role and the scenario, regenerating until
satisfied before beginning. This design allows learners who know exactly what they want to practice to specify it
precisely, while learners who are uncertain can start from a setting and let the system suggest what to work on.
4.2
Scene Generation and Interaction
A narrative module selects a subset of learning goals for each scene, choosing goals that cohere within a single social
encounter (DG1). Given the setting, atmosphere, character description, and remaining goals, it generates a scene
description, a character with a role appropriate to the situation, and the scene’s specific goal set. The prompt instructs
the module to choose goals “that the user can achieve by speaking or listening to the character in the scene” and to
scope each scene to what can be achieved in one static setting. The module also maintains a record of the remaining
goals, removing the goals assigned to each scene when that scene is generated.
A scene module then generates a landscape-orientation background image and a foreground character image from
the same detailed scene description, both displayed at room scale in the learner’s physical space through a mixed reality
headset (DG3). A narrated scene description is read aloud, orienting the learner to the situation, the character, and the
objectives of the scene (DG3).
The learner converses with the character using push-to-talk speech input (DG5), then their speech is transcribed and
added to a message log. A dialog module generates the character’s response and accompanying narration based on the
full message log, the current scene’s goals, and the scene description. The character’s prompt instructs it to stay in
role, keep the scene’s learning goals in mind, create opportunities for the learner to practice them, and to respond with
in-role confusion when the learner makes errors, triggering the support system rather than coaching directly (DG5).
Narration describes the character’s behavior in third person and in English, bridging what the learner sees with what

is happening and helping the learner follow the social dynamics even when they cannot fully parse the character’s
Japanese dialog.
When the conversation has reached a natural close, the dialog agent sends a system message indicating the conversa-
tion has ended, but the learner may decide whether to continue the conversation or proceed to reflection (DG5).
4.3
Support Windows
To ensure that learners have the guidance and structure necessary to learn from the experience, GELLI provides support
information in panels positioned adjacent to the character image, accessible without leaving the roleplay (DG5). GELLI
generates the following support from the most recent messages:
Dialogue transcription. The full conversation between the character and the learner. A small replay button
accompanies any Japanese text so that the learner can hear it again.
Learning goals. The list of learning goals assigned to the current scene. This list is updated as the learner exercises
them, helping the learner to stay oriented to what they are practicing.
Translations. Key words and phrases from the character’s most recent dialog, translated for the learner with
furigana for kanji.
Response suggestions. Vocabulary and short phrases that the learner could use in responding to the character.
These are never full sentences, preserving the need for the learner to construct their own utterances (DG5).
Real-time corrective feedback. When the learner’s response does not make sense in context or shows misunder-
standing, the character responds with in- role confusion, asking them to repeat or clarify. In this case, the response
suggestions are replaced by corrective feedback that tells the learner what they may have done wrong, provides a model
correction, and offers a metalinguistic explanation of the underlying rule.
These supports are generated by a dedicated support agent that monitors the conversation independently of the
dialog agent. It has access to the full message log, the scene’s learning goals, and the overall session goals, allowing it to
tailor support to both the immediate conversational context and the broader learning objectives.
In addition to addressing learner needs, these supports were designed to facilitate two phases of the experiential
learning cycle (DG4). Translations and response suggestions give learners a concrete alternative to try on their next
turn, creating immediate opportunities for active experimentation (AE). When real time feedback addresses a mistake
by explaining a general rule and how or when to use it, it supplies an opportunity for abstract conceptualization (AC),
and the interface then invites an immediate retry, linking AC and AE in the same exchange. In cases where feedback is
corrective without a stated rule, it still advances AE by indicating a specific fix to attempt on the spot.
4.4
Reflection and Feedback
After each scene, the learner answers a brief spoken prompt: “Describe one thing you feel went well and one thing you
struggled with or would like to improve on.” Their response is transcribed.
This reflection is designed to facilitate Reflective Observation (RO) by asking the learner to articulate successes and
difficulties in relation to the just completed interaction (DG4). Using that self report together with the scene transcript
and learning goals, a reflection module then generates feedback in three parts:

setting, atmosphere, and character descriptions. Its prompt instructs it to “maintain narrative coherence” by taking this
information into account when selecting the next subset of goals and generating the next scene description (DG2). As
each scene is generated, the list of remaining goals is updated, and this loop continues until all learning goals have
been covered or the learner ends the session.
4.5
Additional Implementation Details
GELLI coordinates these functions through a multi-agent architecture where separate agents handle narrative, dialog,
support, goal tracking, and reflection, each grounded in the same scene context and learning goals but only accessing
information necessary for its role (e.g. the dialog agent does not see support messages). Cross-scene coherence is
maintained by passing the previous scene’s conversation history, situation, and character details to the narrative agent
when generating each successive scene. The system is built in SwiftUI on Apple Vision Pro using OpenAI models (o3
for text generation, dall-e-3 for imagery, gpt-4o-mini-tts for voice and whisper-1 for transcription), delivering generated

in our evaluation.
5
Study Design
The goal of GELLI is to produce practice that is reliably aligned with learning goals, situated, and engaging — properties
that authored approaches achieve through extensive design — while allowing learners to practice whatever goals they
choose with full agency and no pre-authored content. Our approach is to generate such practice at runtime, structured
around the experiential learning cycle. This ‘high wire’ approach introduces significant risks: generated scenes and
imagery may be incoherent or distracting, the lightweight representation may not produce the sense of place and social
presence it is designed for, the conversation may drift from learning goals, and the generated support and feedback
may not be pedagogically adequate.
To evaluate the degree to which GELLI achieves its goal despite these risks, we compare it with expert-authored
conversation practice videos1 that aim the same learning goals in a within-subjects study. The videos represent the
pattern described in Section 2: narrative situations designed around specific learning goals, provisioned through
media. We selected conversation practice videos because these were cited by Japanese conversation class instructors as
representative of what they recommend to their students for out-of-class practice. An author with instructional design
expertise and Japanese fluency analyzed each video and cross referenced its content with a Japanese language textbook
to derive a precise set of tasks, grammar patterns, and vocabulary. Then these learning goals were provided as input to
GELLI. During the video condition, every participant saw identical, carefully sequenced practice; during the GELLI
condition, every participant experienced a unique generated scenario. No two GELLI sessions were identical.
We investigate the following research questions:
• RQ1: Did they learn? Given the agency to say anything and the openness of LLM generation, do learners still
learn from generated roleplay practice compared to expert-authored practice targeting the same goals?
• RQ2: Did they feel immersed in a narrative world? Can narrative generated on the fly produce narrative
immersion without plot? What forms does it take, what does it depend on, and what did the learners say it did
for them?
• RQ3: How did they experience the co-authoring of the narrative with the AI? How do learners engage
with narrative authoring, steering, and continuity, jointly shaped by them and AI?
5.1
Participants
We recruited 47 participants through flyers, mailing lists, and word of mouth. Eligibility requirements included being at
least 18 years old, the ability to come to our institution in person, at least one year of university-level Japanese language
study or equivalent, and the ability to read text comfortably at a medium distance without glasses (as the Apple Vision
Pro is not compatible with glasses).
Two participants were pilots and are excluded from analysis. Three did not complete the full study due to time
constraints, and two have missing data due to collection errors. Three of these five were able to use the tool and provide
feedback, yielding 𝑁= 43 for qualitative analysis and 𝑁= 40 for quantitative analysis. The study was approved by our
institution’s Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent before participating.

5.2
Conditions
Our study had three conditions provided by the same mixed reality headset:
Conversation practice videos (control). Participants watched expert-curated conversation practice videos. To make
the experience more similar to a roleplay, participants were instructed to shadow one speaker’s lines by repeating after
the speaker and to pause the video after questions and produce their own answer first. Participants were welcome to
repeat and review any parts of the video.
GELLI preset (treatment). The study participants used GELLI with a predetermined set of learning objectives for
each context, derived from the conversation practice videos described above. Although the inputs were always the
same for each context, GELLI generated a new set of scenes, characters, and dialog every time it was used. Participants
were asked to complete the roleplay of all scenes generated by GELLI.
GELLI create your own (CYO). Participants created custom scenarios, specifying their own setting, tasks, grammar,
and vocabulary or having GELLI generate them. They practiced their custom scenario with the option to stop early at
any point.
5.3
Procedure
Because each participant underwent both controlled conditions, we prepared two contexts: Cafe and Shopping. Partici-
pants were assigned to one of four counterbalanced groups determining which condition they experienced first and
which context was used for each condition. Both conditions were delivered with the same Apple Vision Pro headset.
Each participant completed the following sequence: pre-test and pre-survey for the first context, a headset tutorial,
the first intervention (control or treatment depending on group), post-test and post-survey, an optional break, then
pre-test and pre-survey for the second context, the second intervention, and post-test and post-survey. After both
controlled conditions, participants created and practiced their custom scenario (CYO), completed a post-survey, and
participated in a semi structured interview. The sessions lasted two to three hours. The participants were compensated
with a $75 USD gift card.
5.4
Measures
We measured learning outcomes, confidence, narrative engagement, presence, sense of agency, and support quality
using the following instruments.
Knowledge test. Participants completed pre- and post-intervention knowledge tests with two versions (Cafe and
Shopping contexts), each consisting of a vocabulary quiz and a speaking test. The vocabulary quiz asked participants to
recall five Japanese words given English meanings (scored 0-2 per item, range [0,10]). The speaking test used structured
roleplay in which participants heard Japanese dialog, read English response instructions, and responded aloud in
Japanese until completion. A native Japanese-speaking researcher with second language acquisition experience wrote
the roleplay scripts using the learning goals for each context, avoiding exposure to either condition to prevent bias. The
speaking test assessed task completion and language use for four goals (scored 0-2 per goal, range [0,8]). Both tests
were graded by two Japanese-speaking researchers blind to participant identity, condition, and timing, with excellent
inter-rater reliability: ICC(2,k) = 0.990 for vocabulary and ICC(2,k) = 0.971 for speaking.

Confidence. Immediately after each knowledge test, participants rated on five-point Likert scales how confident
they felt about their ability to converse in Japanese in the corresponding context and whether the tool used in that
condition could help them prepare.
Narrative engagement. We adapted 11 items from the Narrative Engagement Scale [9] to assess four subscales:
narrative understanding, attentional focus, narrative presence, and emotional engagement. Each item was rated on a
five point Likert scale. 2
Presence. We included items on social presence and spatial presence from the New Presence Scale [60]. Each item
was rated on a five point Likert scale.
Support evaluation. Two Japanese-speaking researchers coded all real-time corrective feedback and post-reflection
lesson feedback from system logs, assessing whether feedback was warranted, whether it correctly identified errors,
whether it modeled the correct answer, and whether metalinguistic explanations were appropriate.
Log coding. Two researchers coded the session logs of all 43 participants (232 scenes, 841 assigned goals). For each
goal assigned to a scene, the authors coded whether the scene created an opportunity to practice it and whether the
learner made a recognizable attempt, recording reasons for missed opportunities. For each of the 148 scene transitions
within a session, they coded whether the new scene meaningfully continued the previous one, was compatible but
stand-alone, broke continuity, or was unclear, recording break types where applicable. Disagreements were reconciled
to consensus.
Interview. A semi-structured interview at the end of the study asked about the general experience of the participants,
comparisons between conditions, and impressions of GELLI’s coherence, support, and potential for regular use. The
interviews were transcribed and analyzed using thematic analysis [6]. Two researchers developed an initial codebook,
calibrated in five interviews, and then divided the remainder.

6.1.3
Support windows let learners explore and discover language, and attempt more than they would by

Support provided security. Learners described feeling anxious or uncertain during conversations, but that having
support available helped them continue. P012 explained, “having the transcripts and suggestions gave me something to
lean on, so I didn’t just freeze. It made me feel more reassured and confident.” The way feedback was delivered also

was confused.” P036 described this behavior as making them feel like “the system was helping [them] figure it out.”

leaving the learners to construct their own responses, consistent with the design choice to offer phrases rather than full
responses (DG5). P020 shared that “this type of learning, I think it kind of encourages you to come up with the whole
sentences yourself, and then it provides me with some of the useful phrases that I might need.” Having several options
helped learners consider different ways to express themselves. P029 valued the notes because “It gives you so many
different options to choose from.” P017 connected the suggested vocabulary to wanting to “express myself with the
exact words I wanted,” describing the experience as “a lot of freedom.”
Suggestions prompted discovery and experimentation with new language. Many participants valued how suggestions
surfaced unfamiliar language they could try on the spot. P026 described “getting exposure to like new vocabulary

completely unfamiliar things in there. So, at first it was frustrating and confusing to see it, but once I actually said it like
in practice or listened to someone else saying it, it was pretty helpful.” P035 described the same friction: “Sometimes
it responded with vocabulary I didn’t know, like I just wanted basic words, but it used advanced terms. That was
frustrating, but also kind of fun.” Opportunities for reflection extended this experimentation beyond the immediate

6.1.4
Generated feedback was mostly warranted and reliably modeled corrections. We evaluated whether
generated feedback addressed actual errors and provided useful explanations, and whether post-scene lessons were

6.1.5
The amount of support could initially feel overwhelming. The amount of available language could make
it difficult to decide what to use. P026 described “choice paralysis” from the vocabulary appearing during interaction.

scrolled through tabs without retaining anything, but “once I realized I could build up gradually, in the second and third
scenarios I started picking vocab and phrases, combining them, and making progress.” They described the information
becoming “useful, instead of just noise.”

Discussion
Our findings suggest that GELLI’s language practice generated at runtime can support learning and narrative immersion
while giving learners a meaningful role in authoring and steering their experience. We discuss these findings in relation
to learning goals and narrative agency, immersion in generated worlds, scaffolding, and learner authoring, then draw
design implications for generated practice.
7.1
Learning Goals, Narrative Coherence, and Learner Agency
Interactive narrative learning environments must manage learner agency while guiding them toward learning goals
and keeping the narrative coherent [14, 47]. Our findings show how this coordination can work at runtime and where
it can break down.
7.1.1
Providing targeted practice while preserving learner agency. Expert-authored practice can guarantee that the
activities learners complete are aligned with their learning goals, whereas high-agency learning environments risk
leaving learners without enough structure to make progress toward them [14]. Open-ended LLM dialogue can drift
from intended tasks or fail to elicit target language forms [20, 42]. Our coding suggests that focused narrative scenes
with adaptive support provided opportunities for most goals without constraining what learners said (Section 6.1.2).
Uptake was highest for communicative tasks, lower for vocabulary, and lowest for grammar, suggesting that scenes
could prompt communicative acts without necessarily eliciting the specific language targeted for practice. Learners’
exploration of unfamiliar forms encountered in support, or alternative directions for the narrative, may have contributed
to this pattern (Sections 6.1.3 and 6.3.2).
Learners nevertheless recalled more target vocabulary and reported greater confidence than after expert-authored
practice, while speaking performance improved in both conditions with no significant between-condition difference
(Section 6.1.1). Exposure to target words in character dialogue and support windows may have contributed to the
vocabulary advantage even when learners did not produce those words; our coding did not assess this possibility, which
warrants further study. Together, our findings suggest that targeted practice need not come at the cost of agency and
interactivity.
7.1.2
Keeping generated narrative coherent while steering toward learning goals. Prior work on experience management
addresses the tension between a coherent narrative and a participant’s ability to change its direction [47]. In a learning
environment, the system must also steer that narrative toward learning goals. Our approach of generating successive
scenes around preceding events and remaining goals often produced connected encounters, including developments
learners had not anticipated but found believable (Section 6.3.3).
Within scenes, however, a helpful, believable character response could advance the encounter while removing
the learner’s reason to perform the intended communicative act, for example by volunteering information they had
intended to request. Conversely, steering the conversation toward unfinished goals could interfere with the learner’s


20
Symonette et al.
direction and make the exchange feel less natural (Section 6.3.2). Preserving practice opportunities therefore requires
coordinating what makes sense for the narrative with what gives the learner a reason to use the language. Resolving
this tension may require better predictions of what the learner intends and how the narrative might unfold, and room
to move goals to later scenes rather than forcing them into the current one.
7.2
Immersion in Generated Narrative Worlds
Bruni et al. distinguish system immersion, produced by the technology, from narrative immersion, produced by imagery,
characters, and storytelling, and argue that the second can be evoked largely independently of environmental fidelity
[8]. Our findings suggest that learners experienced narrative immersion without an authored plot or an explorable
environment.
7.2.1
Narrative immersion with lightweight representations. Presence research finds that perceived realism depends
on whether an environment meets the user’s expectations, not only on how it looks [54]. Learners reported greater
narrative presence with GELLI than with videos on the same headset (Section 6.2.1). Their accounts linked immersion
to agreement among imagery, narration, character responses, and expectations, with inconsistencies disrupting the
experience (Sections 6.2.2 and 6.2.4). These findings suggest that lightweight representations can support immersion
when what learners see, hear, and do fits together, making consistency across generated scenes a design priority.
7.2.2
Narrated non-verbal cues. Embodied conversational agents convey understanding and coordinate conversation
through gaze, gesture, and expression [11]. Studies of LLM-based embodied agents for language learning report that
these cues matter to learners [42, 43]. In our study, learners used narrated gestures and reactions to picture character
behavior and, in some cases, decide how to respond (Section 6.2.3). Our findings suggest that narration can make social
cues available for interpretation and response without requiring each behavior to be animated.
7.3
Scaffolding Without Taking Over
The involvement-load hypothesis emphasizes searching for word meanings and evaluating words in context in vocabu-
lary learning [31]. Research on computational feedback identifies a risk of relying on feedback instead of evaluating
one’s own work [17], while studies of LLM tutors report difficulties with long, generic, or excessive corrective feedback
[42, 43]. Our findings show why support must balance involvement with security. Having support available helped
learners feel confident enough to attempt unfamiliar language and more ambitious responses. Offering phrases rather
than full responses left them responsible for constructing what they said, using suggestions to express their intentions
and explore unfamiliar language and narrative directions (Section 6.1.3). Yet some learners found the options tiring or
difficult to choose among (Section 6.1.5). This calls for support that keeps exploratory options available while allowing
learners to control how much they see.
7.4
Authoring in Emergent Narrative
Emergent narrative shifts authoring from writing a plot to defining the characters and circumstances from which
a story can develop through interaction [2, 36]. Generative learning systems have begun to give learners a hand in
this authoring [42, 44]. Learners authored scenarios for preparation and play, but sometimes struggled to specify the
situations they imagined.

7.4.1
Learners as authors of practice situations. In our study, learners often created situations they expected to face, such
as a barbecue with a Japanese community, and situations they wanted to play in, such as a fantasy world. Confidence in
conversing in their self-chosen situation rose after practice, and learners rated GELLI as likely to help them prepare
for it (Section 6.3.1). These findings concern perceived preparation; transfer to real conversations remains untested.
Learners also described the encounters as something they and the system created together (Section 6.3.2).
Research links imaginative suggestibility with presence in VR [25]. Our findings suggest that authoring may support
imaginative participation by letting learners define situations from remembered or anticipated encounters and bring
expectations against which they interpret the generated world (Sections 6.3.1 and 6.2.3). Their accounts of immersion
when characters and events fit their expectations suggest that authoring can help establish a situation they find
believable. Yet learners could struggle to convey their intended situation, as some specified too little and others created
scenarios that were too difficult for them to understand (Section 6.3.1). Authoring support should therefore help learners
see and revise how the system interprets their setup, so that the generated situation is both suitable and remains
recognizable as it develops.
7.4.2
Practice as experimentation. Experiential learning theory links experience, reflection, abstraction, and active
experimentation [27]; Morris emphasizes contextually rich, unfamiliar experiences in which learners make decisions and
examine their understanding [40]. Taken together, our findings suggest that choosing a situation, imagining themselves
within it, and having support to try unfamiliar responses made practice a way to explore possibilities, test expectations,
and discover further things they wanted to learn. Generation extended these possibilities by introducing language and
developments learners had not specified and responding to their choices. Authoring gave learners responsibility for
defining their experience, while responding to generated events required them to adapt that initial intention. Learners
could therefore experiment with what to say, which situations to enter, and how the narrative would unfold (Sections 7.3,
7.4, and 7.1). Letting learners revisit an encounter would extend this experimentation by making alternative choices
and their consequences available for comparison. Reflection and abstraction remain less clear: we could not determine
how deeply learners engaged with them (Section 8).
7.5
Design Implications for Generated Practice
We draw seven design implications from the findings above, grouped by the problem they address. Each names the
sections that motivate it.
7.5.1
Steering practice toward learning goals.
Design Implication 1: Model how narrative developments create and remove practice opportunities
(Section 7.1). Systems should combine a model of the unfolding narrative with a learner model that tracks what the
learner appears to know, what they have practiced, what remains, and what they appear to expect next. Planning should
consider several exchanges ahead and make revisions as learners act. Where a goal fits the anticipated direction, the
system can place an opportunity along that path; where it does not, it can introduce a plausible development that gives
the learner a reason to pursue it.
Design Implication 2: Let goals move beyond the scenes initially assigned to them (Section 7.1). If an
exchange removes the learner’s reason to attempt a goal, the system should retain the goal and establish an opportunity
elsewhere in the narrative. Intermediate exchanges can create new reasons to use the language, and additional scenes
or sessions provide space for them. When exploration reveals a new learning interest, the system could suggest an
additional goal while retaining unfinished ones.

7.5.2
Representing generated narrative worlds.
Design Implication 3: Design for consistency between representation, narration, and interaction before
adding fidelity (Section 7.2). Generated imagery, narration, and character behavior should agree with each other
and with the situation the learner specified. Narrated details that are visible should match the image, and details the
image cannot show, such as a character’s actions, can be carried by narration alone. Characters should keep the same
appearance across scenes, and their responses should fit their role and what the learner has said. Where fidelity and
flexibility conflict, favor a representation that generation can keep consistent as scenes change.
Design Implication 4: Use narration to make unseen actions available for participation (Section 7.2). When
deciding how to represent a character’s action, consider what the action communicates and how the learner can respond
to it. Narration can describe hesitation, acknowledgment, or activity beyond the visible scene, giving learners something
to interpret and act on. Connect those descriptions to the character’s subsequent behavior so that learners’ responses
have consequences.
7.5.3
Supporting authoring and exploration.
Design Implication 5: Offer support as a way to think through how the encounter could unfold (Sections 7.3
and 7.4). Use the conversation so far and predictions about where it could go to offer a small set of options that represent
different ways of responding and the different directions each would take the encounter. Let learners adjust how many
options they see and hide or expand them as needed, while preserving their responsibility for selecting, adapting, and
combining them into a response. Provide lookup and explanations for intentions the system has not anticipated.
Design Implication 6: Let learners revisit decisions and compare alternative continuations (Sections 7.3 and
7.4). Allow learners to return to an earlier exchange, try a different formulation or action, and see how the conversation
develops. Connect this to reflection on why language worked in that context and how another intention would call for
a different response. This lets learners examine possibilities they could not pursue in the same exchange and identify
further expressions or situations to practice.
Design Implication 7: Make the generated interpretation of the learner’s scenario visible and revisable
(Section 7.4). Build on scenario previews by helping learners specify and revise the roles, relationships, circumstances,
and intended interactions that matter to them, since a setting alone leaves these ambiguous. Allow learners to correct
the system’s interpretation before and during participation, and carry those corrections into subsequent dialogue,
imagery, and narration. Authoring support can also identify goals that need additional narrative setup and suggest how
they might fit the learner’s scenario.
8
Limitations and Future Work
8.1
Attribution
Our design bundles interactivity, generated imagery, narration, voice, support, and reflection into a single treatment,
and the expert-authored videos were chosen as a reference for pedagogical alignment rather than for interactivity.
The comparison found higher vocabulary recall and confidence after generated practice, with no significant difference
in speaking performance. It does not establish which components produced these outcomes or whether generation
would outperform an interactive authored alternative. The absence of a significant association between presence and
vocabulary recall does not establish the relative contributions of representation, support, and reflection; isolating these
contributions would require further comparisons. We also designed support to carry learners through reflection and
abstraction, but reflections had no required content and we did not measure how closely learners read the generated

lessons, so we cannot confirm that these phases occurred as intended. Our log coding was reconciled to consensus
between two researchers rather than independently rated, so we do not report inter-rater reliability.
8.2
Duration and Transfer
The controlled comparison used two contexts in a single session. We cannot claim that the learning advantages persist,
transfer to real conversations, or generalize to other contexts. The presence and engagement learners reported may
matter more for sustained practice than for immediate gains, but testing this requires a delayed post-test or a longitudinal
study. A natural extension would track learners over multiple sessions with a user model that reincorporates missed
goals, generates new situations as proficiency develops, and spaces review based on what has been practiced. Learners’
reports of preparation and transfer were self-reported, and future work should test performance in real conversations.
8.3
Scope and Generalization
Our participants were adults in one area learning Japanese, and results may differ for other populations, proficiency
levels, or languages. The architecture is largely language agnostic, but Japanese required specific handling of its writing
system, furigana, and register, and low-resource languages would need additional work. We did not test a rendered
environment, so our findings support the sufficiency of a lightweight representation rather than a comparison between
representations; whether selective use of 3D elements or gesture input adds to immersion or learning remains open.
Beyond language learning, goal-conditioned situation generation with in-scene support and post-scene reflection may
transfer to other domains of situated social interaction, such as clinical communication or conflict resolution. Domains
that depend on physical interaction or conceptual reasoning would require different representations and generation
strategies.
8.4
Technical Limitations
Generation latency, inconsistent imagery across scenes, and speech recognition errors interrupted immersion for some
learners and likely affected narrative understanding. These are limitations of the current implementation rather than
of the approach, and faster generation and consistency checks across generated components would address most of
them. We also did not measure presence and narrative immersion against each other; examining how the two relate in
generated practice is a direction for future work.
9
Conclusion
GELLI examined how interactive narratives generated at runtime could combine targeted language practice with learner
authorship and conversational agency. Generated scenes provided opportunities for most assigned goals, and the
controlled comparison found greater vocabulary recall and confidence than expert-authored videos targeting the same
goals. Speaking performance improved in both conditions, with no significant between-condition difference. These
single-session findings do not isolate individual components or establish retention or transfer.
Taken together, our findings suggest that practice can become an experience learners help create and learn through
as it unfolds. Authoring let learners draw on remembered, anticipated, and imagined situations for preparation and play.
Their accounts connected immersion to the relationship between these expectations, the generated representations, and
responsive characters. Support made unfamiliar responses feel safer to attempt while preserving learners’ responsibility
for constructing what they said. Generation extended their initial intentions with language and developments they had

not specified, making practice a way to explore possibilities, test expectations, and discover further things they wanted
to learn.
Supporting this form of practice requires coordinating these elements as the experience develops. Authoring support
must help learners convey and revise the situations they imagine; representations and character behavior must remain
consistent while allowing believable surprises; and language support must enable experimentation without taking
over. Narrative planning must also preserve reasons to use the target language, with room to relocate goals when they
conflict with the unfolding interaction. GELLI contributes a design and empirical account of how learner authorship,
generative narrative, and adaptive support can work together to make language practice both purposeful and open to
discovery.
