Abstract —Generative AI (GenAI) is shifting software engineer-ing students’ work from producing artefacts towards evaluating and supervising AI-generated outputs. However, little is known about what characteristics students identify in outputs when reporting interventions, or how they exercise oversight during authentic software engineering work. We analysed 962 student-AI interaction turns documented by 145 first-year software engi-neering students through structured AI Journals, including 172 student-declared interventions. Using a multi-dimensional coding framework, we characterised the output characteristics identified in these reported interventions, students’ reasons for intervening, the actions they took, and how those actions were enacted. Fitness problems were identified in 74.4% of interventions, compared with 16.9% involving correctness problems, indicating that over-sight more often concerned whether plausible AI outputs were suitable for the engineering task than whether they contained explicit errors. Among classifiable interventions, students most frequently modified generated outputs (51.0%) or refined their prompts (28.2%), with both response types commonly associated with engineering fit. Within-student analysis further showed that outputs perceived as containing minor flaws were associated with lower evaluation confidence than outputs perceived as perfect. Taken together, these findings characterise novice AI oversight as a process that extends beyond error detection to encompass both judgement of fitness for purpose and active intervention in AI-assisted engineering work. 

Index Terms —Generative AI, Software Engineering Education, Human Oversight, Evaluative Judgement, Human-in-the-Loop 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Generative AI (GenAI) tools have become a routine part of software engineering practice and education. Students increas-ingly use large language models (LLMs) to generate require-ments specifications, user stories, interface designs, and code artefacts as part of their coursework [1, 2, 3]. As these systems produce increasingly fluent and plausible outputs, their use can shift part of students’ work from creating engineering artefacts from scratch towards evaluating , adapting, and integrating AI-generated artefacts. This shift places students in a supervisory role, requiring them to decide whether to accept, refine, challenge, verify, or reject AI-generated outputs using their 

engineering judgement .This supervisory role is particularly challenging for novice learners. Students may struggle to critically evaluate AI-generated outputs, especially when those outputs are fluent and structurally plausible [4, 5]. This creates a risk of cognitive of-floading, whereby students rely on AI-generated outputs rather than independently interrogating them [5]. Huynh and Nguyen [6], for example, identify contrasting pathways: AI literacy can promote verification behaviour, whereas familiarity with AI and the fluency of its outputs can encourage uncritical adoption. The challenge, therefore, is not simply whether students use GenAI, but whether they can recognise when its outputs require scrutiny and exercise appropriate judgement when deciding how to respond. The educational capability underlying this supervisory role is evaluative judgement (EJ): the capacity to assess the quality of one’s own and others’ work [7, 8]. EJ develops through sustained engagement with standards, comparison, and feed-back, rather than through access to tools alone [9, 10, 11]. Bearman et al. [12] extend this framing to AI-assisted learning, arguing that the ability to judge the fitness and trustworthiness of AI-generated artefacts is becoming a central dimension of EJ. In software engineering, this form of judgement can be operationalised through activities such as validation, testing, debugging, and comparison of AI-generated artefacts against domain requirements and constraints. Human-in-the-loop (HITL) frameworks complement this educational perspective by conceptualising human judgement as part of the operational workflow of an automated system [13]. Parasuraman et al.’s Levels of Automation model [14], for example, highlights the risks to situation awareness and error detection as automation progresses from recommenda-tion to execution. In AI-assisted coursework, a comparable decision arises whenever students determine whether and how to incorporate an AI-generated artefact into their engineering work. EJ and HITL thus illuminate complementary aspects of the same supervisory process: EJ concerns the student’s capac-ity to judge the quality and fitness of an AI-generated artefact, while HITL foregrounds how that judgement is translated into actions such as accepting, modifying, verifying, or rejecting arXiv:2610.32118v1 [cs.SE] 23 Oct 2026 it. Prior process-level research has shown that students and developers engage with GenAI through prompting, inspection, verification, modification, and iteration [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. However, less is known about the engineering judgement that underpins these behaviours : what characteristics novice students identify in AI-generated artefacts when reporting interventions, why they judge those characteristics to require intervention, and how those judgements translate into par-ticular responses. This gap is especially relevant in design-first, low-code software engineering curricula, where oversight extends beyond code correctness to include judgements about requirements, system scope, stakeholder needs, contextual fit, and design decisions [20]. We address this gap through a process-level empirical analysis of student oversight in an authentic first-year software engineering course. We analyse AI Journal entries [21] submit-ted by 145 first-year students in a design-first, low-code course at the University of Sydney. The journals document students’ prompts and AI-generated responses together with their self-reported evaluations, interventions, and reflective commen-tary. We analyse student-declared interventions across four dimensions: what characteristic of the output was identified when reporting an intervention, why the student intervened, what action they took, and how that action was enacted. We additionally examine how students’ confidence in evaluating AI-generated outputs varies with their perceived correctness. 

RQ1: What characteristics of AI-generated outputs do novice students identify when reporting interven-tions? 

RQ2: How do novice software engineering students inter-vene in AI-generated outputs, and what operational mechanisms and engineering rationales characterise their interventions? 

RQ3: How does students’ confidence in evaluating AI-generated outputs vary with their perceived correct-ness of those outputs? This paper makes three contributions: 1) an empirical characterisation of the AI-output charac-teristics identified in novice students’ reported inter-ventions, distinguishing problems of correctness from problems of fitness for the intended engineering task; 2) a multi-dimensional framework linking characteristics identified in AI-generated outputs to students’ engi-neering rationales, intervention actions, and operational mechanisms, together with empirical evidence of how these dimensions relate in students’ reported oversight; and 3) implications for software engineering education con-cerning how students can be supported to evaluate the fitness of AI-generated artefacts, exercise different forms of intervention, and make their supervisory judgement visible in assessment. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Sec-tion II reviews related work on generative AI in software en-gineering education, evaluative judgement, and human-in-the-loop oversight frameworks. Section III describes the research context, dataset, the AI Journal instrument, and the coding procedures. Section IV presents the results for each research question. Section V discusses the findings and their implica-tions for software engineering education practice. Section VI addresses limitations, and Section VII concludes the paper. II. R ELATED WORK 

Empirical research has increasingly examined GenAI use through students’ and developers’ interaction processes, rather than focusing only on their attitudes towards these systems. Kazemitabaar et al. [15] studied novice learners using an LLM-based code generator, examining prompting patterns alongside how students placed, verified, and manually modi-fied generated code. They identified approaches ranging from single-prompt generation to hybrid AI-human coding. Related observational studies further demonstrate that working with code-generating systems involves ongoing human judgement. Barke et al. [16] distinguished between acceleration , in which programmers use generated code to implement an already-understood solution, and exploration , in which they use the model to investigate possible approaches. Mozannar et al. [17] developed a complementary taxonomy of programmer activities around GitHub Copilot, capturing the activities and associated costs involved in prompting, inspecting, and work-ing with code suggestions. Research with novice programmers further indicates that access to GenAI does not necessarily result in effective oversight. Prather et al. [18] found that GenAI accelerated problem-solving for some novices while compounding ex-isting metacognitive difficulties for others. Rahe and Maalej [19] similarly observed programming students using ChatGPT both to seek conceptual knowledge and generate solutions, with some entering repeated cycles of submitting generated code and asking the model to revise unsuccessful outputs. In response to these challenges, pedagogical approaches have begun to emphasise capabilities such as prompt construction, code reading, and evaluation. For example, Denny et al.’s 

Prompt Problems train students to formulate prompts that satisfy programming specifications [1]. Together, these studies show that novice interactions with GenAI in programming contexts involve iterative prompting, inspection, verification, and modification, while revealing substantial variation in how critically students engage in these activities. Much of this process-level evidence, however, concerns code-generation or programming tasks, where output qual-ity can often be evaluated against functional behaviour or predefined specifications. GenAI is also increasingly being applied to upstream software engineering activities, including requirements elicitation, specification, analysis, and validation [20]. Quality in these activities is multidimensional: an artefact may be internally plausible yet still require judgement about its completeness, consistency, scope, alignment with stakeholder needs, and fit with project constraints [20, 22]. The oversight problem therefore extends beyond determining whether gen-erated content is technically or factually correct to assessing whether it is suitable for the engineering context in which it will be used. Beyond interaction strategies, research on verification raises questions about the quality and consistency of students’ evalu-ative practices. Ardic et al. [23] observed that students working with AI on software testing tasks inspected outputs and refined their prompts following weak results, although manual valida-tion practices varied considerably across individuals and task types. Cognitive offloading, whereby AI-generated artefacts substitute for independent reasoning, has also emerged as a longitudinal concern, with students’ debugging and critical-reasoning capabilities weakening across course cycles [5]. Together, these findings indicate that opportunities to inspect or modify AI-generated outputs do not necessarily translate into effective oversight. Students’ trust in AI outputs may also be miscalibrated. Huynh and Nguyen [6] identify two partly independent path-ways shaping reliance: one driven by familiarity, perceived usefulness, and self-efficacy, which increases willingness to adopt AI, and another driven by AI literacy and habitual verification, which strengthens auditing capacity. Students may therefore be highly familiar with a tool while remaining poorly equipped to distinguish reliable from unreliable outputs. Pitts et al. [24] further show that neither self-reported trust nor expressed scepticism reliably predicts auditing behaviour in practice. Taken together with process-level studies of prompt-ing, verification, and modification, this evidence indicates that understanding responsible GenAI use requires attention to both students’ evaluative dispositions and the actions through which their evaluations are enacted. EJ provides an educational lens through which to understand this capability. EJ develops through sustained engagement with standards, comparison, and criterion-referenced feedback, rather than through access to tools alone [8, 9, 10]. In tech-nical education, EJ involves assessing work against relevant quality criteria, identifying deficiencies, and making justified decisions about its adequacy [8]. Bearman et al. [12] extend this framing to GenAI, arguing that judging the fitness and trustworthiness of AI-generated artefacts is becoming a central dimension of EJ in higher education. Crucially, EJ in AI-assisted contexts entails not only recognising explicit errors but also assessing whether an apparently plausible output is suitable for its intended context and purpose. Such judgement may not be visible in the final artefact or explicitly rewarded by existing assessment designs [12]. HITL perspectives complement EJ by locating this judge-ment within AI-mediated workflows. Rather than treating human involvement as the passive monitoring of an otherwise automated process, HITL emphasises meaningful opportuni-ties for people to evaluate, redirect, modify, override, or reject system outputs [13]. Parasuraman et al.’s Levels of Automation model provides a foundational account of how responsibility can be distributed between humans and automated systems [14]. More recent reviews of HITL in applied AI emphasise that assigning a person a nominal supervisory role does not, by itself, constitute meaningful oversight [13]. This distinction is increasingly important as AI-assisted and agentic workflows distribute software engineering activities across human and automated actors. Effective human involvement depends on whether people retain both the capacity and opportunity to exercise judgement when generated artefacts require interven-tion. Existing process-level research therefore provides substan-tial evidence about how students and programmers interact with GenAI, including how they prompt, inspect, verify, mod-ify, and iterate on generated outputs. Less well understood is the engineering judgement underlying these behaviours: which characteristics students identify in AI-generated artefacts when reporting interventions, why students judge those characteris-tics to require intervention, and how their judgements translate into particular responses. This gap is especially important beyond code-generation tasks, where an output may be tech-nically plausible yet insufficiently scoped, contextualised, or aligned with stakeholder and project requirements. We address this gap through a semester-long analysis of student-reported oversight across four related dimensions: the characteristics identified in AI-generated outputs, students’ engineering ra-tionales, their intervention actions, and the operational mech-anisms through which those actions were enacted in authentic software engineering coursework. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Research Context and the AI Journal 

This study was conducted in ELEC1005: Introduction to Software Engineering, a first-year undergraduate unit at the University of Sydney in Semester 1, 2026. The unit intro-duces core Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) concepts, including requirements engineering, stakeholder analysis, user stories, interface design, and prototyping. It follows a design-first, low-code approach in which students use tools such as Microsoft PowerApps and Figma rather than developing software primarily through conventional source-code program-ming. Because the course emphasised requirements interpre-tation, system design, prototyping, and the justification of engineering decisions rather than algorithmic implementation, students frequently used GenAI for activities where contextual suitability and system constraints were particularly salient. As part of the coursework, students completed structured AI Journals throughout the semester [21]. Journal completion was a hurdle requirement rather than a graded component, thereby reducing, but not eliminating, students’ incentive to optimise their reflective responses for marks. Each journal documented a student’s use of a GenAI tool for a coursework task and contained one or more interaction turns. For each turn, students recorded their prompt and the corresponding AI-generated response, the purpose of the interaction, their assessment of the output’s accuracy, the method used to verify the output, and their confidence in evaluating its accuracy on a five-point scale. When students assessed an output as containing M INOR FLAWS or M AJOR HALLUCINATION ERRORS , they were also required to describe any intervention they made. At the journal level, students reflected on how their interaction process evolved, critical moments in that process, perceived skill dependency, the utility of the AI, and the interaction overall. The resulting records therefore combine documented student-AI interaction turns with students’ self-reported evaluations, interventions, and reflections. 

B. Dataset and Unit of Analysis 

The dataset comprises 962 student-AI interaction turns declared by 145 students across nine weeks. Each journal entry represents one or more documented student-AI inter-actions and contains the student’s prompts, the AI-generated responses, and the associated reflective fields. For RQ1 and RQ2, the primary unit of analysis is the 

student-declared intervention . At each interaction turn, stu-dents first evaluated the correctness of the AI-generated output as Perfect , with Minor Flaws , or with Major Hallucinations .When an output was evaluated as containing minor flaws or major hallucinations, students were required to describe the human intervention they made in response and how they carried it out. These intervention declarations, together with the corresponding prompt, AI-generated response, and surrounding reflective fields, constitute the intervention dataset analysed for RQ1 and RQ2. For RQ3, we consider students’ self-reported confidence in evaluating the accuracy of AI-generated outputs, recorded on a five-point scale for each interaction turn, in relation to their perceived correctness of the corresponding output. 

C. Human Oversight Coding Framework 

We developed the coding framework iteratively from the complete set of student-declared interventions. The first author initially reviewed all intervention records to identify recur-ring characteristics in the factors that prompted students to intervene, their reasons for intervening, the actions they took, and how those actions were enacted. Based on this review, an initial set of codes, definitions, and decision rules was developed. The full intervention dataset was then reviewed a second time using this provisional framework, with the categories and their definitions refined to resolve ambiguities and improve conceptual separation. Rather than treating oversight as a binary distinction be-tween acceptance and rejection, the framework captures four complementary dimensions of the intervention process: (1) the characteristic of the AI-generated output that prompted the intervention ( Trigger , TR); (2) the student’s underlying engineering motivation for intervening ( Intent and Rationale ,IR); (3) the action taken in response ( Intervention Type , IT); and (4) the means by which the intervention was implemented (Operational Mechanism , OM). Together, these dimensions characterise an intervention in terms of what prompted it (TR), why the student acted (IR), what action they took (IT), and how that action was enacted (OM). D. Coding Procedure 

Using the framework described in Section III-C, both au-thors independently coded the intervention events and then compared their assignments. For the single-label IT dimen-sion, inter-rater agreement was 90 .6% (Cohen’s κ = 0 .865 ;Gwet’s AC1 = 0 .886 ). For the multi-label dimensions, agree-ment was assessed using exact-set agreement and Jaccard similarity. Exact-set agreement was 94 .7% for OM (mean Jaccard = 0 .947 ), 91 .3% for IR (mean Jaccard = 0 .920 ), and 

92 .1% for TR (mean Jaccard = 0 .930 ). After completing the independent coding, the authors resolved all disagreements through discussion, referring to the coding definitions and decision rules to reach consensus. All subsequent analyses were conducted using this reconciled human-coded dataset. 

E. Data Analysis 

For RQ1, we examined the prevalence of intervention trig-gers across student-declared interventions. Because multiple triggers could be identified for a single intervention, TR codes were treated as multi-label, and prevalence was calculated as the proportion of interventions in which each trigger was identified. We also examined the underlying journal records to contextualise the observed trigger patterns using representative examples. For RQ2, we first examined the distribution of primary intervention types among student-declared interventions. Cases in which the action taken could not be determined were re-tained as I NSUFFICIENT INTERVENTION DESCRIPTION (IT5) but excluded from the calculation of the relative distribution of the four substantive intervention types. An intervention could receive multiple codes within IR and OM dimensions when supported by the student’s account and surrounding interaction context. Where the available evidence was insufficient to assign any code within a dimension, no code was assigned. We then examined the rationales and operational mechanisms associated with each intervention type using pairwise co-occurrence frequencies for IT-IR and IT-OM. Because IR and OM codes were multi-label, co-occurrences were calculated independently for each dimension and normalised within in-tervention type. For RQ3, we examined the relationship between students’ perceived output correctness and their confidence in evaluating the accuracy of AI-generated outputs. A. Oversight as Judgement of Fitness 

A central finding is that novice oversight was concerned far more often with the fitness of AI-generated artefacts than with their correctness : fitness problems were identified in 74 .4% of student-declared interventions, compared with 16 .9% involv-ing correctness problems. This distinction extends the framing of AI oversight beyond error detection. In software engineer-ing, an artefact can be technically plausible yet unsuitable for its intended use because it does not adequately reflect project scope, stakeholder needs, platform constraints, or implementa-tion context. Effective oversight therefore requires students to ask not only “Is this output correct?” , but also “Is this output appropriate for what we are trying to build?” 

This finding connects EJ and HITL perspectives in AI-assisted software engineering. EJ concerns judging work against relevant standards and contextual requirements [8, 12], while HITL foregrounds the human role in deciding whether to accept, modify, or override automated output [13, 14]. Our findings suggest that developing this supervisory capability requires more than teaching students to detect hallucinations or verify factual correctness: students also need to judge whether AI-generated artefacts are sufficiently specific, appropriately scoped, and contextually suitable for the engineering task at hand. We therefore identify fitness for purpose as an important dimension of evaluative judgement in AI-assisted software engineering. 

B. Oversight as Active Engineering Agency 

The results in Section IV-B show that novice oversight was not limited to verifying AI output or repeatedly prompting the model. Rather, students exercised agency at different points in the human-AI workflow: they directly modified generated artefacts, redirected the AI through prompt refinement, sought clarification, or rejected outputs altogether. Most classifiable interventions involved either Output Modification (IT1; 51 .0% )or Prompt Refinement (IT2; 28 .2% ), both of which were most frequently associated with Engineering Fit (IR1). This pattern suggests that students were not simply deciding whether to accept an AI response; instead, they actively shaped either the generated artefact or the conditions under which the AI produced it to better satisfy engineering requirements. For software engineering education, this suggests that supervisory competence should encompass a range of intervention strate-gies, rather than treating oversight as a binary decision to accept or reject AI-generated output. 

C. Evaluative Confidence and Imperfect Outputs 

Results in Section IV-C show that students’ evaluation confi-dence was sensitive to perceived imperfections in AI-generated output. Within students, outputs perceived as containing minor flaws were associated with significantly lower evaluation con-fidence than outputs perceived as perfect, and this relationship remained robust when confidence was modelled as an ordinal measure. Students who more frequently evaluated outputs as containing minor flaws also tended to report lower evaluation confidence overall, indicating that the association was present both within and between students. These findings suggest that recognising a problem does not necessarily imply confidence in judging its extent or consequences; rather, subtle imperfec-tions may introduce evaluative uncertainty. Together with the results in Section IV-A, this finding highlights an important challenge for AI-assisted software engineering education. Most declared interventions involved fitness rather than correctness problems, while perceived minor flaws were associated with lower evaluation confidence. Su-pervisory competence therefore requires not only recognising that an AI-generated artefact may require intervention, but also developing sufficient evaluative judgement to determine why it is unsuitable and how it should be addressed. The five major-hallucination cases were too few to determine whether conspicuous failures elicit a different confidence response. In other words, the challenge is not simply noticing that something is wrong. Students may recognise that an output is imperfect while remaining uncertain about how confidently they can evaluate it. 

D. Implications for Software Engineering Education 

Our findings suggest that AI oversight should be treated as an explicit software engineering competency rather than an implicit expectation of students using GenAI. First, in-struction should extend beyond factual verification to de-velop students’ ability to judge fitness for purpose , including whether generated artefacts are appropriately scoped, suffi-ciently contextualised, and compatible with system and stake-holder constraints. Second, students should develop a range of supervisory strategies. Effective intervention may involve directly adapting an artefact, refining the conditions provided to the AI, seeking clarification to improve understanding, or rejecting an output, rather than relying predominantly on repeated prompting. Finally, assessment should make this supervisory process visible. Evaluating only the final artefact provides limited evidence of whether students critically evaluated AI-generated content or accepted it uncritically. Process-oriented activities that require students to document what prompted an interven-tion, why they intervened, and how they responded can provide evidence of the evaluative judgement underlying AI-assisted engineering work. Such evidence can support feedback and assessment focused not simply on whether students use GenAI, but on the quality of the engineering judgement they exercise when using it. VI. L IMITATIONS 

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limita-tions. First, the AI Journal captures student-declared oversight rather than independently observed behaviour [21]. Students may omit interventions, provide incomplete descriptions, or reconstruct their reasoning when documenting an interaction. Moreover, intervention reporting was required only when students evaluated an output as containing M INOR FLAWS or MAJOR HALLUCINATION ERRORS , which may have excluded adaptations to outputs rated P ERFECT , such as modifications for contextual fitness rather than correctness. The additional reporting required after a non-perfect evaluation may also have influenced response choices. Furthermore, because the AI Journal explicitly prompted students to consider verification and evaluation confidence, the observed behaviours occurred within a scaffolded reflective activity and may not reflect students’ GenAI use outside of the AI Journal activity. Con-sequently, the absence of a reported intervention cannot be interpreted as evidence that no intervention occurred. Simi-larly, the coded triggers, rationales, and actions represent only what could be established from students’ accounts and the surrounding interaction context. The presence of intervention declarations with insufficient information for substantive clas-sification (IT5) illustrates this limitation. Second, the coding framework necessarily involves inter-pretive judgement. Although coding considered the student’s prompt, the AI-generated response, intervention declaration, and reflective fields together, distinctions between categories such as engineering fit, comprehensibility, and assessment alignment were not always explicit in students’ accounts. We mitigated this threat through independent coding using explicit decision rules and assessed inter-rater reliability using Cohen’s 

κ [28] and Gwet’s AC1 [29] before resolving disagreements through discussion (Section III-D). Although the observed high agreement provides evidence that the coding framework could be applied consistently across raters, reconciliation does not eliminate the interpretive judgement involved in assigning categories to students’ self-reported accounts. Third, RQ3 relies on students’ self-reported perceptions of both output correctness and their confidence in evaluating it. Moreover, our analysis characterises intervention behaviour only among interactions that students perceived as imperfect; it may therefore not capture adaptations or other forms of over-sight applied to outputs that students initially rated as perfect. These measures consequently characterise perceived, rather than objectively established, output correctness and evaluation ability. We accounted for repeated observations within students using mixed-effects modelling, and the relationship between perceived minor flaws and evaluation confidence remained robust when the five-point confidence measure was modelled as ordinal. However, only five interactions were classified as MAJOR HALLUCINATION ERRORS . We therefore excluded these observations from inferential comparisons, and no con-clusions can be drawn about evaluation confidence under severe AI failure. Finally, the study was conducted in a single first-year, design-first, low-code software engineering course at the Uni-versity of Sydney. The prominence of fitness-oriented over-sight and output transformation may therefore partly reflect the course’s emphasis on requirements, system design, scope management, and reflective AI use. The findings should not be assumed to generalise directly to programming-intensive courses, more experienced software engineers, or professional software development settings. Nevertheless, the semester-long coursework setting provides evidence of oversight within authentic educational activities rather than isolated experimen-tal tasks. VII. C ONCLUSION 

As GenAI increasingly participates in the production of software engineering artefacts, students must learn not only to use these systems but also to exercise effective oversight of their outputs. This study examined how novice software engineering students enacted such oversight during authentic, semester-long coursework. Our findings show that oversight extends substantially be-yond detecting AI errors. Fitness problems were identified considerably more often than correctness problems in students’ reported interventions. They exercised oversight by modifying generated artefacts, refining prompts, seeking clarification, or rejecting outputs. Students’ evaluation confidence was also lower when they perceived outputs as containing minor flaws, highlighting the challenge of evaluating imperfect but plausi-ble AI-generated artefacts. Taken together, the findings show that novice oversight involves more than identifying errors in AI-generated outputs. Students also judged whether outputs were suitable for their engineering context and responded by modifying the artefact, redirecting the AI, seeking clarification, or rejecting the output. Understanding oversight in AI-assisted software engineering therefore requires attention not only to whether students recog-nise when an AI output is incorrect, but also to how they judge its fitness for purpose and respond when it does not meet the needs of the engineering task. Future work should triangulate reflective journal accounts with direct interaction traces and examine whether the distinc-tion between correctness- and fitness-oriented oversight per-sists across programming-intensive courses and professional software engineering contexts.
