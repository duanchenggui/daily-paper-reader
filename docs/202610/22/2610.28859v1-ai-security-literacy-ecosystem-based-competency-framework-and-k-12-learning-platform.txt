Abstract 

Adoption of artificial intelligence in K–12 schools has outpaced guidance for students, teach-ers, and administrators. Existing AI-literacy frameworks emphasize understanding and use; cybersecurity frameworks predate AI-specific threats, and neither defines AI security literacy 

as a competency domain in its own right. We propose an AI Security Literacy (AISL) frame-work of six dimensions spanning the school ecosystem, ground its technical dimensions in public AI-safety benchmarks and a reproducible multi-seed adversarial evaluation, and ground its atti-tudinal dimensions in nationally representative survey micro-data from U.S. teachers and adults. Only 34.5% of teachers report a school AI policy, and teachers see their own AI use as easing their work but students’ use as complicating it; on the technical side, a reported robustness figure depends as much on the attack and the particular trained model as on the system evalu-ated. NeuralFortify, a browser-based K–12 platform built on real AI models, is presented as an illustrative operationalization. 

Keywords: AI literacy; AI security; K–12; cybersecurity education; adversarial machine learning; framework development; educational technology. 

# 1 Introduction 

Black-box artificial intelligence is now ubiquitous in students’ lives, and adoption inside schools has been correspondingly rapid. The resulting problem is not solely a technical one about model robustness. It is also a human one: students are frequently unsure of what they are permitted to do, teachers are divided between embracing AI and fearing it, and many schools operate without policy or training to guide either group (Doss et al., 2025). The central claim of this paper is that AI security literacy is best treated as a distinct, teachable construct located on the risk, trust, and security axis, and that its constituent phenomena can be empirically grounded with data available today. We advance it as a proposition supported by evidence rather than as a validated finding; discriminant validation against adjacent literacies is future work. Existing AI-literacy frameworks were designed to help learners understand and use AI systems; existing cybersecurity frameworks were written before AI-specific threats existed. Neither treats operating securely in an AI-mediated environment as a competency domain with its own dimensions and developmental progression.

# 2 Background and Related Work 

This section reviews the three traditions the framework draws on: K–12 AI-literacy frameworks, adversarial machine learning, and cybersecurity education. It then summarizes the survey evidence on AI adoption in schools that motivates the work. Long and Magerko’s competency model (Long and Magerko, 2020) and the AI4K12 Five Big Ideas (Touretzky et al., 2019) establish the vocabulary for K–12 AI literacy, and subsequent work extends it through a conceptual review of AI literacy (Ng et al., 2021), middle-school curricula (Lee et al., 2021), and UNESCO’s competency framework for students (Miao and Shiohira, 2024). Across this body of work, security appears at most under societal impact or ethics and is never an operationalized domain with its own progression (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025). Work on adversarial machine learning in K–12 settings is even thinner. Most classroom AI education centers on using or building classifiers. The closest adversarial effort teaches learners to break a model through spoofing and explainable-AI inspection (Hilke et al., 2025), which is valuable but stops short of the defend-and-evaluate half of the arc. Rigorous adversarial evaluation, meanwhile, exists almost entirely at the expert level (Croce et al., 2021; Athalye et al., 2018; Tram` er et al., 2020), and its vocabulary of attacks and mitigations has been standardized for practitioners rather than learners (Vassilev et al., 2025). The threats that such evaluation addresses are no longer confined to laboratories. Language models can reproduce memorized training data verbatim, exposing what a system retains (Carlini et al., 2021); applications built on them can be redirected by instructions hidden in content they retrieve (Greshake et al., 2023); and their fluent output can be confidently false (Ji et al., 2023). Each of these reaches ordinary users of AI systems directly, and each later anchors a dimension of the framework proposed here. Cybersecurity education frameworks form the third relevant tradition. GenCyber concepts and the NICE Framework (Petersen et al., 2020) predate AI-specific threats and have remained largely siloed from AI-literacy work. The empirical phenomenon motivating this work is documented in nationally representative sur-vey evidence: adoption outrunning guidance, a policy vacuum, teacher apprehension, and student confusion about permission (Doss et al., 2025; Diliberti et al., 2025; Kaufman et al., 2025; Laird et al., 2025; College Board, 2025; Digital Education Council, 2025). One finding is particularly consequential for framework design. In a study of university students, policy awareness had no significant effect on behavior, whereas ethical beliefs did (Lund et al., 2025). If awareness of the rules is not the lever, then literacy rather than policy is where intervention must occur. 23 The Research Gap

# 4 The AI Security Literacy Framework 

This section answers RQ1. It describes how the six dimensions were derived, defines each with its Novice, Apprentice, and Practitioner descriptors, and introduces the two strands that cut across all six. 

4.1 Construction method 

The dimensions were derived through deductive synthesis rather than through empirical clustering, which is the appropriate method when the target construct has no existing instrument to factor-analyze. The procedure had four steps. We began by reviewing three source traditions and extracting every competency statement that bears on risk, trust, or security in an AI-mediated setting: the K–12 AI-literacy frameworks (Long and Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019), the cybersecurity education frameworks that schools already map against (GenCyber, CSTA, NICE (Petersen et al., 2020), ISTE), and the adversarial machine learning literature that defines what model attack and defense actually consist of (Croce et al., 2021; Athalye et al., 2018). This produced an initial pool of candidate competencies, many of them near-duplicates expressed in different vocabularies. 4We then consolidated that pool under three inclusion rules. A candidate was retained only if it was not already covered by general AI literacy or general cybersecurity literacy, since a dimen-sion that either parent framework teaches adequately does not justify a new domain. It had to be teachable and observable at the grades 6–8 band, which excluded several formal-methods and governance competencies that survive in the expert literature. Finally, it had to be instantiable against a real model or a real policy artifact , so that the dimension could be assessed by what a learner does rather than by what a learner reports. The retained competencies were mapped against the ecosystem roles. Any candidate that changed meaning across student, teacher, and administrator was designated a cross-cutting strand rather than a dimension, which is how role and ethics came to sit orthogonally to the six. Con-solidating the remainder yielded six dimensions; the count is an outcome of the merge rules, not a target. Each dimension was then checked for benchmark evidence, and the mapping in Table 5 is that check made explicit. A dimension for which no public dataset or policy corpus could establish the underlying phenomenon would have been a candidate for removal. None were removed on that basis, though the evidence available differs sharply in kind across the six, which Table 6 sets out. The framework comprises six dimensions, summarized in Table 3, each with a Novice, Appren-tice, and Practitioner descriptor and each applying across student, teacher, and leader roles. The six divide along a line worth making explicit. D1, D2, and D6 concern a learner’s relation-ship to an AI system — what is permitted, what is exposed, and whether the learner engages at all — and are largely dispositional. D3, D4, and D5 concern the system’s behavior — how it can be manipulated, when its output should be trusted, and how it fails under attack — and are largely analytic. The division is not a hierarchy, and neither half is prior to the other: a learner who understands adversarial failure perfectly but discloses nothing about their AI use is not securely literate, and neither is one who follows every policy but accepts fluent output uncritically. The progression is defined by what a learner can do unaided, not by content coverage. The Prac-titioner descriptors deliberately require a defensible judgment rather than a correct answer, because every dimension in this framework terminates in a decision under uncertainty: which threshold, which disclosure, which evidence is sufficient. That is also what makes the levels assessable from behavior rather than from self-report. 6Two strands cut across all six dimensions rather than sitting within any one of them. The role and stakeholder strand recognizes that the same competency looks different for a student, a teacher, and an administrator: permission means requesting for one and granting for another. The ethics strand recognizes that every technical capability in this framework is dual-use, and that teaching learners to attack a model obliges the curriculum to address why and when they should not. The dimensions are concurrent rather than sequential. A learner does not complete D1 before beginning D3; each dimension advances at its own rate, and the progression levels describe depth within a dimension rather than stages through the framework as a whole.

5.1 Tier 1: construct relevance from teacher and adult survey micro-data 

Tier 1 draws on four nationally representative survey files obtained under free registration: the Fall 2025 RAND American Teacher Panel survey on teacher careers and AI, fielded with Gallup (RAND American Educator Panels, 2025), and three waves of the Pew Research Center American Trends Panel, a probability-based panel of U.S. adults (Pew Research Center, 2024a,b, 2025b). Items were assigned to dimensions from their questionnaire wording, and the response categories counted for each item are fixed in the pipeline and printed beside every estimate. Each estimate is the weighted share of respondents giving the stated response, excluding item non-response, with a 95% interval computed from the Kish effective sample size. In all, the analysis yields 21 estimates from 4 sources, and the teacher estimates rest on up to n = 1,987 public school teachers. 8Figure 2: Weighted share of respondents giving the stated response for each mapped survey item, grouped by dimension, with 95% intervals. The permission problem D1 names is visible among teachers directly. Only 34.5% of teachers report that their school has a policy on how AI may be used, and 25.8% do not know whether one exists; among those whose school has a policy, only 36.2% describe it as very or extremely clear, and 30.1% cannot say whether their school is adopting AI too quickly or too slowly. Most teachers are therefore working without a rule they can state. Norms are unsettled among adults as well, of whom 53.9% hold that generative AI should credit the sources it draws on, and among students: in Pew’s 2024 survey of teens, 54% judged ChatGPT acceptable for researching new topics but only 18% for writing essays (Pew Research Center, 2025a). Permission, in other words, is task-specific and widely uncertain, which is why D1 is framed as resolving cases rather than memorizing a policy. 9For D2, 70.9% of adults are extremely or very concerned that AI will lead to people’s personal information being misused, and 59.3% feel they have little or no control over whether AI is used in their lives. Concern without control is the condition D2 addresses: the competency it names is reasoning about what a system retains, exposes, and infers, and so deciding what to withhold, which is the agency these respondents report lacking. For D3, 79.5% of adults describe online scams as a major problem, 63.9% receive scam text messages at least weekly, and 20.8% have lost money to an online scam. Self-assessed protection is high, with 71.0% saying they know a great deal or a fair amount about protecting themselves, so reported knowledge alone is not evidence of protection. Adults also expect the threat to grow with AI: 67.8% expect AI to make online scams more common, and 78.0% are extremely or very concerned about AI being used to impersonate people. For D4, 66.4% of adults are extremely or very concerned about people getting inaccurate in-formation from AI, and among adults asked about ChatGPT, 58.5% report little or no trust in its information about the 2024 presidential election. That pattern is consistent with skepticism on a high-stakes topic but is not evidence of calibration, since trust is reported rather than set against accuracy; the measured failure rate in the Tier 2 D4 analysis supplies the other half of that comparison. For D6, adults lean toward concern: 50.9% are more concerned than excited about the increasing use of AI, and 34.2% expect its effect on K–12 education to be negative. Teachers, by contrast, are already users: 68.9% used AI tools in their work in the 2025–26 school year, and 56.6% support teachers using AI. The sharpest result is an asymmetry of role. 72.5% of teachers expect teachers’ own use of AI to make teaching easier, while 61.9% expect students’ use to make it harder. Teachers regard AI as a tool in their own hands and a problem in their students’, which is the gap the role strand of the framework exists to close: the same competency, exercised by the learner and not only by the teacher. This evidence has three boundaries. The Pew respondents are adults, not students, and the teacher items concern teachers’ own use and their schools’ policies; neither measures a learner’s com-petency. Each dimension rests on a few single items drawn from separate cross-sectional samples, so no reliability or factor structure can be estimated, and none is claimed. And the item-to-dimension assignment is a judgment of face validity made from the questionnaires, not an empirically derived mapping. Tier 1 therefore establishes that the phenomena behind D1, D2, D3, D4, and D6 are prevalent and measurable in the national population surrounding a student, which is the construct relevance this tier was designed to supply. 

5.2 Tier 2: phenomenon-level construct relevance 

Tier 2 asks what public data can establish about the three technical dimensions. The evidence it yields is phenomenon-level construct relevance : it shows that the phenomena each dimension names are real, measurable, and behave in ways a learner must understand. It does not establish that D3, D4, and D5 form distinct latent dimensions in learners, which is a psychometric question no public benchmark can answer and which Section 9 assigns to a validation study. We use the narrower term throughout and never claim that Tier 2 validates the dimensional structure. All Tier 2 analyses use a fixed seed (20260817); the adversarial evaluation additionally replicates across 3 independently trained models. 10 D3: threat and manipulation recognition 

RealToxicityPrompts is constructed by sampling sentences uniformly from four equal-width toxicity bands, then splitting each sentence into a prompt and a continuation and re-scoring both halves independently (Gehman et al., 2020). That construction is what makes the corpus suitable for D3: it permits direct comparison between the toxicity of a whole sentence and the toxicity of the fragment a reader encounters first. From a seeded random sample of the 99,442-prompt corpus, n = 19,906 prompts carried non-null Perspective toxicity scores, with mean 0.287 and 95th percentile 0.872. 

Figure 3: Share of prompts labeled toxic as a function of threshold (left), and prompt-half toxicity across the four bands from which parent sentences were sampled equally (right). The right panel of Figure 3 carries the more consequential result. Although the underlying sen-tences were drawn in equal proportions from all four toxicity bands, the prompt halves concentrate heavily in the lowest band, far from the 25% design target marked on the plot. Within this corpus, an initial text fragment can therefore substantially underrepresent the harmfulness of the complete passage. That is the recognition problem D3 targets, demonstrated on a corpus deliberately built to contain harmful material in equal measure rather than inferred from an estimated prevalence. We claim it for this corpus and this construction procedure, not as a general property of AI-mediated communication. The left panel establishes a second and independent D3 claim. The toxic label is not a property of text but a consequence of a chosen operating point. Raising the threshold from 0 .3 to 0 .7 moves the share of prompts labeled toxic from 35.62% to 12.4%, a swing of 23.2 percentage points, with 10.47% of prompts falling in the ambiguous 0 .4–0 .6 band around the conventional cutoff, where the share is 21.82%. A learner who treats a moderation verdict as a fact about content, rather than as a decision made at a threshold someone selected, has misunderstood the mechanism. The same mechanism governs the scam detection that D3 most directly concerns. On the SMS Spam Collection (Almeida et al., 2011), 5,574 real text messages of which 13.4% are spam, a multi-hot bag-of-words classifier over a 500-word vocabulary, the same input representation as the platform’s own scam model, reaches an average precision of 0.976 on a held-out set of 1,673 messages containing 224 scams. The detector is strong, and its operating point is still a choice. At a threshold of 0 .3 it raises 15 false alarms and misses 18 scams; at 0 .7 it raises 2 false alarms but misses 36. The generation configuration of TruthfulQA comprises 817 questions across 38 categories, each written so that a plausible-sounding but false answer is the tempting one; the largest category is Misconceptions, with 100 items.

The breadth visible in Figure 6, spanning health and law through conspiracies and common misconceptions, establishes the relevance of the verification and trust-calibration competencies D4 names. Misplaced confidence in fluent output is a general and enumerable failure mode rather than a niche one, which is why trust calibration can be taught as a transferable skill rather than as a list of topics to distrust. Category counts establish coverage, however, not difficulty, so we additionally report an observed failure rate. Scoring gpt2 (Radford et al., 2019) on the multiple-choice configuration by mean per-token log-likelihood, the model selects the true answer on 27.7% of 817 questions (95% CI 24.7–30.8), against a chance level of 22.6% given a mean of five options per item. 13 Figure 7: Truthfulness of gpt2 by category for the fifteen largest categories, against the overall rate and the chance level. Figure 7 shows how thin that margin is and how unevenly it is distributed: the interval clears chance, but only just, and several categories fall below it. A model that produces fluent prose is therefore barely better than guessing at distinguishing a true answer from a plausible false one on this benchmark, which is precisely the gap trust calibration exists to close. Fluency at production does not confer reliability at selection, and a learner who reads the former as evidence of the latter has made the error D4 names. The benchmark supplies the failure opportunities and now a measured failure rate ; it does not itself measure calibration, which requires subjective confidence set against objective accuracy and is a property of a learner or a model rather than of a corpus.

D5: adversarial and technical understanding 

D5 is the dimension where a learner must grasp not only that models fail, but that a reported failure rate is itself a measurement subject to method. Establishing that requires models under our own control rather than a leaderboard entry, so we train them and attack them in two ways. The purpose is not to contribute a robustness result — RobustBench (Croce et al., 2021) exists for that, and we defer to it — but to produce, at a scale a classroom can reproduce, the divergence between two defensible evaluations of the same system. A convolutional classifier trained for 15 epochs on CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky, 2009), and replicated across 3 independently initialized runs, reached 79.3% mean clean test accuracy with a standard deviation of 0.45 percentage points across seeds. Figure 9 shows the two curves diverging at every non-zero budget, and the divergence is not marginal: at ϵ = 4 /255 the single-step attack still leaves the model above 5% accuracy while the ten-step attack has already driven it to near zero. A reader consulting only the single-step curve would substantially overstate what the model withstands. A robustness number therefore describes how a model was evaluated at least as much as what was trained, which reproduces at classroom scale the argument that apparent robustness collapses under an attacker permitted to adapt (Athalye et al., 2018; Croce and Hein, 2020; Tram` er et al., 2020). This is why the platform’s Defender level terminates in an adaptive evaluation rather than a fixed-attack score, and why we defer throughout to RobustBench (Croce et al., 2021) for standardized robustness measurement. 16 Figure 10: Single-step accuracy against perturbation budget, with the ten-class chance level marked and the range across 3 seeds shaded. Figure 10 isolates the single-step curve and adds a second lesson that Figure 9 compresses. The shaded band is the range across the three independently trained models. At ϵ = 8 /255 that range spans 1.4–3.1%, roughly five times the width of the sampling interval computed on the pooled evaluation (2.1–2.4). The dominant source of uncertainty in a robustness figure at this budget is therefore not how many images were tested but which model happened to be trained. A single reported number, however large the test set behind it, conceals that variation entirely. For a learner, this is the companion to the attack-choice lesson: a robustness claim is contingent on the attack, on the evaluation, and on the particular model — three ways for the same system to be described differently by two honest evaluators. The curve also crosses the ten-class chance level between ϵ = 2 /255 and ϵ = 4 /255, meaning that beyond that budget the model is not merely degraded toward guessing but is actively steered into incorrect classes. For a learner, the difference between a model that has stopped working and a model that has been turned against its task is the intuition the Attacker level exists to deliver.

6.2 Level structure and the teacher-support instrument 

Each module is a three-level mission whose roles correspond to the progression in Table 4. In Defend the AI , the Attacker level asks a learner to predict how much perturbation will flip the classifier and then test that prediction against the model’s actual breaking point, clearing the level by fooling three distinct images. The Spy level builds a surrogate purely from queries to the real model and must reach 80% fidelity before running a transfer test, so the query budget becomes a resource the learner manages. The Defender level applies genuine input defenses and then re-attacks them with a BPDA-style adaptive attacker that knows the defense, which is what separates genuine robustness from gradient obfuscation that merely looks strong. In Fooled , the Scammer level inverts the usual exercise by asking the learner to reword a real scam until it scores below the filter’s threshold; the Detective level pits the learner’s verdict against the detector’s on pre-scored messages; and the Scam-Buster level hands over the threshold itself, running a labeled inbox of ten scams and ten legitimate messages and displaying the precision–recall trade-off live, with deployment permitted once F1 reaches 80%. Difficulty therefore rises by shifting the locus of the decision: from producing an effect, to judging against the model, to setting the operating point and defending it. Beyond the learner-facing modules, the platform functions as a teacher-support instrument. Four behavioral signals are captured per student per level — time on level, attempts at the level’s main action, hint openings, and idle episodes — and stored as cumulative totals on the existing per-student progress row rather than as an event stream. Those signals plus mean score are min– max normalized across the class and combined into a 0–100 struggle index with fixed, inspectable weights (time 0.25, attempts 0.20, hint use 0.25, idle 0.15, low score 0.15). A student is flagged only when their index falls in the class’s top quartile and at least one concrete signal sits in the upper half of the class range, and the flag always names those signals, so a teacher sees the reason rather than a score. A small deterministic k-means over the same normalized vectors groups the class into three behavioral cohorts. There are no trained weights: for a class of roughly thirty students and four signals, an explainable index is more trustworthy to a teacher than a supervised predictor, and no labeled record of which students struggled exists yet to train one. All of it computes in the browser, so no student data leaves the device. This supports educator D4 and D6 rather than student competencies alone, which is what the ecosystem framing requires.

# 7 Discussion 

This section answers the three research questions directly and then interprets the results. It sets out the design trade-offs behind the evidence and their implication for teacher preparation. 

7.1 Answers to the research questions 

RQ1. AI security literacy comprises six dimensions: permission and norms, data and privacy secu-rity, threat and manipulation recognition, trust calibration and verification, adversarial and tech-nical understanding, and disposition and agency, each with a three-level progression (Table 4) and each applying across student, teacher, and administrator roles, with role and ethics as cross-cutting strands rather than additional dimensions. The dimensions were derived deductively (Section 4.1); their number is an outcome of the consolidation rules rather than a design target. 

RQ2. Public datasets provide phenomenon-level construct relevance for every dimension, of two different kinds. For the technical dimensions, the benchmarks establish that the named phenomena are real and measurable: labels, including scam-detection labels, depend on a chosen threshold, an initial fragment can underrepresent the harmfulness of a full passage, misconception-prone questions span many domains and a fluent model barely exceeds chance on them, bias is enumerable by attribute, and a robustness figure is a property of the evaluation and of the particular model trained. For the attitudinal dimensions, weighted national estimates show the phenomena are prevalent where students live and learn: most teachers work without a school AI policy they find clear, adults report concern about AI alongside little sense of control over it, scam exposure is widespread and losses common, and teachers see AI as easing their own work while complicating it through students’ use. No public dataset speaks to the dimensional structure of the construct itself, which is the boundary Table 6 draws. 

RQ3. NeuralFortify demonstrates technical operationalization. Every dimension maps to a platform feature (Table 9), and the two technical modules pose their decisions against real trained models; the progression levels correspond to shifts in where the decision sits rather than to content coverage, and the platform generates teacher-facing insights from behavioral signals without student 20 data leaving the device. Building it also revised a Practitioner descriptor (Section 6.1), which is the sense in which the operationalization informs the framework rather than merely illustrating it. Learner efficacy is untested, and no efficacy claim is made. 

7.2 Interpretation and trade-offs 

The convergence of AI literacy and cybersecurity education is the broader movement to which this framework belongs, and the survey evidence suggests why literacy rather than policy is the effective lever: among university students, awareness of institutional rules does not predict behavior, while ethical belief does (Lund et al., 2025). A framework that only tells learners what is permitted will therefore under-perform one that develops the judgment behind that permission. Several choices in this work are deliberate trade-offs rather than shortcomings, and are worth stating as such. The first concerns the grounding corpus for D3. RealToxicityPrompts is stratified for toxicity by design, which makes it unsuitable for estimating how much harmful content exists in ordinary web text. We accepted that limitation in exchange for the comparison the design uniquely enables, between a whole sentence and the fragment preceding it, which grounds a more precise claim than a prevalence figure would have. The second concerns Perspective API scores, which are model-derived and carry documented biases that the threshold analysis inherits. We use them because the operating-point argument concerns how any scored threshold behaves, and holds regardless of which scorer produces the scores. The third trade-off concerns the D5 demonstration, which uses a small convolutional network and two gradient-based attack families rather than a standardized benchmark suite. A larger model would have obscured the pedagogical point behind training cost, and the demonstration is intended to make a phenomenon legible rather than to contribute a robustness measurement, for which we defer to RobustBench. The fourth concerns the D4 baseline, which uses a small model and multiple-choice scoring rather than open-ended generation judged for truthfulness. That choice makes the failure rate reproducible on classroom hardware and comparable to a stated chance level, at the cost of measuring discrimination among supplied options rather than unconstrained generation. The fifth concerns BBQ’s uneven attribute coverage, which constrains the benchmark to within-attribute claims; we treat this as scoping the claim rather than disqualifying the dataset. The sixth concerns Tier 1, which uses surveys of adults and teachers rather than students, and single items rather than scales. These are the only nationally representative micro-data on these phenomena that are openly available, and we report them as prevalence rather than as measurement. Finally, the two tiers rest on different kinds of data and are deliberately not combined into a single model, because the constructs they measure and the sampling frames they rest on are not commensurate. A further implication concerns teacher preparation. Every dimension in this framework applies to educators as well as students, and the Tier 1 evidence shows that most teachers work without a school AI policy they find clear. A curriculum that treats teachers only as deliverers of student competencies will not close the gap that the framework describes. 

# 8 Limitations 

Three limitations bound what this paper claims. First, the framework is grounded but not validated: public data support construct relevance and coverage, not a validated measurement scale, and no psychometric evaluation has been conducted. Second, the evidence differs in kind across the framework: D1, D2, and D6 rest on weighted national estimates from adult and teacher surveys rather than from students, D5 on technical evidence alone, and no dimension on learner data (Table 6). No public dataset was built to measure AISL, and Tier 1 is a secondary mapping of 21 single items rather than a measurement scale. Third, NeuralFortify is used illustratively, and no efficacy or learning claims are made anywhere in this work. 

# 9 Future Work 

This section sets out the work that follows from this paper in order of proximity: extending the survey evidence to students, a validation study with learners, and a longer-term data-generation loop. It closes with the governance obligations that any work involving minors carries. The nearest-term work extends Tier 1 from adults and teachers to students. Student-level micro-data on AI permission and use, such as the teen surveys whose published findings are cited above, would allow the attitudinal dimensions to be estimated in the population the framework addresses. That work requires no new instrument and no human subjects, only access to data that are reported but not yet released. Beyond it lies a validation study that would administer a pre- and post-test AISL instrument across the six dimensions and the progression levels, with design-based iteration and psychometric evaluation. Existing validated instruments provide both a starting point and a comparison: the Meta AI Literacy Scale (Carolus et al., 2023) and the middle-school AI Literacy Concept Inventory (Zhang et al., 2025) supply tested item formats and a reference against which discriminant validity of the security dimensions can be assessed. We describe it only to establish that the framework is testable; it is out of scope here. The longer-term ambition is a closed loop in which the framework informs a platform, the platform instruments learner activity, and that activity generates two reusable products: pre- and post-test competency data and human-in-the-loop adversarial corpora that extend public bench-marks into an adolescent population that is currently excluded. Other researchers could then reuse both to test and refine the framework and the benchmarks together. Any involvement of minors carries governance obligations that shape the design rather than simply following it: IRB approval, parental consent, COPPA and FERPA compliance, and de-identified, sandboxed data release. The platform’s privacy-by-architecture identity model, which collects no PII, is what makes such a release tractable. 

# 10 Conclusion 

We propose AI security literacy as a distinct competency domain, and the evidence assembled here supports treating it as a candidate for one. This paper has defined it across six dimensions spanning the school ecosystem, grounded its technical dimensions in public benchmarks and a reproducible multi-seed adversarial evaluation and its attitudinal dimensions in national survey micro-data, and demonstrated through NeuralFortify that the framework can be implemented on real models rather than being described in the abstract. The grounding results sharpened the framework rather than merely confirming it: harm is often absent from the text that precedes it, a moderation label is a threshold decision rather than a property of content, most teachers work without an AI policy they can state, a fluent model barely exceeds chance at telling a true answer from a plausible false one, and a robustness number describes an evaluation and a particular trained model rather than a system. Each is a lesson a learner can be walked through with real systems, which distinguishes a teachable framework from a taxonomy.
