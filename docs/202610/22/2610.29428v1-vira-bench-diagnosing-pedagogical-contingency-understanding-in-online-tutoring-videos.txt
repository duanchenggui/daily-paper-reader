Understanding a tutoring video requires more than recognizing its events. When a teacher highlights a word, asks a question, and reacts to the learner’s reply, a model must align language with visible actions, recover event order, identify learner up-take, and determine whether the follow-up is con-ditioned on that response. This multimodal NLP problem spans dialogue grounding, discourse struc-ture, and interactional pragmatics. We refer to this focused capability as understanding visually grounded pedagogical contingencies .MLLM evaluation has expanded from visual perception and question answering to long-form video comprehension, fine-grained temporal rea-soning, and social interaction reasoning (Liu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025; Man-galam et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024; Kong et al., 2025). These benchmarks primarily examine event recognition, temporal relation recov-ery, or holistic social-state inference. Educational-dialogue resources model teacher and learner turns but typically rely on transcripts (Demszky and Hill, 2022; Macina et al., 2023, 2025). Neither line jointly tests whether a model can connect a visible intervention to the response it elicits and then con-nect that response to a subsequent adjustment. A model may therefore recognize each action and its order without recovering the interactional contin-gency that links them. Online tutoring provides a natural setting for evaluating this capability. Grounded in the zone of proximal development (Vygotsky, 1978), formative assessment (Black and Wiliam, 1998; Ruiz-Primo and Furtak, 2007), and contingent scaffolding (Hat-tie and Timperley, 2007; van de Pol et al., 2015), effective instruction requires detecting learner un-derstanding and adjusting support in real time. In tutoring interactions, teachers perform visible op-erations on a shared screen (circling, highlighting, dragging); learners respond, and teachers confirm or correct. A short clip therefore exposes an action, response, and adjustment chain with explicit visual and temporal evidence. Table 1 uses five binary criteria. Temporal video requires ordered evidence; pedagogical interaction links teaching, learner response, and follow-up; theory anchor denotes an explicit theoretical ba-sis; diagnostic layers separate cognitive operations; and temporal-order perturbation tests event-order dependence. Existing resources do not combine all five. We present VIRA-Bench , a diagnostic bench-mark for evaluating whether video-language mod-els can understand short-horizon pedagogical con-tingencies, instantiated through one-to-one on-line tutoring interactions. The design draws on two theoretical foundations. The Initiation, Re-sponse, and Follow-up (IRF) structure (Sinclair and Coulthard, 1975) characterizes local classroom ex-changes as three-part sequences. Contingent teach-ing theory (van de Pol et al., 2010; Wood et al., 1976) describes instructional support as adapting to evidence of learner understanding. Based on these foundations, VIRA-Bench decomposes vi-sually grounded interactional contingency under-standing into three diagnostic layers distinguished by the cognitive operation required. Observable Evidence Grounding tests whether models can ground answers in observable actions and screen content. Interaction Contingency Reasoning 

tests whether models can recover directed relations between observations, such as linking an interven-tion to the learner response it elicits. Support-Response Modeling tests whether models can pre-dict learner responses and judge whether the sup-port level matches the learner state. The bench-mark contains 1,005 tutoring clips (15–60 s each) and 1,322 multiple-choice questions across eight diagnostic subtasks, constructed through a mul-tistage pipeline where LLMs generate candidate descriptions and questions, researchers audit evi-dence against source videos, and domain experts verify a stratified item sample. We evaluate 11 commercial and open-source MLLMs under the full Video+ASR setting and analyze five representative models with ASR-only and temporally shuffled inputs. Overall accuracy ranges from 20.3% to 61.3%, leaving a 29.7-point gap between the best model and the 91.0% human reference on a stratified subset. Removing video re-duces Observable Evidence Grounding accuracy by 12.7–22.0 points, whereas disrupting visual event order reduces Interaction Contingency Reason-ing accuracy by 11.9–19.1 points. Free-response, STEM +O PTIONS , and synchronized-shuffle con-trols further probe dependencies on response for-mat, static question cues, and video–ASR align-ment. Together, these results show that the three layers expose distinct behavioral dependencies in visual grounding and temporal-order reasoning rather than collapsing into a single video-QA score. 2 Related Work 

Video benchmarks. Video benchmarks now cover short-form perception, long-form understand-ing, and domain-specific challenges (Li et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025; Mangalam et al., 2023; Ataallah et al., 2025; Ray et al., 2025; Shafique et al., 2025). Diagnostic suites further test reliance on tempo-ral evidence or static shortcuts (Liu et al., 2024; Cores et al., 2025; Shangguan et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025), motivating con-trolled input conditions. Adjacent work studies LLM reasoning and agents (Wu et al., 2026a,b,c; Yang et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026a,b), and cal-ibration, hallucination, context optimization, and culture-dependent multimodal interpretation (Chen et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026). Causal video QA benchmarks, however, still emphasize physical or event causality rather than response-dependent participant contin-gency (Xiao et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2019); Appendix I provides a detailed comparison. 

Educational dialogue and scaffolding theory. 

Recent work has begun studying social and ed-ucational interaction in video (Kong et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Selvakumar et al., 2025). In edu-cational NLP, Demszky et al. (2021) operational-ize conversational uptake, the NCTE Transcripts (Demszky and Hill, 2022) provide large-scale class-room dialogue data, Demszky and Liu (2023) show NLP-powered feedback improves instruction, and MathDial (Macina et al., 2023) and MathTutor-Bench (Macina et al., 2025) evaluate LLM tutoring with pedagogically grounded criteria. Demszky et al. (2026) identify the gap between transcript-based NLP and video-based observation. Auto-mated classroom observation tools such as CLASS (Pianta et al., 2008) have been adapted with LLMs (Whitehill and LoCasale-Crouch, 2024; Wang and Demszky, 2023), and ClassMind (Qu et al., 2025) extends this to video-based feedback, though on pre-segmented lessons. Dou et al. (2025) show that user simulators can proxy multi-turn evaluation, highlighting the need for complementary bench-marks that directly test interaction understanding from recorded evidence. VIRA-Bench addresses this gap by translating the IRF exchange structure (Sinclair and Coulthard, 1975) and contingent scaf-folding theory (Wood et al., 1976; van de Pol et al., 2010) into a computational benchmark with paired evidence conditions.

The central construct is visually grounded interactional contingency : a short-horizon ex-change in which a teacher intervention elicits a learner response and the teacher then follows up based on that response, all inspectable from the clip. Routine responses emerge within seconds, while cognitively demanding responses may take longer (Rowe, 1986); VIRA-Bench focuses on local con-tingency within a single exchange rather than lon-gitudinal scaffolding fading across sessions. 

3.2 Diagnostic Framework 

We design a three-layer diagnostic framework grounded in the IRF exchange structure and the contingent teaching cycle. The layers are separated by the type of cognitive operation required (Chi and Wylie, 2014), rather than by a simple difficulty ordering. In Observable Evidence Grounding, mod-els must ground the interaction in observable evi-dence from the screen, teacher action, and student behavior. In Interaction Contingency Reasoning, they must connect these observations through tem-poral and contingent relations, asking whether the teacher’s intervention plausibly triggered the stu-dent’s response and whether the teacher’s follow-up targeted that response. In Support-Response Modeling, they must model the support–response relation that the exchange implies: predicting how a learner is likely to respond to a teacher’s sup-port move, or judging whether that support level matches the learner state revealed by the response. The eight subtasks operationalize these three cognitive operations while keeping each question targeted at one primary capability. For example, a causal attribution question may show a teacher circling a word on the shared screen, followed by the student typing a response in the chat box. The model must distinguish whether the student’s re-sponse follows from the visual intervention, pre-cedes it, merely coincides with it, or reflects an un-supported alternative explanation. Such questions test whether the model can use temporal evidence to recover a plausible I →R contingency, rather than only recognizing isolated events. 

3.3 Clip Extraction from Tutoring Sessions 

Source videos are drawn from a corpus of over 1,000 professionally recorded online English tu-toring sessions, each lasting 1–2 hours, collected through video-conferencing platforms between 2018 and 2021. The released benchmark spans 72 learners and 22 teachers at primary and secondary levels. Most learners speak Mandarin as their first language, while a small number are native English speakers. Teacher speech therefore naturally in-cludes code-switching between English instruction and Mandarin clarification. All faces are blurred to protect participant privacy, with empirically veri-fied impact on pedagogical content visibility below 3%. Clips are extracted through a coarse-to-fine pro-cess. First, ASR-based coarse segmentation iden-tifies candidate instructional intervals, reducing each 1–2 hour session to approximately 15–30 minutes of likely interaction. Second, Visual Con-tent Event (VCE) detection compares consecutive shared-screen frames using structural similarity (Wang et al., 2004) to locate moments where the teacher produces a visual intervention, such as writ-ing, circling, highlighting, or dragging an object. Third, each VCE is used as an anchor point for clip generation, yielding a 15–60 second window centered on a single teacher intervention and its surrounding interactional context. The clip-level pipeline starts from 2,760 VCE an-chors. After merging nearby anchors and removing duplicate windows, 1,840 candidate clips remain, filtering 33.3% of the anchors. Clip quality screen-ing further removes 19.6% of candidates whose shared screen is unclear, whose student response window is missing, or whose interaction contains multiple overlapping exchanges. This yields 1,480 clips. After structured evidence description and researcher audit, 1,370 clips remain, with 7.4% removed due to unrecoverable missing or incon-sistent evidence. Finally, question-level filtering keeps 1,005 clips with at least one valid MCQ, filtering 26.6% of clips at this stage. Extraction parameters are reported in Appendix A. 

3.4 Multi-Stage Question Construction 

VIRA-Bench follows a construction–evaluation separation protocol with two main stages. First, each clip is converted into an audited structured de-scription recording the observable and temporal ev-idence needed for pedagogical interpretation. Sec-ond, candidate MCQs are generated from these de-scriptions and filtered through model-based checks and expert audit. To reduce direct construction– evaluation overlap, the two stages use disjoint model sets: GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) produces structured descriptions and GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) generates candidate MCQs, while held-out valida-tors GLM-5V-Turbo (GLM-V Team et al., 2026) and Claude-Haiku-4.5 (Anthropic, 2025) handle shortcut filtering and diagnostic calibration. All construction models are excluded from the evalua-tion leaderboard. 

3.4.1 Structured Evidence Description 

GPT-4o receives each clip as video input together with its ASR transcript and produces a structured JSON description covering five evidence dimen-sions: (1) screen events , including timestamped teacher actions on the shared screen such as draw-ing, highlighting, dragging, and slide transitions; (2) student behaviors , including observable ver-bal responses, gestures, gaze shifts, and screen interactions; (3) teacher speech acts , including ASR-derived utterances categorized by pragmatic function such as instruction, question, feedback, and elicitation; (4) temporal dynamics , including the intervention–response pattern and estimated response latency; and (5) cognitive demand level ,describing the complexity of the task posed by the teacher. These descriptions are treated as evidence drafts rather than final annotations. Before question gen-eration, researchers audit each description against the source video, correct factual errors, and remove clips whose key events cannot be reliably recov-ered. Only audited descriptions are used for MCQ generation. This step provides an explicit evidence interface between raw videos and question con-struction, reducing the risk that generated items depend on unsupported interpretations. 

3.4.2 Question Construction and Filtering 

GPT-5 generates candidate questions from the au-dited descriptions, with the target subtask specified in the prompt. Distractors are produced in a sepa-rate pass under three normalization constraints that reduce superficial answer cues: (1) length match-ing , where all four options must fall within 20% of the mean option length; (2) specificity match-ing , where distractors must refer to concrete events in the clip rather than generic alternatives; and (3) tone consistency , where all options use the same register and hedging level. This process yields 2,400 candidate MCQs. We remove 5.8% that con-tain invalid option formats, repeated options, or near-duplicate questions within the same clip, leav-ing 2,260 questions. Full prompts and templates are provided in Appendix B. 

Video-agnostic shortcut filtering. Following ad-versarial filtering approaches that reduce dataset biases (Bras et al., 2020; Sakaguchi et al., 2020), we test each question under a S TEM +O PTIONS 

condition. GLM-5V and Claude-Haiku-4.5 receive only the question stem and four options, without video frames, ASR transcript, or structured evi-dence. Items answered correctly by both valida-tors are treated as likely containing option-level shortcuts that allow answering without interaction evidence, and are removed. This filter reduces, but cannot eliminate, surface-level answer cues. It re-moves 10.6% of the remaining questions, leaving 2,020 candidates. 

Evidence-supported diagnostic filtering. The two validators then inspect each question against the audited structured evidence and answer it under the full input condition. A question is removed if its gold answer is not explicitly supported by the ev-idence, if more than one option is compatible with the evidence, or if the question requires informa-tion outside the clip. A correct answer from both validators does not itself trigger removal. Evidence-supported questions are regenerated only when the validators also identify evidence leakage or weak distractor discrimination. This step is used to cali-brate diagnostic difficulty rather than to construct a validator-hard subset. In the final set, neither validator answers 293 items correctly, exactly one answers 587 correctly, and both answer 442 cor-rectly. Together, this filtering stage removes 34.4% of the remaining candidates, leaving 1,326 ques-tions. 

Expert audit. Three expert auditors with back-grounds in education and second-language peda-gogy independently review a 360-question strat-ified audit set comprising 300 random questions balanced across all layers and subtasks, and 60 high-risk questions selected from inference-heavy subtasks and cases where the two validators dis-agreed. Each item is judged on four dimensions: question clarity, gold-answer correctness, evidence sufficiency, and subtask alignment. Of the audited questions, 340 are accepted without change, 16 retained after minor revision, and 4 removed, yield-ing a 94.4% direct acceptance rate and a 1.1% re-moval rate. Sampling adequacy analysis and inter-annotator agreement are reported in Appendix C. After expert adjudication, 1,322 MCQs are retained in the final benchmark.

4.2 Human–Model Gap and Layer Profiles 

Table 2 reports leaderboard results under the Video+ASR condition. The best model, Gemini 3 Pro, reaches 61.3% overall accuracy, leaving a 29.7-point gap below the 91.0% human reference on the stratified subset. The five leading models cluster between 55.9% and 61.3% with overlapping confidence intervals, while the remaining models range from 20.3% to 43.0%. We therefore focus on the broad human–model gap and recurring layer profiles rather than fine-grained model ranks. Across diagnostic layers, all top-tier models fol-low a consistent ordering of SRM > OEG > ICR. Among the top five models, the OEG–ICR gap ranges from 0.8 to 10.7 points. This recurring pro-file, rather than any individual model comparison, motivates the evidence-dependency analyses below. The human reference follows the same layer order-ing (SRM 94.0% > OEG 92.0% > ICR 88.0%) but at uniformly high accuracy. Its 95% interval is 86.2–94.2%, leaving its lower bound about 25 points above the best model. This subset result supports a robust human-model gap but should not be read as a precise human estimate for all 1,322 questions. The three below-chance models do not collapse onto a single answer position. Their pre-dicted labels span A–D, with each label accounting for 22.0–26.6% of outputs, and all 1,322 responses per model are parsed successfully. Their scores therefore reflect incorrect item-level choices rather than invalid responses or a dominant-label parsing artifact. Together, the leaderboard and layer scores establish the first experimental claim: current mod-els remain far from the human reference, with the most consistent weakness appearing in interaction contingency reasoning. 

4.3 Response-Format and Shortcut Controls 

The leaderboard could be inflated if models exploit labeled choices or static question cues rather than interaction evidence. We test these alternatives with two controls. Under free response, models answer without labeled choices and a deterministic normalized-text matcher scores the output; it agrees with manual judgments on 96.0% of a 50-item au-dit. Under S TEM +O PTIONS , models receive the question and answer options but no video, ASR, description, or construction metadata. Figure 4 shows that free response lowers overall accuracy by 12.1 and 11.7 points, so answer op-tions provide useful scaffolding, yet the same layer ordering persists: both models remain stronger on OEG than ICR. We next test the source of each layer’s evidence by evaluating five representative models under the perturbation conditions defined in §4. 4.6 Diagnostic Implications 

The paired interventions turn layer scores into testable development targets. For OEG, the large no-video drop points to event-level alignment among speech, teacher actions, and screen changes. For ICR, the selective shuffle effect identifies preservation of actor, target, and order across in-tervention, response, and follow-up as the relevant evaluation target. For SRM, the best model remains 29.1 points below the human reference, motivating further tests of learner-state tracking beyond partial cues from individual channels. These targets also define how to interpret future gains. Stronger OEG should reflect better recovery of actions and screen state from video; stronger ICR should appear under intact order and dimin-ish when the exchange is permuted; and stronger SRM should not be attributable to static question cues. Reporting full-input scores together with perturbation deltas therefore distinguishes gains in grounding, order-sensitive reasoning, and pedagog-ical judgment more clearly than aggregate accuracy alone. 

5 Conclusion 

We introduced VIRA-Bench, a focused benchmark that decomposes short-horizon pedagogical con-tingency understanding into three theory-anchored layers in one-to-one online tutoring. Across 11 models, the best reaches 61.3% against a 91.0% hu-man reference on a stratified subset, and ICR gen-erally has the lowest layer accuracy among leading models. Removing video most strongly degrades OEG, whereas disrupting event order most strongly degrades ICR; this ICR-selective decline persists when aligned video–ASR segments are shuffled together. These results identify distinct behavioral evidence dependencies rather than an internal archi-tectural mechanism. Within its one-to-one tutoring scope, VIRA-Bench separates progress in observ-able grounding from progress in order-sensitive contingency reasoning. 

6 Limitations 

VIRA-Bench focuses on 15–60-second excerpts from one-to-one online English tutoring, so its find-ings may not transfer directly to multi-party, cross-domain, or longer-horizon interactions. Although its LLM-assisted construction is checked by mul-tiple models and experts, occasional annotation artifacts may remain. Finally, the multiple-choice video-plus-ASR protocol targets reproducible mul-timodal understanding rather than open-ended gen-eration, action, or multi-turn adaptation. 

Ethical Considerations 

The source videos are online tutoring sessions in-volving child and adolescent learners, recorded by a commercial educational institution between 2018 and 2021 with parental or guardian consent for educational and research use. The original data-collection protocol was approved by the source institution’s ethics review committee. The insti-tution provided de-identified recordings under a data-sharing agreement limited to non-commercial academic research and prohibiting re-identification. Before analysis or release, faces are blurred, identi-fying metadata are removed, and person mentions in transcripts are replaced with role placeholders; no question relies on identity, and the research team has no access to unblurred recordings or participant identities. Only processed clips and transcripts are distributed under a restricted license, and VIRA-Bench is intended for model evaluation rather than high-stakes assessment of individual teachers or students.

A Clip Extraction Details 

We extract clips from long online tutoring sessions through three stages. First, ASR-guided coarse seg-mentation transcribes each session with Whisper-large-v3, identifies teacher utterances containing in-structional functions (explanation, elicitation, cor-rection, feedback, task prompting), and merges ad-jacent instructional intervals separated by less than 10 seconds. This reduces each 1–2 hour session to approximately 15–30 minutes of likely interaction. Second, Visual Content Event (VCE) detection samples shared-screen frames at 2 fps within the ASR-selected intervals and compares consecutive frames using structural similarity (Wang et al., 2004) and pixel-level difference ratio. A VCE is triggered when either the SSIM score drops below 0.88 or the changed-pixel ratio exceeds 3% of the frame area. Events are classified by magnitude: 

large changes (SSIM < 0.88 and diff > 30%) in-dicate slide transitions or major screen rewrites, while local changes (SSIM < 0.88 and diff > 3%) capture teacher drawing, highlighting, or annota-tion. A minimum gap of 2 s is enforced between consecutive events to avoid duplicate triggers from gradual animations, and low-level cursor-only mo-tion is removed unless accompanied by a stable pointing, drawing, highlighting, dragging, or slide-transition pattern. Third, clip windows are generated by merging detected VCE events within a 5-second window into a single anchor group, then extending 3 s be-fore the first event and 12 s after the last event. The window is expanded to nearby ASR turn bound-aries when this helps include the student response or teacher follow-up. Clips are truncated at 60 s and discarded if shorter than 15 s. 

Quality screening and face blurring. Candidate clips are screened by Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google AI for Developers, 2025a) on five dimensions: vi-sual clarity, teaching activity, interaction complete-ness, audio quality, and benchmark value. A clip is rejected if any dimension fails. All faces are blurred using MediaPipe face detection with a

B Prompting and Item Construction Details 

VIRA-Bench uses a construction-evaluation sepa-ration protocol. GPT-4o drafts structured evidence descriptions, GPT-5 generates candidate MCQs and distractors, and GLM-5V and Claude-Haiku-4.5 serve as held-out validators. None of these con-struction or validation models is included in the leaderboard. 

B.1 Structured Evidence Description 

GPT-4o receives the tutoring clip and its ASR tran-script, and is instructed to output a structured JSON record. The prompt specifies five evidence fields with typed sub-fields and timestamp requirements. The model is instructed to focus on observable evi-dence and to mark uncertain observations explicitly rather than infer hidden mental states. 

You are an expert educational video analyst spe-cializing in online tutoring sessions. Your task is to produce a detailed, structured description of the teaching interaction in this video clip. The video shows an online classroom with two main visual areas: (1) webcam feeds showing teacher and student (body language, gestures, and posture are visible); (2) teacher’s shared screen showing instructional content. Respond in JSON with the following fields: 

screen_events : list of {timestamp_start, timestamp_end, action_type, action_description, content_context, pedagogical_tool}. Action types: drawing_writing, pointing_highlighting, slide_transition, drag_drop_interactive, anima-tion_reveal, none_ambient. 

student_behaviors : list of {timestamp_start, timestamp_end, behavior_type, behav-ior_description, verbal_content, body_language}. Behavior types: verbal_active, verbal_passive, gestural_physical, screen_interaction, no_observable_response. 

teacher_speech : list of {timestamp, content, speech_act}. Speech acts: instruction, question, feedback_positive, feedback_corrective, explana-tion, elicitation, social_talk. 

temporal_dynamics : {in-tervention_response_pattern, response_latency_seconds, causal_chain_description}. Patterns: im-mediate ( <3s), delayed (3–10s), cumulative, interleaved, no_response. 

cognitive_demand : one of recall_recognize, ap-ply_practice, analyze_create. 

B.2 MCQ Generation 

GPT-5 receives the audited evidence record, a tar-get layer/subtask, and per-subtask instructions spec-ifying the cognitive operation to test. It generates one four-option MCQ per subtask in a single pass, including the stem, four options, the gold answer, and a distractor strategy label. The system prompt enforces the following global constraints: 

Global Rules. (1) Each MCQ has exactly 4 op-tions (A–D) with exactly one correct answer. (2) Distractors must be plausible but clearly wrong given the video evidence. (3) Anti-information-leakage: the question stem + options alone must not be sufficient to determine the correct answer without watching the video. (4) Anti-shortcut: distribute correct answers roughly evenly across A–D; include distractors that would fool a model relying only on audio, only on vision, or only on common sense. (5) Evidence-based: the cor-rect answer must be verifiable from the structured description. (6) Questions must require under-standing the video, not just text. 

Option Length Parity. All four options must be approximately equal in length (within ±20% of the average option length in characters). The correct answer must not be systematically longer or more detailed than distractors. 

Distractor Quality. Distractors must share the same grammatical structure, level of specificity, and concreteness as the correct answer. Never use absolute language (“no”, “never”, “always”) or extreme scenarios. Never use hedging language that contrasts with concrete correct answers. 

Anti-Transcript-Leakage. The question stem must not quote or closely paraphrase specific speech content from the clip. The correct answer should depend on visual information that cannot be inferred from the audio transcript alone. Each subtask receives dedicated instructions specifying the target cognitive operation, the ev-idence fields to focus on, and the distractor de-sign strategy. For example, the Action Identi-fication (AI) subtask instructs the model to fo-cus on screen_events and generate distractors from plausible but non-occurring action types; the Causal Attribution (CA) subtask instructs the model to focus on temporal_dynamics and gener-ate distractors that reverse causal order or confuse co-occurrence with causation. 

B.3 Generate-Judge-Regenerate Loop 

Each clip undergoes up to three iterations of a closed-loop quality cycle: 1. Generate : GPT-5 produces one MCQ per pend-ing subtask. 2. Judge : Each MCQ passes through three sequen-tial checks (described below). Failed MCQs receive structured feedback identifying the spe-cific violation. 3. Regenerate : Failed MCQs are regenerated with the original prompt augmented by the judge feedback, which specifies what to fix. MCQs that pass all three checks are accepted; those that fail after three iterations are discarded. 

Phase 1: Programmatic quality checks. Before any API call, each MCQ is checked for: (a) op-tion length ratio (max/min ≤ 1.2); (b) absence of absolute language in distractors; (c) valid JSON structure with exactly four options; (d) correct an-swer present among options. Items failing these checks are immediately returned for regeneration with specific feedback. 

Phase 2: Shortcut detection. GLM-5V and Claude-Haiku-4.5 receive only the question stem and four options, without video, ASR transcript, or evidence description. Each model selects an answer and reports confidence and any detected shortcut type (length bias, absolute language, se-mantic outlier, common sense, grammatical cue). If both validators answer correctly, the item is flagged as containing an option-level shortcut and returned for regeneration.

D Benchmark Statistics 

Clip and item statistics. Across 1,005 clips, the mean duration is 32.4 s (SD = 11.4 s; 25th/50th/75th percentiles: 23/30/40 s; 94% within the 15–60 s design window). Each clip has between 1 and 3 MCQs (mean 1.32): 72% have exactly 1, 24% have 2, and 4% have 3. Multiple MCQs on the same clip always target different subtasks. 

Cross-lingual characteristics. All source ses-sions are online English tutoring, where the teacher instructs in English while adapting explanations to students with diverse first-language backgrounds (primarily Mandarin Chinese, with some English L1 students). Teacher speech naturally includes code-switching between English instruction and native-language clarification. The shared screen content is predominantly in English (instructional materials, exercises, vocabulary). The source corpus contains over 1,000 recorded sessions (each 1–2 hours); the 1,005 clips are drawn from sessions selected for instructional richness and interaction density. Students include both Chinese L1 and English L1 learners at primary and secondary levels. Teacher speech is approxi-mately 75% English and 25% other languages by word count (estimated from ASR output). ASR is performed with Whisper-large-v3, which handles code-switched multilingual input. Because all sessions share the same instructional format (English-medium online tutoring), the pri-mary variation is in student L1 background and teacher code-switching patterns rather than in the language of instruction itself. We acknowledge the limited diversity of instructional contexts as a scope limitation in §6. 

E Evaluation Protocol 

All models use provider-default generation param-eters (temperature and top-p are not overridden). Video clips are uploaded at native resolution (720p, 15–60 s duration) and sampled at 1 frame per sec-ond across all providers. The ASR transcript is provided as a separate text field in the prompt. All models are queried via their respective provider APIs in a zero-shot setting with no system prompt. 

Inference prompt templates. Under the default Video+ASR condition, models receive the video clip together with the ASR transcript provided as a separate text input. The transcript is not burned into the video frames; models read it from the prompt text. The prompt is:     

> Watch the video clip and read the speech tran-script, then answer the question. Use the transcript as spoken-language evidence; use the video for visual context. ## Speech Transcript {transcript} ## Question {stem} ## Options A. {option_A} B. {option_B} C. {option_C} D. {option_D} Return ONLY valid JSON: {"answer": "<A/B/C/D>", "confidence": "high |medium |low", "reasoning": "brief explanation"}

Under the ASR T EXT ONLY condition, the video is removed entirely and only the speech tran-script is provided:     

> Read the speech transcript from a tutoring clip, then answer the question. You do NOT have ac-cess to the video. ## Speech Transcript {transcript} ## Question {stem} ## Options A. {option_A} . . . D. {option_D} Return ONLY valid JSON: {"answer": "<A/B/C/D>", "confidence": . . . }

Under the V ISUAL SHUFFLE WITH FIXED ASR condition, the video segments are permuted at VCE-detected scene boundaries before upload while the original transcript remains fixed. The prompt is identical to the Video+ASR condition; models are not informed that the temporal order has been altered. Under the SYNCHRONIZED VIDEO +ASR SHUFFLE condition, each visual segment is paired with its timestamp-aligned ASR span and the pairs are permuted together. Order within each pair is preserved. This retains local video-text alignment while disrupting the original intervention, response, and follow-up sequence. Under the S TATIC FRAME condition, 5 evenly-spaced key frames are extracted from the clip and presented as images:     

> Look at these 5 key frames extracted from a video clip of an online tutoring session, then answer the question. ## Question {stem} ## Options A. {option_A} . . . D. {option_D} Return ONLY valid JSON: {"answer": "<A/B/C/D>", "confidence": . . . }

Response parsing and scoring. Models are in-structed to return a JSON object containing an an-swer letter (A–D), a confidence level, and a brief reasoning string. We first attempt JSON extraction via regex; if parsing fails, we fall back to extract-ing a bare option letter or matching the normalized response to the option text. Responses that match neither pattern are scored as incorrect. After fall-back parsing, all models have zero invalid outputs under the full condition. Option order is random-ized independently for each model–item pair using a fixed random seed, and the gold label is remapped accordingly. For API-based models, responses in-dicating the model did not receive the video trig-ger up to 3 automatic retries before being marked as failures. Accuracy is computed as the propor-tion of items matching the gold answer (no partial credit; random baseline 25%). We report accuracy at three levels: overall (1,322 items), layer-level (Observable Evidence Grounding, Interaction Con-tingency Reasoning, Support-Response Modeling), and subtask-level (eight subtasks), computed di-rectly over items in each group. 

Answer-label diagnostics. The remapped gold labels are balanced with 330 A labels, 331 B labels, 331 C labels, and 330 D labels. Table 8 reports model output labels under the full condition. The distributions show no output collapse and zero in-valid responses. On the 924 errors shared by the two 32B Qwen3-VL variants, the models choose the same distractor content in 47.6% of cases af-ter independent option shuffling. A permutation test gives p < 0.001 , which is consistent with re-lated models favoring the same plausible distractors rather than a fixed answer position. 

Statistical testing. For the evidence dependency analysis, we apply McNemar’s test for paired bi-nary outcomes, comparing item-level accuracy un-der the Video+ASR condition against each per-turbation condition at the layer level. We apply Holm correction for multiple comparisons and re-port adjusted p-values with significance thresholds at α = 0 .05 , 0.01 , and 0.001 .

F Human Reference Details 

The 200-question human evaluation set is stratified by subtask (25 questions × 8 subtasks), sampled uniformly at random from the full 1,322-question set. Three domain experts with graduate training in applied linguistics independently answered all 200 questions after watching the corresponding video clips with ASR transcripts. Table 9 reports individual and majority-vote accuracy. Using the Wilson score interval at 95% confi-dence: Observable Evidence Grounding: 92.0% [83.6%, 96.3%]; Interaction Contingency Reason-ing: 88.0% [78.7%, 93.6%]; Support-Response Modeling: 94.0% [83.8%, 97.9%]; Overall: 91.0% [86.2%, 94.2%]. The lower bound of the overall CI (86.2%) remains substantially above the best model (61.3%), confirming the human–model gap is ro-bust. Individual expert totals are 175, 170, and 177 out of 200 (mean 87.0%); majority voting raises ac-curacy to 91.0%, indicating that disagreements are resolved by at least two experts agreeing. Pairwise agreement on the binary correct/incorrect decision exceeds 90% across all expert pairs, with agree-ment highest on Observable Evidence Grounding items and lowest on Interaction Contingency Rea-soning Causal Attribution items. This sample size is comparable to human evaluation protocols in recent video understanding benchmarks (Li et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025). 

G Paired Significance Analysis of Evidence Dependency 

To determine whether the observed performance changes are statistically reliable, we conduct two sided McNemar tests using paired item level cor-rectness outcomes. For each model and diagnostic layer, we compare predictions under the Full set-ting against predictions under ASR Text Only and 

Visual Shuffle with Fixed ASR on the same set of questions. ASR Text Only removes visual evi-dence while retaining the standardized ASR input. Visual Shuffle with Fixed ASR retains the ASR input and visual content, but disrupts the tempo-ral organization of the visual stream. We apply Holm correction across all comparisons reported in Table 10. The significance analysis supports a selective ev-idence dependency pattern across diagnostic layers. Removing visual input produces the largest degra-dation on OEG for every evaluated model, with drops ranging from 12.7 to 21.9 percentage points. All OEG declines under ASR Text Only remain sig-nificant after correction. This result indicates that observable teacher actions, learner behaviors, and shared screen content cannot be reliably recovered from ASR input alone. Visual Shuffle with Fixed ASR produces a com-plementary effect. Its largest degradation consis-tently occurs on ICR, with drops ranging from 11.9 to 19.1 percentage points, all significant after cor-rection. Because the ASR input remains unchanged in this condition, the ICR decline reflects the loss of visual temporal organization required to connect teacher intervention, learner response, and teacher follow up. In contrast, Visual Shuffle with Fixed ASR has no significant adverse effect on OEG for four of the five evaluated models, suggesting that the perturbation largely preserves access to local observable evidence while disrupting relation bear-ing temporal structure. SRM exhibits a less uniform response to the two perturbations. Most SRM changes are not signifi-cant after correction, with the exception of larger model specific effects such as the degradation un-der ASR Text Only for Gemini 3 Flash and under Visual Shuffle with Fixed ASR for Kimi-K2.5. This mixed pattern is consistent with the design of SRM, which requires predictive or evaluative judgments supported by multiple forms of evidence, including visual context, language cues, learner state, and broader instructional priors.

H Detailed Control Results I Positioning Against Causal Video QA 

VIRA-Bench differs from existing causal video QA benchmarks in three respects. First, causal video QA benchmarks (Xiao et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022) primarily test physical causality or event-level “why” ques-tions in third-person observation settings. VIRA-Bench tests interactional causality: whether one participant’s action triggers another’s response and shapes subsequent behavior. Second, VIRA-Bench grounds its diagnostic layers in established peda-gogical theory (IRF structure, contingent teaching), providing a principled decomposition rather than ad-hoc question categories. Third, VIRA-Bench in-cludes controlled evidence perturbation (ASR-only, temporal shuffle) as part of the evaluation proto-col, enabling construct validity analysis beyond aggregate accuracy. 

J Transcript De-identification 

Spoken names can remain in ASR transcripts af-ter visual and metadata de-identification. We therefore process all 1,005 released transcripts with multilingual named-entity recognition and rules for vocatives and honorific-plus-name con-structions in English and Mandarin. The pro-cess detects 1,340 person mentions. Each men-tion is replaced with [TEACHER] , [STUDENT] , or 

[SPEAKER] when the role cannot be determined. Replacement rather than deletion preserves who ad-dresses whom, which can be relevant to Sequence Recognition and Causal Attribution. The released transcripts are de-identified versions of the raw Whisper-large-v3 output used during construction. No subtask asks for identity and no name is used as answer-bearing evidence.

Composition. The benchmark contains 1,322 four-option multiple-choice questions spanning 1,005 video clips from online tutoring sessions (duration statistics in Appendix D). Questions are distributed across three diagnostic layers (Observ-able Evidence Grounding: 497, Interaction Contin-gency Reasoning: 529, Support-Response Model-ing: 296) and eight subtasks. All clips depict real instructional interactions; no synthetic or staged content is included. The dataset does not contain offensive content. All faces are blurred. 

Collection process. Source videos are online tu-toring sessions recorded by a commercial educa-tional institution between 2018 and 2021. Sessions involve English-medium instruction with Chinese L1 students at primary and secondary levels. In-formed consent for educational and research use was obtained from all participants’ parents or legal guardians.
