Abstract. Early-warning models in learning analytics can identify learn-ers at risk of failure or withdrawal, but the resulting alerts are often only weakly connected to inspectable learning evidence. A risk score may indicate that intervention is needed without showing which traces, as-sessment events, or temporal patterns justify the alert. This paper intro-duces an incremental Learner Digital Twin for evidence-grounded early warning, implemented as a lightweight knowledge graph. The twin is up-dated at temporal checkpoints by replaying learning traces and assess-ment records, and it serves both as a feature-extraction substrate and as an explanation-grounding substrate. Predictive features are derived through graph-mediated queries, while features, attributions, alerts, ev-idence items, and raw records are represented as queryable graph enti-ties. The framework is instantiated and evaluated on a public learning-analytics benchmark under a temporal protocol. The results show that graph-mediated feature extraction preserves the predictive behavior of equivalent raw-table features across model families and checkpoints. Gen-erated alerts are materialized as evidence chains linking risk decisions to feature attributions, evidence items, and raw platform records. The study shows that early-warning prediction can be routed through a se-mantic and provenance-aware representation while preserving predictive performance and adding traceability, verifiability, and evidence-grounded explanation. 

Keywords: Learner digital twin · Learning analytics · Early warning · Knowledge graph · Explainability · Provenance 

## 1 Introduction 

Web-based learning platforms continuously record learner activity, including resource accesses, submissions, scores, registration events, assessment results, and withdrawals. Learning analytics has built on these traces to develop early-warning systems that estimate, before the end of a course, which learners may arXiv:2610.36820v1 [cs.AI] 27 Oct 2026 be at risk of failing or dropping out [1, 12]. Such systems can support timely pedagogical intervention, but their usefulness depends on more than predictive accuracy. For an alert to be actionable, instructors also need to understand which learning traces, assessment events, or temporal patterns justify the risk signal. The predictive dimension of early-warning learning analytics is now rela-tively well served. Tree-based ensembles, gradient-boosting methods, and neural architectures can achieve strong discrimination when trained on interaction and assessment features [19, 31]. However, the explanatory status of the resulting alerts remains limited. In a typical pipeline, raw platform logs are transformed into tabular features, a predictive model assigns a risk score, and a post-hoc attribution method such as SHAP [24] may indicate that an engineered vari-able, for example total_clicks , contributed to the prediction. Such feature-level explanations are useful for model inspection, but they often stop short of what instructors need for pedagogical interpretation and action. An instructor-facing alert should not only indicate that a learner is at risk; it should also make ex-plicit which learning evidence supports this claim. A feature attribution names an engineered quantity, but it does not necessarily identify the concrete learning events behind it: which interactions decreased, which assessment was missed, which raw records substantiate the alert, and whether the explanation can be queried, traced, or contested. This creates a gap between predictive performance and evidence-grounded interpretability, a limitation emphasized in explainable learning analytics and AI-in-education research [17]. We address the following problem: given a stream of timestamped learning traces and assessment records, how can an early-warning system produce risk alerts that are not only predictive, but also evidence-grounded, queryable, and traceable? To this end, we introduce an incremental Learner Digital Twin (ldt )implemented as a lightweight knowledge graph (KG). The twin is updated at suc-cessive temporal checkpoints by replaying learning traces and assessment data in chronological order, thereby simulating the recurrent synchronization that a ldt 

would perform in a live learning platform. The central design principle is that the graph is not a post-hoc explanatory layer added after prediction; instead, it acts as the operational substrate for feature construction, prediction, attribu-tion representation, and evidence verification. At each checkpoint, learner feature vectors are extracted from the twin using a graph-mediated query catalogue and passed to an early-warning classifier. The resulting prediction features, feature attributions, generated alerts, and evidence items are then materialized back into the graph, with each evidence item linked to raw learning records following a provenance-aware modeling perspective inspired by PROV-O [22]. Graph in-tegrity is checked using SHACL validation [18], so that explanation chains can be verified as graph properties. The framework is later instantiated and evaluated on the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD) [19]. The objective is not to improve predictive accuracy, but to show that early-warning prediction can be routed through a semantic and provenance-aware rep-resentation while preserving predictive performance and making alerts evidence-grounded, queryable, and traceable. The paper makes three contributions: it in-troduces an incremental knowledge-graph ldt synchronized at temporal check-points from learning traces and assessment data; it represents predictive fea-tures, feature attributions, alerts, evidence items, and raw records as first-class graph entities; and it empirically verifies that graph-mediated feature extrac-tion preserves the behavior of an equivalent raw-table pipeline while enabling SHACL-validated evidence grounding and traceability. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on early warning, explainable learning analytics, learner digital twins, and semantic provenance. Section 3 presents the knowledge-graph ldt . Section 4 describes the experimental setup and results. Section 5 discusses implications and limitations. Section 6 concludes the paper. 

## 2 Related Work 

We review evidence-grounded early warning in learning analytics. We focus on three areas: early-warning and explainable learning analytics, learner model-ing and digital twins, and knowledge graphs for provenance and traceability. Early-warning systems are among the most established applications of learn-ing analytics. They use traces of learner activity, assessment behavior, regis-tration information, and other platform data to identify learners who may fail, withdraw, or require support before the end of a course. Operational systems such as Course Signals [2] and OU Analyse [13] illustrate how predictive learn-ing analytics has been deployed as institutional decision support. In parallel, public benchmark datasets have enabled reproducible experimentation on early risk prediction using activity and assessment traces [19]. Related work has also examined leakage-excluded early-warning prediction under strict temporal con-straints [21] and semantics-constrained counterfactual recourse for educational decision support [20]. Most work in this area has focused on predictive perfor-mance, timeliness, and deployment feasibility. Explainability is particularly important in learning analytics because predic-tions may inform pedagogical decisions, learner support, or institutional inter-ventions. Post-hoc explanation methods such as LIME and SHAP are commonly used because they attribute individual predictions to input features without re-quiring a fully transparent model architecture [27, 24]. However, a feature-level attribution remains incomplete if the instructor cannot inspect which resources were accessed, which assessment was missed, or which raw records substantiate the feature. The issue is therefore not only whether an attribution is locally faith-ful to the model, but whether the attributed feature is grounded in interpretable educational evidence. Learner modeling has long addressed the representation of learner states. Overlay models, open learner models, and adaptive educational systems rep-resent learners’ knowledge, progress, preferences, goals, and interaction histo-ries [4]. Ontology-based learner models further contribute semantic structure and interoperability by representing learners, contexts, resources, and educa-tional situations through explicit conceptual models [28]. However, many learner models are built from a given snapshot of learner data or are used primarily as profiles for adaptation and visualization. They are less often operationalized as time-indexed representations that are recurrently synchronized with platform traces and directly connected to predictive alert generation. The notion of a digital twin originated in product lifecycle management, where it refers to a virtual counterpart of a physical asset connected to the real system through data flows [10]. Work in smart manufacturing emphasizes that a digital twin is not merely a static model, but a data-driven representa-tion updated from observations and used to support analysis, simulation, and decision-making [29, 30]. When transposed to education, the twinned entity may be a learner, a course, or a learning environment [11]. In this paper, we use the term in a restricted operational sense: a checkpoint-indexed learner repre-sentation synchronized by replayed platform observations and used to support prediction and evidence-grounded explanation. Ontologies and semantic web technologies have been used to structure learner information, educational resources, competencies, contexts, and learning activ-ities. Early standards such as IMS LIP provided foundations for learner profile interoperability [15]. Educational knowledge graphs and linked learning resources have also shown how semantic representations can support search, recommenda-tion, integration, and reasoning over educational data [5, 7]. These approaches demonstrate the value of semantic modeling, but they generally focus on repre-senting learners, resources, activities, or competencies rather than the internal objects produced by a predictive early-warning pipeline. Provenance modeling provides the conceptual and technical basis for con-necting analytic outputs to their sources. The W3C PROV model represents entities, activities, agents, and derivation relations, allowing data products to be linked to the processes and sources from which they were generated [22, 25]. SHACL complements this perspective by providing a standard mechanism for validating structural constraints in RDF graphs [18]. In early-warning learning analytics, however, provenance is often associated with datasets or processing workflows, while the explanatory structure of individual alerts remains weakly represented. Existing approaches rarely integrate learner states, predictive fea-tures, attributions, alerts, evidence, and raw records into a single representation that can be jointly queried and traced. 

## 3 Methodology 

This section presents the incremental Learner Digital Twin for evidence-grounded early warning. We define the temporal prediction problem, introduce the checkpoint-indexed graph, and describe the ontology, synchronization, fea-ture extraction, prediction, attribution mapping, and evidence grounding. 

3.1 Problem Formulation

3.2 Overview of the Incremental Learner Digital Twin Definition 1 (Learner Digital Twin). A Learner Digital Twin is a time-indexed digital representation of a learner, synchronized from observed learning-platform data to support analysis, prediction, explanation, and traceability. Fol-lowing the general view of digital twins as data-driven representations connected to observed systems [10, 29, 30], and their adaptation to educational settings [11], this paper operationalizes the learner twin as a checkpoint-indexed knowledge graph Gτi .

Figure 1 summarizes the workflow implemented at each checkpoint. Tempo-rally available records are first mapped into the ldt graph. The graph is then queried to construct checkpoint-specific feature vectors, which are passed to the predictive function. If the estimated risk exceeds the alert threshold, a risk alert is generated. The alert is then linked to feature attributions, predictive features, evidence items, and the raw records from which the evidence was derived. Thus, the same graph-based representation supports both prediction and traceability. 

3.3 Ontology and Evidence Path 

The ldt ontology is designed as a lightweight and task-oriented model for evidence-grounded early warning. It does not aim to cover all aspects of learner modeling or educational knowledge representation. Instead, it contains the classes and properties required to represent the entities involved in the early-warning workflow: platform observations, predictive features, attributions, alerts, evi-dence items, checkpoints, and raw records. Consistent with Eq. (8), the ontology is organized into an observation layer and an analytic layer. The observation layer represents entities directly derived from platform data, including Learner , Course , LearningResource , LearningTrace , Assessment , As-sessmentResult , and RegistrationEvent . Each observation-layer entity derived from a platform row is linked to a RawRecord individual through ldto:fromRecord .The RawRecord class stores the source table or stream name and record identi-fier, thereby anchoring the graph in the original data. The analytic layer represents the objects produced by the early-warning pipeline. It includes PredictionFeature , FeatureAttribution , RiskAlert , Eviden-ceItem , PredictionRun , and Checkpoint . A PredictionFeature represents one fea-ture value for one learner at one checkpoint and is linked to the evidence items that support its computation. A FeatureAttribution stores the contribution of a feature to a specific alert. A RiskAlert represents the generated risk decision and is linked to the prediction run, the corresponding checkpoint, the relevant attributions, and the evidence selected for presentation. An EvidenceItem represents a typed and human-readable statement sup-porting a predictive feature. It may correspond to activity evidence, assessment evidence, temporal evidence, absence evidence, or another evidence type de-fined by the application. Each evidence item carries a textual description, a strength value, a temporal scope, and one or more links to RawRecord individu-als through ldto:derivedFrom . ## 4 Experimentation 

Our experimental study addresses three research questions: RQ1 whether the 

ldt can be incrementally constructed and validated at temporal checkpoints; 

RQ2 whether graph-mediated feature extraction preserves the predictive be-havior of an equivalent raw-table feature pipeline; and RQ3 whether generated alerts are grounded in traceable evidence chains. The experimental instantiation uses a compact feature catalogue covering activity indicators, resource-use counts, assessment progress, temporal behavior, and absence-based signals such as no activity or no submitted assessment before a checkpoint. The same feature definitions are used in both experimental variants. 

4.1 Dataset and Temporal Protocol 

The empirical evaluation uses OULAD [19] as a public benchmark for early-warning learning analytics. The dataset covers 22 presentations of 7 modules, 32,593 students, and about 10.6 million VLE interaction records. Its day-based temporal structure and its inclusion of registrations, assessments, and final out-comes make it suitable for early-warning prediction and evidence-grounded alert generation. Following common practice, learners who withdrew before the presentation start ( date_unregistration ≤ 0) are excluded, since no in-course behavior exists to twin. Learners with no VLE activity and no assessment record before the first checkpoint are retained; their emptiness is itself predictive and generates absence evidence. After preprocessing, six modules have at least one 2013 presentation for training and one 2014 presentation for testing. 4.3 Results 

All results reported in this section are obtained from the OULAD instantia-tion described above. 

RQ1 - Incremental construction and validation: Table 2 reports the incremental construction results, averaged over the six test modules. The test graph contains 2,210 learners on average per module. The number of newly integrated traces de-creases over time because a large share of the available activity occurs before the first checkpoint: the update adds on average 148,560 traces at Week 4, 106,204 at Week 8, and 66,638 at Week 12. The persistent graph nevertheless grows monotonically, reaching 321,401 cumulative learning traces and 3.28M triples on average by Week 12. Both observation graphs and analytic graphs conform to the SHACL shapes at all checkpoints, with zero violations. These results show that the twin can be maintained incrementally while pre-serving the structural invariants required for traceable explanations. The graph grows as expected with newly available learning traces and assessment results, while SHACL validation confirms that generated evidence chains remain struc-turally valid. 

RQ2 - Predictive parity: Table 3 reports the KG-mediated early-warning per-formance on OULAD. The corresponding raw-table results are omitted for com-pactness because they are identical to the KG-mediated results for every model, checkpoint, and metric. Performance improves from Week 4 to Week 12 as more activity and assessment evidence becomes available. The strongest KG-mediated model is TABTX at Week 4 (AUC 0.735), XGB at Week 8 (AUC 0.809), and ET at Week 12 (AUC 0.847). The central result is therefore feature-substrate par-ity: graph-mediated feature extraction preserves the predictive behavior of the equivalent raw-table pipeline while adding provenance, validation, and evidence materialization.

## 5 Discussion 

The results lead to three findings. First, the ldt can be populated incre-mentally across checkpoints while preserving the structural constraints required for evidence-grounded alerts. The graph grows from Week 4 to Week 12, and SHACL validation reports no violations, indicating that the observation and analytic layers remain coherent over time. Second, the KG-mediated pipeline preserves the predictive behavior of the raw-table pipeline. The identical F1 and AUC values are expected because both variants use the same feature definitions; the value of the KG is not predictive improvement, but the explicit connection between features, evidence items, and raw records. Third, alerts are traceable through evidence chains. Direct coverage is lower for absence and inactivity fea-tures because these signals are grounded through contextual records, such as registrations or due-assessment metadata, rather than direct activity rows. A key methodological choice is to make the graph load-bearing rather than merely explanatory. If the graph were populated only after prediction, it would serve as an external explanation store and could diverge from the actual pre-processing pipeline. In contrast, the same representation is used here to derive predictive features and to preserve their evidential basis. This design reduces the risk of inconsistency between raw data processing, model input, and presented explanations. It also makes explanation completeness verifiable through SHACL constraints and graph queries. The use of the term Learner Digital Twin should be understood in a re-stricted operational sense. The study simulates synchronization by chronologi-cally replaying a historical dataset at predefined checkpoints. It does not evalu-ate live ingestion, out-of-order events, data correction, real-time system integra-tion, or instructor feedback loops. The implementation therefore demonstrates a checkpoint-indexed ldt with recurrent synchronization and analytic history, rather than a fully deployed live educational digital twin. 

## 6 Conclusion 

This paper introduced an incremental knowledge-graph Learner Digital Twin for evidence-grounded early warning in learning analytics. The approach routes prediction through a semantic representation in which learner observations, pre-dictive features, feature attributions, risk alerts, evidence items, and raw records are explicitly represented as graph entities. The ldt therefore serves both as a feature-extraction substrate and as an evidence-grounding substrate. The em-pirical instantiation on OULAD shows that graph-mediated feature extraction preserves the predictive behavior of equivalent raw-table features across model families and checkpoints. At the same time, generated alerts can be linked back to feature attributions, evidence items, and raw platform records through queryable evidence chains. These results suggest that semantic provenance can make early-warning pipelines more transparent, traceable, and verifiable with-out reducing predictive performance. Future work will extend the learner twin toward competency-aware learner states, evaluate the framework in live learn-ing platforms, and study whether evidence-grounded alerts improve instructors’ understanding, trust, and intervention decisions.
