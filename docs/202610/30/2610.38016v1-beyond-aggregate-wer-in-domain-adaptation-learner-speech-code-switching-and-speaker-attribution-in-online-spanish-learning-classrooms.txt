Abstract 

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) increas-ingly supplies transcripts for educational NLP, but aggregate word error rate (WER) can con-ceal errors concentrated in learner speech, multilingual turns, and speaker attribution. We compare platform-integrated, open-weight, adapted, and commercial ASR configurations on held-out sessions of online L2 Spanish in-teraction between L1 English learners and their instructors and tutors. Because English is the learner’s shared first language, the interactions contain Spanish-only, English-only, and code-switched turns. Evaluation combines overall WER and character error rate (CER), WER dis-aggregated by speaker role and language con-text, and diarization error rate (DER). We also adapt Whisper large-v3 and MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize with LoRA using human-corrected in-domain classroom data. MOSS adaptation pro-duced consistent improvements across speaker roles and language contexts, whereas Whisper showed smaller gains. Domain match proved more important than data volume: Larger out-of-domain bilingual and learner corpora did not outperform the smaller classroom cor-pus, where learner speech and code-switching are embedded in instructor–learner interaction. The open-weight MOSS adapter was compet-itive with commercial Muse Voice Transcribe on WER and CER and achieved lower system-provided DER on the recordings eligible for each system. Learner, English-only, and code-switched speech remained more difficult than instructor and Spanish-only speech. The find-ings demonstrate the value of condition-aware evaluation and interactionally matched data for bilingual educational ASR.

1 Introduction 

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) increasingly supplies transcripts for educational technologies and downstream NLP applications, including ac-cessibility tools, spoken learner-error detection, proficiency assessment, feedback generation, and classroom-discourse analysis, so recognition errors affect not only readability but also any measure-ment or prediction based on the transcript. Authentic second-language (L2) classrooms are a demanding recognition environment. Learner speech may contain developing pronunciation, non-target forms, hesitations, repetitions, and repairs, and recordings involve multiple speakers, vari-able microphones, rapid turn-taking, and switches between the target language and another shared language. Instructors and learners also differ in proficiency, speaking time, and interactional role, so aggregate word error rate (WER) can obscure poorer performance on learner, English-only, or code-switched speech. Moreover, a lexically accu-rate transcript may be unsuitable for participation analysis if speech is assigned to the wrong speaker. Prior research has documented substantial ASR variation across educational recordings, accents, speakers, and recording conditions (Southwell et al., 2022; Kuhn et al., 2023; Graham and Roll, 2024). Matched learner data can improve recogni-tion (Kim et al., 2024), but conventional ASR may normalize precisely the learner forms needed for linguistic analysis or feedback (Michot et al., 2024; Koizumi, 2025). Code-switching introduces fur-ther language-confusion errors even in multilingual systems (Hillah et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025), and speaker attribution is a related but separate evalu-ation problem (von Neumann et al., 2023). These 1arXiv:2610.38016v1 [cs.CL] 27 Oct 2026 dimensions have generally been studied separately rather than within a single evaluation of authentic bilingual instructional interaction. We evaluate six ASR configurations on syn-chronous online Spanish L2 interaction: Zoom au-tomated captions, Whisper large-v3 (Radford et al., 2023), a Whisper large-v3 adapter trained with in-domain low-rank adaptation (LoRA; Hu et al., 2022), MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize (Yu et al., 2026), MOSS with LoRA adaptation, and Muse Voice Transcribe (Meta, 2026). The learners were L1 English speakers, and English was used alongside Spanish in learner production, explanations, and classroom management, so the interaction includes Spanish-only, English-only, and code-switched turns. Whisper and MOSS were adapted on 38 human-corrected Zoom transcripts (15.6 hours), with the recipe and training budget fixed by five-fold, learner-disjoint cross-validation before eval-uation (Appendix A). Evaluation uses seven held-out sessions from graded university classes and ungraded Language-Tutoring conversations. We report pooled WER and character error rate (CER), disaggregate WER by instructor versus learner role and by language context, and evaluate speaker attri-bution separately using diarization error rate (DER). Figure 1 summarizes the design. Adapters and de-identified transcripts will be released on request only; scoring code is in the supplementary material (Appendix B). The study makes three principal contributions: • We provide a condition-aware comparison of platform-integrated, open-weight, adapted, and commercial ASR configurations on au-thentic bilingual interaction from online L2 instructional settings. • We adapt Whisper and MOSS on the same in-domain corpus: gains are substantial and consistent for MOSS across roles and lan-guage contexts, modest for Whisper, and not improved by larger out-of-domain bilingual and learner corpora. • We show that Muse and the MOSS adapter form a stable leading pair across speaker roles and language contexts, while learner and mul-tilingual speech remain difficult and speaker attribution yields a different ranking, led by the open-weight MOSS adapter. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 L2 and Classroom ASR 

ASR performance on non-native speech varies with accent, first-language background, proficiency, and speech type (Graham and Roll, 2024), and sponta-neous learner speech adds developing pronuncia-tion, non-target forms, hesitations, repetitions, and repairs. Kim et al. (2024) showed that fine-tuning Whisper on spontaneous non-native English sub-stantially reduced WER, demonstrating the value of matched learner data. Lower WER, however, does not necessarily yield a more useful transcript for learner-focused NLP, because ASR systems may normalize a learner’s production to its presumed target form. Michot et al. (2024) therefore distinguish recognition accu-racy from learner-error preservation, and Koizumi (2025) documents similar normalization in adult L2 speech. These findings motivate preserving comprehensible non-target forms in our references. Classroom recordings add background noise, overlapping talk, and multiple speakers. South-well et al. (2022) found high error rates on middle-school collaborative speech, with errors affecting downstream discourse analysis. Attia et al. (2025) demonstrated that approximately 5,000 hours of noisy teacher–student transcripts could support classroom ASR adaptation when followed by accu-rate in-domain fine-tuning. However, prior studies either focus on student speech or treat teacher and student speech as one classroom domain, and do not directly compare instructor and learner WER within the same interactions. We address this gap through role-conditioned evaluation. 

2.2 Code-Switched ASR and Adaptation 

Multilingual pretraining does not eliminate lan-guage confusion in code-switched speech. Ma-terial in the locally non-dominant language may be deleted, translated, or replaced with words from the dominant language. For Spanish–English speech, Hillah et al. (2024) showed that jointly fine-tuning Whisper for language identification and transcrip-tion improved code-switched recognition. Dhawan et al. (2023) incorporated token-level language identification into English–Spanish and English– Hindi ASR, while Yang et al. (2023) found that relatively small quantities of matched Mandarin– English data substantially improved Whisper. 2.3 Role-Conditioned and Speaker-Aware Evaluation 

Aggregate WER can conceal differences across speakers and conditions. Imai et al. (2025) found substantial variation across conversational datasets, acoustic conditions, and speaker characteristics, while Rai et al. (2025) showed that performance disparities vary across demographic groups. In-structional role introduces another potentially im-portant distinction because instructor and learner speech support different educational and NLP ap-plications. Lexical accuracy also does not indicate whether speech was attributed to the correct participant. von Neumann et al. (2023) provide speaker-aware mea-sures that jointly evaluate transcription and speaker assignment, whereas DER isolates speaker confu-sion, missed speech, and false alarms. The two perspectives are complementary: a system may rec-ognize the words accurately while assigning them to the wrong speaker. Previous research has therefore examined learner speech, classroom adaptation, code-switching, con-ditioned evaluation, and diarization largely as sepa-rate problems; the present study combines them. 

3 Dataset 

3.1 Corpus and Data Splits 

The corpus consists of synchronous online Span-ish L2 interaction recorded over two academic terms. It includes graded, task-oriented classes led by trained instructors and ungraded Language-Tutoring conversations led by native Spanish speak-ers. Classroom sessions contained structured activi-ties, assessment, corrective feedback, presentations, and media playback; Language-Tutoring sessions were organized around tutor-selected conversation topics. Whisper and MOSS were adapted on 38 sessions representing 15.6 hours of interaction, and evalua-tion used seven sessions excluded from adaptation, representing approximately 3.51 hours (Table 1). The adaptation sessions were approximately 25 minutes long. Because the corpus consisted pre-dominantly of beginner-level, instructor-led interac-tion, instructor speech generally exceeded learner speech, and Spanish was the primary language. 3The 38 adaptation sessions contained 101,678 reference words before filler removal, 76,998 (75.7%) from classroom sessions and 24,680 (24.3%) from Language-Tutoring sessions. Learn-ers produced about 24% of the words overall, 21.2% in the classroom sessions and 34.3% in Language-Tutoring. By language context, 58.3% of the words occurred in Spanish-only turns, 33.1% in code-switched turns, and 8.6% in English-only turns (Section 4.3). The share of English words per session ranged from 1.1% to 62.7%, with a median of 15.6%. Instructors and learners used English at almost the same rate, 21.5% and 21.8% of their words. The evaluation sessions contained a smaller share of code-switched words (18.3%, Section 5.1.3). 

3.2 Reference Transcripts 

Transcribers proficient in English and Spanish pro-duced the primary transcripts by manually correct-ing Zoom-generated transcripts. Transcribers cor-rected recognition errors, added omitted speech, removed spurious material, and verified speaker attribution, code-switching, repetitions, incomplete words, and overlapping speech. Unintelligible material was marked explicitly. Comprehensible learner pronunciations and non-target forms were preserved rather than normalized because such forms are relevant to learner-focused NLP. Com-plete annotation conventions are provided in Ap-pendix C.1. To test the correction method, independent anno-tators transcribed a small audio subset directly from the recordings, without Zoom or other ASR output (Appendix C.1). The human-corrected transcripts agreed closely with these audio-first transcripts, in-dicating that correction introduces no systematic differences from what was spoken (Appendix C.2). We therefore use the human-corrected Zoom tran-scripts, which cover all evaluation sessions. 

4 Methods 

4.1 ASR Systems and Adaptation 

We evaluated Zoom automated captions, Whisper large-v3 (Radford et al., 2023), MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize (Yu et al., 2026), and Muse Voice Tran-scribe (Meta, 2026), a commercial service accessed through its API, together with Whisper large-v3 adapter and MOSS adapter, the same two models with an in-domain LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2022) trained on the 38 sessions described in Section 3.1. We used LoRA because the adapted models must continue to recognize English as well as Spanish, and full fine-tuning on 15.6 hours of speech can overwrite that ability: LoRA forgets less of the orig-inal model than full fine-tuning (Biderman et al., 2024), and it has been used to add languages or do-mains to Whisper without degrading the languages already covered (Song et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). MOSS was selected as the second open-weight model because it transcribes and attributes speak-ers in a single pass over a complete recording. 

Adapter configuration. Both adapters used rank 

r = 64 , scaling parameter α = 128 , and dropout 0.1. In MOSS, LoRA was applied to every at-tention and feed-forward projection of the audio encoder and the language decoder, giving 68.7 million trainable parameters (7.5% of the model), while the projector between the two components remained frozen. In Whisper, LoRA was applied to the same projections, giving approximately 115 million trainable parameters (7.4%). Both were trained with PEFT and the Hugging Face Trainer on one NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPU, at a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 selected on development data. A MOSS training example was a complete recording and a Whisper example was a window of at most 30 seconds of contiguous audio, a difference that carried over to batch size, schedule, and checkpoint selection (Appendix A.1). 

Training budget and model selection. The training budget was selected by five-fold cross-validation over the 38 adaptation sessions, with sessions grouped so that no learner appeared in both the training and the test portion of a fold; each fold trained on approximately 28 sessions and se-lected its checkpoint on two development sessions. For MOSS, each fold trained for six epochs; four of the five folds selected the final checkpoint, 252 to 264 steps, and one selected epoch 4, 176 steps, the two differing by less than one point of development WER in every fold. The final adapter was therefore trained for 264 steps, the budget selected by most folds, which on the 57 examples of the full set is 4.6 passes. For Whisper, the budget was 330 steps, the mean of the best steps of the five folds. The final checkpoint, step 275, was chosen on windows of its own training data, without evaluation sessions (Appendix A.1). Appendix A.2 reports the cross-validated effect. 4.2 Inference and Processing 

Whisper processed reference-defined speaker turns, whereas MOSS and Muse processed longer seg-ments or complete recordings; Zoom retained its original meeting segmentation. Whisper therefore received reference-derived turn boundaries, which removes segmentation errors from its score, and the results compare complete system configurations rather than model architectures alone. Whisper detected the language automatically at inference rather than being forced to Spanish. Outputs were ordered temporally and aligned with each reference as a continuous sequence. References and hypotheses underwent identi-cal normalization: text was lowercased, punctu-ation and nonevaluated annotations were removed, and numerical expressions were converted to spo-ken forms. Diacritics and markers representing contractions or incomplete words were retained. For the primary analysis, standardized filler vari-ants were removed from both references and hy-potheses because filler-only turns could not be as-signed reliable language-context labels and destabi-lized sequence alignment, while repetitions, repairs, fragments, and incomplete words were preserved. The complete filler inventory is provided in Ap-pendix D. 

4.3 Language Classification 

The classifier assigned each token to Spanish or English by comparing its Zipf frequency in the two languages (Speer, 2022). Tokens whose frequency difference fell below a margin of 0.75 were classi-fied as ambiguous. Named entities and established loanwords were identified using curated lists; re-maining tokens without sufficient evidence were classified as unknown. Ambiguous tokens did not contribute evidence for either language. Turns containing Spanish but no English evi-dence were classified as Spanish-only, those con-taining English but no Spanish evidence as English-only, and those containing evidence from both languages as code-switched. Turns with no evi-dence for either language were excluded from the language-context analyses. Manual review of one complete transcript found 265 of 271 turn classi-fications to be correct (97.79%). The tagger and accompanying word lists are provided in the sup-plementary material (Appendix B); 

4.4 Transcription Evaluation 

We report WER and CER based on standard edit-distance alignment (Morris et al., 2004). Error counts and reference units were summed across recordings before each rate was calculated; session-level percentages were not averaged. Intervals con-taining external media without corresponding refer-ence speech were excluded. The normalization and scoring scripts are provided in the supplementary material (Appendix B). WER and CER were calculated overall, and WER was additionally disaggregated by speaker role and language context. Substitutions and dele-tions inherited the category of the corresponding reference token, while insertions were assigned to the preceding reference category. Reference-word and turn counts accompany the condition-specific results. 

Uncertainty estimation. We estimated uncer-tainty in overall WER using a session-level boot-strap, treating the seven evaluation sessions as the sampling units (Koehn, 2004). For each of 10,000 bootstrap replicates, seven sessions were sampled with replacement, and pooled WER was recom-puted by dividing the total number of substitutions, deletions, and insertions across the sampled ses-sions by the corresponding total number of refer-ence words. We report 95% percentile-bootstrap confidence intervals based on the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting WER distributions.

5.1.3 Performance by Language Context 

Table 4 reports WER for Spanish-only, English-only, and code-switched speech. The language-context subset covers all 2,051 role-labeled turns: 1,635 Spanish-only turns with 13,064 reference words (77.5%), 251 code-switched turns with 3,086 words (18.3%), and 165 English-only turns with 716 words (4.2%). System rankings were generally consistent across language contexts. Muse obtained the lowest WER for code-switched and English-only speech, at 20.41% and 22.93%, respectively, and also ob-tained the lowest Spanish-only WER at 14.05%. The MOSS adapter followed closely across all three language contexts, with 21.44% WER for code-switched speech, 14.77% for Spanish-only speech, and 24.14% for English-only speech. Zoom cap-tions showed the highest error rates across the three conditions, reaching 47.20% on code-switched speech and 54.55% on English-only speech. Adaptation effects were more strongly differen-tiated by model configuration than by speaker role or language context. MOSS adaptation produced substantial improvements across both roles and all three language contexts whereas Whisper adap-tation produced consistently smaller gains. The joint role–language analysis showed the same pat-tern: Muse obtained the lowest WER in all six conditions, followed closely by the MOSS adapter, and both adapters improved all six conditions, with considerably larger reductions for MOSS (Ap-pendix E). Both adapters were trained on the same 38-session in-domain corpus, so the contrast cannot be attributed to differences in adaptation-data quantity or composition. The configurations nevertheless differed in architecture, training segmentation, and inference procedure: Whisper processed shorter, reference-defined segments, whereas MOSS was trained and evaluated on longer recordings or com-plete sessions. The results therefore establish a configuration-level difference in adaptation effec-tiveness; development experiments point to the decoding regime as one such factor. In cross-validation, the Whisper adapter gained 4.54 points when decoded in 30-second windows, its training regime, but lost the gain under long-form decoding, whereas MOSS, which decodes complete record-ings, kept its gain (Appendix A.4). Because instructor speech and Spanish-only speech constitute most of the evaluation words, ag-gregate WER could primarily reflect performance on these relatively easier majority conditions. The disaggregated results show instead that the advan-tages of Muse and the MOSS adapter extend to learner, code-switched, and English-only speech. At the same time, substantial gaps remain even for these leading configurations: learner WER is approximately 10 percentage points higher than instructor WER, while code-switched and English-only WER exceed Spanish-only WER by approxi-mately 6–9 points. The condition-specific analyses therefore establish the robustness of the leading configurations while identifying where recognition remains most difficult.

6 Conclusion 

This study compared six ASR configurations on spontaneous online Spanish L2 interaction. Muse obtained the lowest overall WER point estimate at 15.59%, closely followed by the MOSS adapter at 16.39%. The 0.80-percentage-point difference is small relative to the session-level variability of both systems, so we do not read it as establishing an ordering. In-domain LoRA adaptation reduced MOSS WER by 38.2% relative, bringing the open-weight, long-form system close to commercial Muse, whereas Whisper adaptation produced con-siderably smaller gains. The effect was confirmed by learner-disjoint cross-validation and came from matched data rather than volume: adding larger out-of-domain bilingual or learner corpora never improved on the classroom sessions alone. Muse and the MOSS adapter led across speaker roles and language contexts, but substantial diffi-culty persisted: learner speech produced higher WER than instructor speech for every system, and code-switched and English-only speech were harder than Spanish-only speech. Condition-aware is therefore valuable because it tests whether aggre-gate advantages generalize and shows where errors remain concentrated. Transcription and speaker attribution also pro-duced different rankings. Muse provided the low-est transcription-error point estimates, whereas the MOSS adapter obtained the lowest system-provided DER among the evaluated long-form sys-tems, although DER comparisons were based on partially different sets of eligible recordings. For applications that measure participation, the system with the lowest WER is therefore not necessarily the one that produces the most usable transcript.An adaptable open-weight model that runs locally and attributes speakers in the same pass is a practical alternative to a commercial service for classroom data. System selection for bilingual educational data should thus weigh aggregate accuracy, robust-ness across the intended speakers and language contexts, and the accuracy of speaker attribution.

10 Appendix A Training and Adaptation Details 

This appendix completes the training configuration of Section 4.1 and reports the development results behind it; the recipe was fixed before the seven evaluation sessions were scored. The values be-low are not comparable with those in Section 5: they cover the 38 adaptation sessions, fillers were retained, every system decoded complete record-ings, speaker role came from the speaker mapping of concatenated minimum-permutation WER (cp-WER; von Neumann et al., 2023) rather than from reference labels and each session was transcribed by the fold-specific adapter that had not seen it. 

A.1 Training Configuration 

Both adapters used default LoRA initialization without rank-stabilized scaling, optimized with AdamW in bfloat16. A MOSS example was a com-plete recording, split at silence gaps into parts of at most 30 minutes, with batch size 1, no gradient accumulation, linear decay to zero, no warm-up, gradient checkpointing, float32 adapter weights, and seed 42. A Whisper example was a window of at most 30 seconds of contiguous audio, cut only at gaps between turns and closed when a gap exceeded 5 seconds. Its target used the native times-tamp format, with the language token fixed to Span-ish in training and detected at inference. Whisper used 24 windows per optimizer step (batch size 12, gradient accumulation 2), cosine decay after a 10% warm-up, weight decay 0.02, gradient clipping at 0.5, and seed 0. Six epochs were budgeted for both models. For MOSS, we kept the epoch checkpoint with the lowest development WER (ties broken by CER), discarding checkpoints that covered less than 90% of the audio or whose output length fell outside 0.8 to 1.3 times the reference. For Whisper, the development set was scored at fixed intervals, with early stopping after five evaluations without improvement. The final MOSS adapter was trained on the 57 training examples of the full 38-session set for 264 steps, the budget selected by four of the five cross-validation folds (Appendix A.2). The final Whisper adapter is the step-275 checkpoint of a 330-step run, 330 being the mean of the best steps across the five folds. With no development session left, it was scored every 55 steps on 200 windows sampled from its own training data, and the lowest window WER was kept: 14.31% at step 275 against 14.47% at step 330. 

A.2 Cross-Validated Adaptation Effect 

Five folds were formed over the 38 adaptation ses-sions so that no learner appeared in both the train-ing and the test portion of a fold; each fold trained on approximately 28 sessions, selected its check-point on two development sessions, and transcribed the sessions it had not seen. Each fold trained for six epochs; four folds selected the final checkpoint (252 to 264 steps) and one selected epoch 4 (176 steps), with less than one point of development WER between the two in every fold. Differences are reported with a paired session-level bootstrap of 10,000 replicates (Koehn, 2004). Adaptation reduced MOSS WER from 20.99% to 17.85% (Ta-ble 6), the paired interval excluded zero, and 31 of the 38 sessions improved. The reduction was con-centrated in learner speech, 15.92 points against 2.11 for instructor speech, and cpWER fell from 24.91% to 19.65% without any diarization-specific training. When a model stopped generating before the end of a recording, the remainder was decoded again, identically for every system; without this recovery the reduction was 2.80 points with an in-terval of [−6.47 , 1.05] , widened by two sessions on which one model stopped early.

C Transcription Guidelines and Reference Verification 

C.1 Transcription Guidelines 

The independent audio-first dataset was transcribed directly from approximately 45 minutes of audio. Annotators did not consult the Zoom-generated transcripts or any other ASR output during the initial transcription pass. The central instruction was to write what was heard without correcting, translating, or smoothing the production . This pro-cedure prevented the audio-first transcripts from inheriting lexical choices, omissions, segmentation decisions, or speaker labels from Zoom. Annotators used stable anonymized speaker iden-tifiers such as Teacher , Student1 , and Student2 ,together with an instructor–learner role label. Each row represented one coherent speech turn. Rows were split when the speaker changed and merged when a continuous turn by one speaker had been divided artificially. Timestamps were treated as guides rather than as frame-accurate annotations and were changed only when an incorrect boundary affected speaker attribution or transcription. The following conventions were used: • Verbatim learner speech: Grammatical vari-ation, word order, non-target forms, compre-hensible pronunciation-based forms, and non-standard varieties were retained as produced. • Code-switching: Spanish and English words were retained in the positions in which they occurred and were not translated. • Fillers, repetitions, and repairs: Filled pauses, repeated words, false starts, and self-repairs were preserved. • Incomplete words: An incomplete word was marked with a double hyphen at the point of interruption, as in empeza-- or I was go-- go-ing .• Unclear speech: Unintelligible material was marked with double parentheses rather than silently guessed. • Overlap: Both speakers were transcribed when possible, and simultaneous speech was marked as overlap. • Non-speech events: Only a controlled set of labels, including {laugh} , {cough} ,

{breath} , and {pause} , was used. • Numbers and contractions: Numbers were written as spoken. Spoken contractions such as I’m , it’s , and gonna were preserved. When a turn boundary or speaker identity re-mained uncertain, the annotator retained the clos-est defensible segmentation and documented the uncertainty rather than silently normalizing it.

Fillers were grouped into canonical forms and their orthographic variants. The canonical inventory was { ahm , ah , uh , eh , erh , emm , hm , mhm , uhuh ,

E Joint Disaggregation by Speaker Role and Language Context 

Figure 2 jointly disaggregates WER by speaker role and language context. The particularly small English-only cells should be interpreted cau-tiously. Adaptation improved all six conditions for both model families.
