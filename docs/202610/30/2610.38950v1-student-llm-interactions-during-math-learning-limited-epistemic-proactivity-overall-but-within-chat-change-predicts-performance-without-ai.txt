ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for learning, raising a key question: can learners remain epistemically proactive , retaining responsibility for the cognitive and regulatory activities through which learning is produced while working with AI? Because this responsibility may change as interactions unfold, we examine epistemic proactivity as a developing process. We conceptualize epistemic proactivity through self-regulated learning (SRL) moves, complemented by help-seeking strategies and manifestations of epistemic vigilance. We analyzed these behaviors in 880 substantive turns from 112 secondary-school students completing open-ended mathematical-modeling practice with a general-purpose LLM (Mistral). Overall, students showed limited observable epistemic proactivity: their interactions were strongly request-dominated, with planning, monitoring, and evaluation relatively rare, although low-quality AI responses elicited more explicit evaluation. Temporal analyses qualified this picture: explicit SRL-related moves increased modestly over the chat, but help-seeking strategies became less understanding-oriented. Crucially, these temporal trajectories in help-seeking strategies predicted their post-test performance without AI beyond pre-test and aggregate patterns: students whose requests became more understanding-oriented performed better once AI assistance was removed. Thus, while students had a limited epistemic proactivity overall, its temporal development still was a critical predictor of post-test performance without AI. These results underscore the importance of examining the temporal organization of epistemic proactivity, rather than isolated prompt types, for understanding AI-supported learning. We release our fully-anonymized dataset publicly in this anonymous OSF repository.

1 Introduction 

Students are increasingly using general-purpose large language models (LLMs) for learning [1]. While they can use LLMs to improve immediate task performance, their use does not necessarily support the knowledge-construction processes needed for durable learning—such as planning, reasoning, and structuring problems [2]. Learners may instead delegate these learning-relevant cognitive and regulatory work to the tool without an immediate or visible cost to task completion [3]. A central question for AI-supported learning is therefore whether learners can retain responsibility for these cognitive and regulatory processes through which learning occurs, while benefiting from the assistance these systems provide. This concern is increasingly discussed in terms of learner agency: whether learners remain able to direct and take responsibility for their own learning when working with AI [3]. Agency, however, is a broad and context-dependent construct that has been conceptualized as encompassing regulatory and reflective abilities, agentic actions such as selective and responsible engagement, and motivational characteristics such as self-efficacy and persistence [4]. This breadth makes agency an important overarching concern, but difficult to investigate empirically. This challenge is even more pronounced in complex learning environments such as GenAI-supported ones where the system itself can reshape several components of learners’ agency [5]. We therefore focus on one specific epistemic dimension of learner agency, which we term epistemic proactivity : the extent to which learners retain responsibility for initiating, monitoring, advancing, and evaluating their own knowledge-building processes with AI [6, 7]. Epistemic proactivity therefore distinguishes initiating a learning trajectory from primarily reacting within one proposed by the system [8]. In doing so, it also distinguishes behavioral from epistemic engagement during AI-supported learning: a learner may remain behaviorally agentic—asking questions, evaluating suggestions—while becoming epistemically reactive if the AI is increasingly determining what should be explored next, and how the interaction should proceed [9, 10]. We examine this dimension through self-regulated learning (SRL) as the overarching regulatory framework, complemented by help-seeking strategies and epistemic vigilance as finer-grained accounts of what learners ask AI to contribute and how they evaluate its contributions. Importantly, epistemic proactivity is unlikely to remain fixed during a learning process: it may shift as task demands, learners’ resources, and experience with the tool evolve [11]. Studying it therefore means asking how it develops across an interaction, not only how often particular behaviors occur. However, such examinations are still missing in literature; and, specifically, two main gaps remain. First, process-level analysis of student–AI interactions is still limited for secondary-school students compared with university populations, despite their growing use of these systems [1, 12]. Second, little is known about whether such interaction dynamics relate to learning beyond the performance achieved while AI assistance is available. This question is especially relevant during adolescence, when capacities required for an epistemically-active use of AI are still developing [13]. Against this background, we studied 112 Grade-9 students using a general-purpose LLM during an open-ended mathematical-modeling activity between a pre-test and post-test completed without AI. We address two questions: to what extent do students remain epistemically proactive while learning with AI, and whether the way epistemic proactivity develops over time is associated with what they can subsequently do without AI. We operationalize epistemic proactivity in conversational traces through SRL, complemented by finer-grained analyses of help-seeking strategies and epistemic vigilance. By answering these questions, we aim to clarify when AI-supported interaction reflects productive learning rather than successful performance with assistance, and to inform the design of scaffolds for adolescent learners. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Generative AI in Mathematics Education: Between Affordance and Reliance 

Mathematics makes the tension between the affordances and risks of LLM-supported learning particularly visible. LLMs can provide explanations, worked examples, and feedback across mathematical problem-solving activities [14, 15, 16]. Yet mathematical learning requires more than obtaining correct solutions: learners must learn how to structure problems, select and justify representations, interpret results, and evaluate whether they meaningfully address the problem [17]. This is especially salient in mathematical modeling for instance, a core mathematical competency where learners must progressively structure an initially open situation rather than follow a predefined procedure [18]. While LLMs can support these processes, they can also perform them on the learner’s behalf, when their assistance is not calibrated to the learner’s current knowledge and background [19].Their outputs may also be inaccurate, oversimplified, or pedagogically weak, creating a further risk when such problems go undetected [14, 20]. Empirical work illustrates this tension. Pre-service mathematics teachers have been found to misjudge erroneous ChatGPT responses and subsequently produce incorrect solutions [21]. Among secondary students solving mathematical-modeling problems with ChatGPT, AI use concentrated on early phases such as simplifying and structuring, whereas 2A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 interpretation and validation were comparatively rare, potentially suggesting that AI use led them to bypass such important processes [19]. Furthermore, a large-scale study by Bastani et al. found that unrestricted GPT-4 improved performance while assistance was available, but this advantage did not persist when students later worked without AI, whereas pedagogical guardrails largely mitigated the effect [22]. These results suggest that students delegated the full epistemic work to the tool when they had access to it. Related work beyond mathematics similarly suggests that delegating learning-relevant cognitive work to LLMs can reduce learners’ processing and integration of information [23, 6, 24]. Together, these findings suggest that AI can facilitate immediate performance while shifting learning-relevant work from learner to system. Compared with classical forms of cognitive offloading such as externalizing memory or information retrieval [25], LLMs broaden what can be delegated to include problem structuring, reasoning, information synthesis, and decisions about how an inquiry should proceed [9, 24]. Productive AI-supported learning therefore cannot be understood from task success alone: it also requires examining how epistemic responsibility for initiating, advancing, and evaluating learning–i.e. epistemic proactivity– is shared between learner and AI. 

2.2 Epistemic Proactivity through Self-Regulation: Help-Seeking and Epistemic Vigilance 

We use self-regulated learning (SRL) as the overarching framework for understanding how epistemic proactivity unfolds during AI-supported learning. SRL concerns how learners set goals, plan how to pursue them, monitor their strategies and understanding, and evaluate progress to guide subsequent learning [26, 27]. These processes closely correspond to the forms of epistemic responsibility captured by epistemic proactivity. Effective self-regulation is an important topic in education research as it is generally associated with stronger learning and engagement [28]. In student–AI dialogue, explicit expressions of plans, uncertainty, requests, and evaluations can therefore provide observable evidence of regulation, although regulation that remains internal cannot be directly observed. We therefore focus our analysis on detecting observable expressions of planning, monitoring, requesting information, and evaluation in student–AI dialogue. Within this broader regulatory process, help-seeking provides a finer-grained account of how learners implement their learning strategies, once they identify a learning goal [29]. The help-seeking lens is particularly informative for epistemic proactivity because different types of requests leave different amounts and types of epistemic work with the learner. For example, help-seeking research distinguishes instrumental assistance, which supports continued independent reasoning, from executive assistance, which transfers more of the task to the external source [29]. Question-asking research similarly distinguishes requests supporting explanation and knowledge construction from those primarily seeking convergent information [30, 31]. In this work, we therefore distinguish conceptual and procedural requests oriented toward advancing understanding from answer- and verification-seeking requests oriented more toward epistemic closure [30]. This perspective also avoids equating prompt sophistication with productive learning: an elaborate prompt may delegate substantial reasoning, whereas a brief request may target a precise conceptual gap [32]. A second specific lens within this regulatory process is epistemic vigilance : evaluating communicated information before accepting or integrating it [33]. Such appraisal can regulate subsequent activity, for example by prompting learners to seek clarification, reformulate a question, or test an alternative [34]. In AI-supported learning, epistemic vigilance is therefore relevant not only to whether learners detect problematic assistance, but also to whether this appraisal informs how they subsequently regulate the interaction. Epistemic vigilance may depend on domain knowledge, the quality of the information received, and perceived source reliability [35]. Accordingly, in this work, we aim to examine conversational traces of both evaluative detection and subsequent regulatory response. In short, our framework is hierarchical: SRL captures the broader regulatory functions through which learners plan, monitor, seek information, and evaluate their learning. Within this process, help-seeking further characterizes what 

learners ask the AI to contribute when seeking assistance, while epistemic vigilance characterizes how they evaluate and respond to what the AI provides. Examining these levels together allows us to characterize epistemic proactivity without assuming that any single behavior or prompt is inherently productive: what matters is how learners retain the overall responsibility for identifying what needs to be understood, deciding what to delegate, and using AI contributions to regulate subsequent learning. 

2.3 Epistemic Proactivity as an Evolving Process in Student–AI Dialogue 

Epistemic proactivity is unlikely to remain fixed throughout an interaction. As learners gain experience with an AI, and as task demands and available cognitive resources change, they may adapt what they regulate themselves, what assistance they seek, and how carefully they evaluate the system’s contributions [12]. They may increasingly delegate structuring or reasoning when the system appears capable of performing these activities, or reclaim responsibility when its assistance proves insufficient or unsatisfactory [9, 36]. 3A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 Importantly, such delegation is not inherently detrimental. Cognitive offloading can reduce demands that would otherwise exceed learners’ available resources [37], potentially freeing effort for other learning-relevant activity [38, 11]. This is also in line with the broader "assistance dilemma": external support can be beneficial for learning if it is carefully designed to enable progress without replacing the learner activity required for learning [39]. With LLMs, the relevant question is therefore not simply whether learners delegate cognitive or regulatory work, but what they delegate, when ,and whether epistemic responsibility is subsequently retained or reclaimed. This makes the analysis of temporal organization of AI-supported learning important. Recent studies have begun to move beyond behavioral frequencies using process-oriented methods that capture how interaction patterns unfold. For instance, work in [40] coded student–GenAI exchanges using Bloom’s taxonomy and combined Epistemic Network Analysis with frequency-based Transition Network Analysis to examine both co-occurring cognitive processes and their sequential transitions, revealing recurrent rather than strictly linear patterns of cognitive engagement. Similarly, work in [12] followed students across four assignments, qualitatively coded their prompts, and used Co-Occurrence Network Analysis to examine how prompting and cognitive-offloading patterns developed with repeated AI use. Related work has used sequential pattern mining and transition analysis to trace how epistemic functions are organized across student–AI dialogue [41]. Complementing these observational approaches, experimental evidence also suggests that ordering matters: learners who generated their own ideas before consulting ChatGPT subsequently performed better without AI than those given unrestricted access from the beginning [42]. Together, these studies show that AI-supported learning can be examined at multiple temporal scales, from local transitions between interaction states to broader changes across repeated use and experimentally structured sequences. Such analyses can reveal patterns of AI use and cognitive engagement that are obscured by overall behavioral frequencies, particularly as students’ prompting and offloading strategies change with repeated experience. While informative, a key question remains under-explored in this literature: whether such changes during AI use are associated with what learners can subsequently do without AI. This is important as successful assisted performance does not establish that knowledge or skills remain available once assistance is removed [2, 22]. Existing temporal work provides limited evidence on whether/what features student-AI regulation carry such information beyond learners’ initial domain knowledge and what can already be inferred from aggregate patterns of AI use. We therefore examine both broader within-chat changes and local sequential organization, and test whether these dynamic features are associated with later performance without AI beyond pre-test performance and static interaction patterns. As discussed above, we focus on adolescent school students because this developmental period may be particularly sensitive to over-delegation to AI: the very same cognitive and regulatory capacities needed to decide what to delegate, monitor AI assistance, and retain control over learning are themselves still developing [13]. 

3 Current Study and Research Questions 

We examine epistemic proactivity among Grade-9 school students using a general-purpose LLM during open-ended mathematical modeling practice situated between a pre-test and post-test without AI. Mathematical modeling provides a particularly suitable context because learners must not only apply mathematical knowledge, but also structure the problem, select and revise strategies, interpret results, and evaluate their validity [43, 18, 44]. Moreover, rather than being asked to use the LLM to solve a specific problem, students were instructed to use the interaction to advance their understanding of mathematical modeling in preparation for an exam. Decisions about what needed to be learned, how to organize the interaction, and what assistance to seek were therefore largely left to the students. Together, these two design choices allow us to examine students’ spontaneous expression of epistemic proactivity, as no particular interaction strategy, specific problem-solving goal, or pattern of assistance-seeking was imposed. Drawing on the framework developed above, we examine epistemic proactivity through students’ SRL functions and the epistemic function of their help-seeking requests, complemented by an exploratory analysis of how students respond to low-quality AI assistance as an indicator of their epistemic vigilance. Beyond these overall patterns, we examine two complementary scales: the local sequential organization of behavior and broader within-session changes in SRL and HS. Finally, we test whether these dynamic features account for subsequent post-test performance without AI, beyond pre-test performance and static patterns of AI use. 4 Methods 

4.1 Participants 

Participants were 112 Grade-9 high-school students from three public Gymnasium schools (academically oriented secondary schools) in Baden-Württemberg, Germany. Students were 14–15 years old ( M = 14 .67 ). For analyses involving post-test performance, we only had a sample of 95 students as some them had missing data for either one of the tests. Students were familiar with the underlying mathematical concepts from their regular curriculum but had not previously worked on the specific modeling skills used in the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tübingen (AZ: A2.5.4-438_hb) and by the Ministry of Culture, Youth and Sports of Baden-Württemberg. Written informed consent was obtained from all students and guardians prior to participation. 

4.2 Study Material and Procedure 

The study was conducted during regular mathematics lessons on tablets and lasted approximately 60 minutes. Students first completed a 10-minute mathematics pre-test assessing the targeted modeling competencies. They were then presented with a screen describing the learning activity Figure 1. As seen in the figure, the task was explicitly framed as a learning activity : students were told that their goal was to improve their understanding of mathematical modeling with linear and quadratic functions, rather than to solve a predefined set of problems. These topics formed part of their current mathematics curriculum and had been studied throughout the school year; they would also remain relevant in subsequent years. The activity was therefore intended 5A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 to consolidate and extend competencies that students had already begun to develop. Students were instructed to identify uncertainties in their understanding of this topic, ask the tutor for assistance, and use the interaction to prepare themselves to solve mathematical-modeling problems in a subsequent post-test where AI assistance will be removed. To make this goal concrete, the instructions provided examples of questions they could ask the tutor and of the types of problems they should be able to solve on their own after they finish the training. After reading these instructions and receiving additional explanations from the researcher, students then engaged in approximately 40 minutes of open-ended mathematical-modeling practice with the AI tutor. No fixed sequence of problems or questions was imposed. Students were therefore responsible for deciding which aspects of mathematical modeling to explore, what assistance to request, and how to use the tutor. To ensure a minimum amount of interaction, they were required to contribute at least six task-related turns before ending the chat. Empty, nonsensical, and off-task messages were automatically filtered, excluded from this minimum, and not added to the conversation history. Finally, students completed a 10-minute post-test without access to the AI tutor. Pre- and post-test items differed but assessed the same mathematical-modeling competencies and were selected for comparable difficulty. The items were drawn from official curriculum materials and required similar mathematical reasoning steps and application of linear and quadratic functions while differing in wording and problem context. Further details about the assessment items are provided in section 4.3. 

4.3 Measures Mathematical Modeling Performance Students’ mathematical modeling performance was assessed using pre- and post-test items drawn from the mathematics item pool developed by the German Institute for Educational Quality Im-provement (IQB): https://www.iqb.hu-berlin.de/de/schule/aufgaben/seki/vera-8-mathematik/ . The selected items specifically targeted the mathematical-modeling competencies practiced during the AI-supported activity. Items were selected based on the official IQB classifications of competence area, content focus, and difficulty. Different but matched items were administered at pre- and post-test to minimize repetition and practice effects while targeting comparable modeling competencies. Pre-test performance served as a baseline measure of students’ modeling perfor-mance and was included as a covariate in the analyses predicting subsequent post-test performance. Responses were scored manually by the research team. 

4.3.1 Codebook Development, Validation, and Application to Student-AI Conversations Codebook Development. Building on the hierarchical framework developed above, we constructed a theory-driven, turn-level coding scheme to operationalize epistemic proactivity in student–AI conversations. As summarized in sub-figure (a) of Figure 2, each student turn was first coded for its broad SRL function; SRL turns that contained assistance-seeking were then further characterized through help-seeking content. Tutor responses were separately rated for quality, and students’ subsequent activity was used as an exploratory indicator of epistemic vigilance—that is, which SRL and/or help-seeking behavior followed an identified low-quality response. 

SRL function captured the regulatory role of each student turn: PLAN for explicitly communicating epistemic goals or strategies, MONITOR for expressing uncertainty, lack of (or achievement of) understanding, or knowledge gaps, 

REQUEST for seeking assistance, and EVALUATE for judging the correctness, relevance, or usefulness of the tutor’s response. PLAN , MONITOR , and EVALUATE therefore provided observable evidence of explicit learner regulation, whereas REQUEST captured assistance-seeking. Importantly, REQUEST was not itself treated as epistemically reactive; its epistemic function depended on what assistance was requested and how it was situated within the interaction and learning process. Turns classified as REQUEST were therefore further coded for help-seeking content : conceptual, procedural, verification-seeking, or answer-seeking [29, 30, 45]. Conceptual and procedural requests were grouped as understanding-oriented ,whereas answer- and verification-seeking requests were grouped as more closure-oriented . These categories do not imply that individual requests are inherently productive or unproductive; rather, they distinguish requests that tend to extend reasoning from those primarily seeking or confirming an outcome. These contrasts were subsequently used to examine how students’ help-seeking changed across the interaction. Finally, and as discussed in section 2, the presence of an EVALUATE turn alone does not fully capture epistemic vigilance, which also concerns whether learners use their appraisal of information to regulate subsequent learning behavior [34]. Tutor responses were therefore rated on five-point scales for accuracy and need alignment . Local sequential organization. Session-level trajectories capture broader directional change but not how behaviors are organized from one interaction step to the next. We therefore complement them with first-order transition analyses of SRL functions and Help-Seeking strategies [40]. Observed transitions will be compared with a within-student permutation null that preserves each student’s overall behavioral composition while disrupting sequential order, allowing us to identify transitions that occurred more often than expected from students’ aggregate behavior alone.

5.2 RQ1: Interaction Profile of Epistemic Proactivity SRL Functions and Help-Seeking Strategies. We first characterized students’ overall SRL functions and the epistemic function of the assistance they sought using the validated coding procedure described in subsubsection 4.3.1. As shown in sub-figure (a) of Figure 3, interactions were strongly dominated by requests for assistance. Of the 880 substantive turns, 717 (81.5%) were classified as REQUEST , compared with 9.9% PLAN , 6.4% MONITOR , and 2.2% EVALUATE . Thus, although requesting assistance is itself part of self-regulation, other parts of the cycle were comparatively rare in the dialogue. Students thus rarely communicated their state of knowledge ( MONITOR ) or articulated how they intended to advance their learning ( PLAN ) before seeking assistance. This provides limited observable evidence that students themselves were framing their epistemic goals and trajectories, potentially leaving greater space for the AI to do so. The epistemic function of these requests was more evenly distributed. Among the 717 REQUEST turns, 28% were procedural, 26% conceptual, 24% answer-seeking, and 22% verification/ validation-seeking. Understanding-oriented requests—conceptual and procedural assistance intended to support continued understanding or problem solving— therefore accounted for 54% of Help-seeking, compared with 46% answer-seeking or validation. Students thus relied extensively on the tutor for assistance, while differing in whether that assistance primarily supported further knowledge construction or sought an answer or confirmation. Taken together, these results indicate limited observable epistemic proactivity in students’ overall interaction profiles, despite substantial behavioral engagement and important variation in the epistemic function of their help-seeking behaviors. Indeed, we have little explicit evidence that they were themselves initiating, monitoring, or evaluating the broader epistemic trajectory, and nearly half of their help-seeking strategies closure-oriented requests.

As discussed in subsubsection 4.3.2, we first examined whether students’ overall SRL functions and help-seeking strategies changed as the interaction unfolded. 5.4 RQ3: Static and Dynamic Interaction Features and Links with subsequent Performance without AI 

Finally, we examined whether students’ AI-interaction features were associated with subsequent post-test performance beyond pre-test performance (i.e. prior knowledge estimation), and whether dynamic features provided information beyond static, whole-session summaries. Of the 95 students with complete pre- and post-test data, six did not have a computable Help-Seeking trajectory because they had no subtype REQUEST in one half of the interaction. All models in this section were therefore estimated on the same matched sample of 89 students. 

Static interaction features. The static block comprised four static whole-session features: the proportion of epistemically-proactive SRL turns, i.e. classified as PLAN , MONITOR , or EVALUATE ; the proportion of help-seeking proactive requests, classified as conceptual or procedural; total chat time; and mean AI-response review time. We add the two latter as previous work has used them as proxies for behavioral engagement in AI environments [46]. Together, these captured students’ overall regulatory and help-seeking composition as well as interaction amount and pace. Our results show that pre-test performance alone accounted for R2 = .161 of post-test variance. Adding the static features increased explained variance only to R2 = .184 , ∆R2 = .023 , F (4 , 83) = 0 .57 , p = .684 ; see Table 2. Thus, students’ whole-session interaction composition, chat duration, and AI-response review time did not account for detectable additional variance in the subsequent performance beyond prior knowledge, once AI assistance was removed. These findings therefore caution against treating the overall epistemic function of students’ requests, greater time spent interacting with AI, or longer exposure to its responses as straightforward indicators of more productive learning or better subsequent performance without AI.

6 Discussion and Practical Implications 

This study examined how Grade-9 school students managed epistemic proactivity when using a general-purpose LLM for open-ended mathematical-modeling practice. We conceptualized this through the SRL framework, complemented by help-seeking and epistemic vigilance, and examined how these behaviors were expressed in students’ conversations with the LLM. Three findings define the overall picture. First, students showed limited observable epistemic proactivity overall: their interactions were strongly request-dominated, with relatively little explicit planning, monitoring, or evaluation, although they were more likely to evaluate AI responses when those responses were of low quality. Second, within-chat change in epistemic proactivity behaviors was uneven and informative: help-seeking became less understanding-oriented on average while explicit SRL activity increased modestly, so different aspects of epistemic proactivity moved in different directions. Finally, individual differences in the help-seeking trajectory mattered, with students whose requests became relatively more understanding-oriented subsequently performing better without AI. 

6.1 RQ1: Students Showed Limited Epistemic Proactivity Profiles Overall. 

Students relied extensively on the AI for assistance. Although slightly more than half of their requests sought conceptual or procedural support, interactions were dominated by requests, with relatively few explicit instances of other SRL moves such as planning, monitoring, or evaluation. This pattern should not be interpreted as complete epistemic reactivity: requesting assistance is itself part of regulation, and regulatory processes that remain internal cannot be observed from conversational traces. Nevertheless, the open-ended task required students themselves to identify what they needed to understand and determine how AI should contribute. The scarcity of explicit regulation therefore suggests that students often made limited observable contributions to structuring the broader learning trajectory. This is indeed be particularly challenging for adolescent learners whose information-search and self-regulatory capacities 12 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 are still developing [47], especially given the additional regulatory demands that open-ended AI environments may impose [48, 47]. The epistemic-vigilance results provided a slightly different picture. Students became more explicitly evaluative when AI assistance was inaccurate or poorly aligned, suggesting that they were not uniformly uncritical users. This contrasts with evidence that learners can overlook or adopt erroneous AI-generated information [21, 19, 7]. However, such opportunities were rare in our data because most AI responses were rated as accurate and well aligned, limiting what can be inferred about students’ vigilance more generally. In this context, it is also important to consider that highly fluent and generally adequate assistance may itself create a regulatory challenge: it provides few cues that careful evaluation is required, even though accurate responses may still be poorly calibrated to learners’ knowledge, insufficiently explanatory, or pedagogically suboptimal [14, 36]. Future work should therefore examine whether prolonged exposure to mostly reliable AI increases perceived source reliability and, in turn, gradually reduces evaluative effort [35]. 

6.2 RQ2: SRL and Help-Seeking Strategies Showed Diverging Development Across the Session. 

Students’ epistemic proactivity was not stable and did not develop uniformly. Their SRL behavior shifted modestly away from request-dominated interaction toward more explicit regulation, while their help-seeking became less understanding-oriented and more focused on answers and verification (epistemic closure). Although these trajectories may appear contradictory, they capture different aspects of epistemic responsibility. Students could become more explicit in monitoring or regulating the interaction while simultaneously asking the AI to perform more of the underlying knowledge-building work. One possible explanation is that sustaining both explicit regulation and understanding-oriented help-seeking places substantial cognitive and metacognitive demands on learners [48]. Answer- and verification-seeking also showed local persistence, suggesting that once students adopted these forms of assistance, they were more likely than expected to repeat them in the following request. This is consistent with the view that over-reliance may emerge progressively through interaction with AI, as users adapt to the ease of delegating cognitive work, rather than appearing as an immediate and fixed mode of use [8]. One possible explanation for the shift in help-seeking is adaptation to the low cost of obtaining AI assistance. Once explanations, answers, and confirmation become immediately available, while the longer-term learning consequences of delegating this work are not immediately visible, relying on the system to carry more of the task may become increasingly attractive [12]. Here, a related challenge is that learners must regulate their behavior with respect not only to the immediate task, but also to the knowledge and competencies they are trying to develop beyond it. Making these longer-term learning goals more explicit may therefore help influence how students decide what assistance to seek [13]. A complementary explanation concerns the fit between assistance and learners’ evolving task representations and available knowledge. Conceptual or procedural explanations requested before sufficient relevant knowledge has been activated may be difficult to process or integrate, particularly when a general-purpose LLM is poorly calibrated to the learner’s current understanding [38, 36]. Students may consequently shift toward answer-seeking or verification as a lower-cost way to continue and manage cognitive demands, whereas movement toward conceptual or procedural assistance may reflect a better match between the support requested and the knowledge available to make use of it. 

6.3 RQ3: Help-Seeking Strategies Development, rather than static AI-use measures, Was Associated with Better Later Performance Without AI. 

The clearest association between student–AI interaction features and subsequent performance without AI concerned the broader development of students’ help-seeking, rather than either the overall prevalence or local persistence of particular strategies. Students whose requests increasingly shifted toward conceptual and procedural assistance subsequently performed better without AI than students whose help-seeking shifted toward answer- and verification-seeking. Although answer- and verification-seeking showed local persistence across consecutive requests, these self-transitions were not themselves associated with the post-test performance without AI. Thus, occasional use or repetition of particular help-seeking strategies did not distinguish subsequent performance; what did was the broader direction in which help-seeking developed across the interaction. This supports our hypothesis that individual request types should not be treated as intrinsically productive or unproductive independently of where they occur within the learning process [11], and points instead to how epistemic responsibility is organized over time. These results are consistent with broader accounts of cognitive offloading, which suggest that delegating cognitive and metacognitive work can be beneficial or detrimental depending on when it occurs and on the learner’s available resources, and extend this principle to AI-supported learning environments [11, 38, 37]. Understanding-oriented help-seeking is itself cognitively demanding, as are the processes of knowledge construction and self-regulation that students need to carry out during learning. Students may therefore benefit from strategically deciding which parts of 13 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 the work to offload to the AI and which to retain. Offloading some reasoning may be useful when it frees cognitive resources for activities that are more important at that moment, whereas retaining responsibility may be necessary when performing the reasoning itself is essential for building or consolidating knowledge. Productive AI use may thus depend less on minimizing or maximizing delegation than on regulating when delegation is helpful and when learners need to carry out the cognitive work themselves. Our findings also challenge the idea that successful learning with AI can be inferred primarily from the quality of the information students receive. They instead highlight the importance of the epistemic work involved in seeking and using that assistance. Indeed, in our data, AI-response quality did not significantly differ according to whether the preceding request was understanding- or closure-oriented: students generally received high-quality assistance irrespective of the epistemic orientation of their request. Yet the development of their help-seeking was associated with their subsequent performance without AI. Thus, receiving high-quality AI-generated information does not necessarily imply that learners have engaged in the cognitive activity required to learn from it. This extends to AI-supported learning broader concerns that exposure to relevant information—and even its evaluation—does not guarantee that it will subsequently be accessible without assistance [3, 2]. Durable learning depends on learners engaging in the operations through which knowledge is actively sought and retrieved [13, 11]. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that learners benefit when they generate or structure their own work before consulting AI [42], and that unrestricted solution-giving can improve assisted performance without producing comparable subsequent performance without AI [22]. Taken together, the results suggest that limited epistemic proactivity at the aggregate level does not mean that its temporal organization is irrelevant to learning. Rather, how learners redistribute epistemic responsibility over the interaction—particularly through their help-seeking strategies—can carry information about what they are subsequently able to do without AI. Productive AI-supported learning may therefore depend less on particular prompt types than on whether learners retain or reclaim responsibility for cognitive and regulatory work that is itself important for learning. 

Practical implications. For educational AI design, these findings suggest moving beyond evaluating isolated prompts toward conversation-sensitive analysis and scaffolding. Individual answer- or verification-seeking requests should not necessarily be treated as problematic; rather, systems could monitor whether help-seeking progressively becomes more closure-oriented or whether learners contribute less to advancing their own understanding over time, which may indicate increasing offloading. More fundamentally, AI assistance should be calibrated so that the system does not perform the very cognitive operations that learners are expected to develop. If the learning objective requires students to retrieve and apply knowledge, explain a concept, connect ideas, or justify a solution, the AI should support rather than replace these activities. This could involve first eliciting learners’ current understanding and knowledge gaps, prompting an initial attempt, providing hints or partial information, or asking learners to retrieve and connect relevant concepts before more complete assistance is given. AI systems could also maintain an estimate of learners’ current knowledge and use it to calibrate the assistance they provide. When a learner has not yet acquired a target knowledge component, the system could avoid immediately taking over the reasoning required to practice it and instead scaffold the learner’s own attempt. Conversely, once that component is sufficiently established, delegating it to the AI may be appropriate as doing so can free resources for a more advanced learning objective. The objective is therefore not to minimize AI use or prevent offloading, but to preserve learning-relevant cognitive work while allowing other demands to be strategically delegated. This follows the assistance-dilemma principle [39] and is consistent with evidence that pedagogically constrained LLM support can better preserve subsequent performance when AI assistance is removed, than unrestricted solution provision [22]. For teachers, this means that AI literacy should extend beyond teaching students how to formulate effective prompts or operate AI tools. Before consulting AI, teachers should encourage students to articulate what they are trying to learn, what they already understand, what remains unclear, and, where possible, make a first attempt themselves. This can help establish a sequence in which AI augments students’ own reasoning—for example, by critiquing, extending, or helping them revise an initial attempt—rather than becoming the default go-to whenever they encounter difficulty. More broadly, epistemic proactivity with AI can itself become an instructional objective. Students need to learn not only how 

to obtain useful assistance, but also which cognitive work can appropriately be delegated and which should remain with them because performing that work is itself part of what they are learning. This requires helping students distinguish longer-term learning goals from short-term outcomes such as immediate task completion, and to regulate their AI use accordingly. In this sense, teaching students how to formulate questions that require them to articulate knowledge gaps, retrieve relevant information, or seek deeper understanding may also contribute to more productive use of the system. Students also need explicit practice evaluating AI responses even when they appear plausible and correct, for example by identifying remaining uncertainties, justifying whether an explanation is sufficient, or checking it against their own knowledge or other sources. At the same time, the importance of foundational instruction should not be overlooked: prior knowledge remained the strongest predictor of subsequent performance without AI in our study. Building these 14 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 foundations therefore remains essential, and AI should not routinely perform the very retrieval, reasoning, or application processes through which students are expected to acquire them. Finally, when having students work with AI, teachers should avoid treating isolated answer- or verification-seeking requests as inherently unproductive; greater attention may instead be warranted when these strategies become increasingly dominant over the course of an interaction without a return to more understanding-oriented activity. 

7 Limitations and Future Work 

Several limitations qualify these findings and point toward future work. First, the interaction took place within a single, relatively short learning session. The trajectories identified here therefore capture within-session changes in students’ observable behavior rather than the development of stable SRL or Help-Seeking skills. Repeated interactions with AI may produce different dynamics as learners gain experience with the system, calibrate their trust, and develop more stable strategies for regulating AI assistance. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to examine whether the trajectories observed here persist, intensify, or change across repeated use, and whether students can learn to maintain epistemically proactive patterns over time. Relatedly, the post-test immediately followed the interaction, so the present findings concern subsequent performance rather than long-term retention. Second, our analysis focused primarily on domain-general regulatory and help-seeking processes. We initially sought to complement these measures with students’ progression through mathematical modeling processes, but the available modeling framework could not be applied with sufficient reliability to these open-ended dialogues and was therefore excluded from the analysis. This limits our understanding of how changes in epistemic proactivity were intertwined with students’ mathematical reasoning—for example, whether understanding-oriented help-seeking accompanied productive movement between important modeling steps or helped students overcome particular difficulties. Future work should develop more reliable, domain-grounded approaches for tracing learners’ knowledge states within open-ended AI conversations and examine how these interact with SRL and help-seeking over time. Third, the associations between interaction dynamics and subsequent performance are observational and should not be interpreted causally. In particular, the present data cannot establish whether increasingly answer-oriented help-seeking impairs learning, whether students who struggle are more likely to shift toward such strategies, or whether both reflect other learner characteristics. Experimental work manipulating how and when AI systems provide assistance will be necessary to test the mechanisms suggested by the present results. Our measures also capture only regulation that becomes visible in students’ dialogue; planning, monitoring, or evaluation may occur internally without being verbalized. Similarly, the epistemic-vigilance analysis should remain exploratory because low-quality AI responses were rare, limiting our ability to characterize how systematically students evaluated AI output. Finally, the study involved Grade-9 students completing mathematical modeling activities with one general-purpose LLM. The extent to which the observed trajectories generalize to other age groups, domains, tasks, models, or more pedagogically constrained AI tutors therefore remains an open question. In addition, participants’ gender were not collected sought to avoid collecting potentially sensitive personal information from minors, preventing us from examining whether patterns of epistemic proactivity or their associations with performance without AI differed across sex or gender groups. This further limits the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should examine these dimensions explicitly, alongside replication across different learner populations and instructional contexts. 

8 Conclusion 

Productive learning with AI may depend not only on what learners ask, but on how their relationship with AI assistance develops over time. In this study, grade-9 students showed limited observable epistemic proactivity overall when learning with a general-purpose LLM, yet individual differences in how their help-seeking developed were meaningfully associated with later performance without AI. Although help-seeking became less understanding-oriented on average, students who developed in the opposite direction performed better subsequently, whereas static interaction patterns and local persistence did not provide comparable information. These findings position epistemic proactivity as a dynamic property of AI-supported learning rather than a characteristic that can be fully understood from isolated prompts or session-level averages. Supporting productive AI use may therefore require moving beyond teaching students how to formulate good prompts toward helping them sustain understanding-oriented patterns of interaction over time. 15 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026
