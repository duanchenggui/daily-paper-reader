## Abstract 

Effective tutoring requires determining not only 

what information to provide, but also which form of assistance to deliver. A tutor may ad-dress the same student error using various ped-agogical strategies, adapting its approach as the interaction progresses. We introduce Compar-ative Pedagogical Supervision (CPS), a train-ing framework to learn to select the optimal strategy and feedback for student learning. We evaluate CPS on math and science reasoning datasets. After five tutoring turns, CPS out-performs the strongest fixed-strategy baselines. The learned tutor transfers across datasets, and its strategy selection can also inform a separate API-based tutor. 

## 1 Introduction 

Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) must balance instructional support with opportunities for inde-pendent problem solving, a challenge known as the assistance dilemma (Koedinger and Aleven, 2007). Research in cognitive science shows that the level of support needed varies as students progress (Salden et al., 2009), and identifies distinct forms of assistance such as hints, scaffolding, worked examples, and self-explanation (VanLehn, 2006; Wood et al., 1976; Chi et al., 1989). Tutors built on Large Language Models (LLMs) must there-fore decide not only what information to provide, but also which form of assistance suits a student’s misconception and progress. For example, Figure 1 depicts a student who ini-tially misinterprets the phrase ”twice as many.” As the student’s responses reveal progress or continu-ing difficulties, the tutor should reconsider which pedagogical strategy to use next. A student who un-derstands the relationship but cannot apply it may benefit from a W ORKED EXAMPLE illustrating the concept, whereas a student who applies it correctly may require only a H INT for the remaining steps. 

Figure 1: Pedagogical strategy varies according to the tutoring context: Depending on the student’s response, further support may be provided through a W ORKED EXAMPLE or a H INT for subsequent steps. 

LLMs make it easier to generate various forms of pedagogical assistance. Recent studies use peda-gogical dialogues and error-grounded supervision (Macina et al., 2023; Sonkar et al., 2024b; Da-heim et al., 2024). Other approaches train tutors to provide effective guidance (Scarlatos et al., 2025; Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025) and learn to select tutor-ing actions or prompts (Nam et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2026a). The appropriate strategy depends on the student’s current work and how the interaction has progressed. At each tutoring context, the tutor generates its response conditioned on a jointly identified pedagogical strategy. We then use independent simulated stu-dent samples to estimate the probability of solv-ing the problem following each response. The strategy-response pairs with the highest probability are then used to supervise the tutor. It generates a

Our contributions are: • We introduce CPS, a framework to train AdaTu-tor that adapts its pedagogical strategy to the student’s progress and difficulties. • We demonstrate improved tutoring across three datasets and multiple students, with analyses of dialogue context, strategy selection, and response generation. • We further demonstrate cross-dataset transfer and show that strategy selection learned by CPS im-proves the performance of an independent API tutor. 

## 2 Previous Work 

Pedagogical Feedback and Remediation: Ped-agogical dialogue datasets and training objectives facilitate the generation of instructional responses. MathDial provides annotations of mathematical tutoring dialogues with teacher moves (Macina et al., 2023). Pedagogical Alignment promotes scaffolded guidance using preference-based train-ing (Sonkar et al., 2024b). SocraticLM is trained on Socratic dialogues that involve six distinct stu-dent types (Liu et al., 2024). For error remediation, Daheim et al. (2024) ground responses in stepwise verification of student reasoning. AdaTutor instead compares responses from each strategy based on their impact on subsequent student outcomes. 

Training Tutoring Policies: Scarlatos et al. (2025) evaluate candidate responses based on pre-dicted student correctness and a pedagogical rubric, subsequently training models with DPO. TutorRL employs multi-turn RL to balance student task suc-cess with pedagogical support (Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025). Nam et al. (2025) develop long-horizon policies for high-level tutoring actions derived from compact student representations. Learning to Prompt selects session-level pedagogical prompts using topic and subject information (Chang et al., 2026a), while LEAP generates scaffolding plans from inferred cognitive states (Dong et al., 2026). CPS constructs supervision using alternative feedback from each strategy within the same tutor-ing context. The optimal strategy–feedback pairs are then used to train a tutor that jointly generates a strategy and feedback at each turn. 

## 3 Method 

At each tutoring turn, CPS selects a pedagogical strategy based on the student’s work and the preced-ing exchanges. We train these choices by compar-ing feedback from different strategies at the same point in the dialogue. The student’s answers after each feedback message determine which strategy and feedback to use as a training target (Figure 2).

and a separate answer to the original problem x.We check that answer against y. 3.2 Pedagogical Strategies 

Pedagogical research has long studied how differ-ent forms of tutoring assistance affect student learn-ing. They differ in how much of the reasoning the tutor provides. A M INIMAL HINT directs attention to the relevant concept and leaves the next step to the student (VanLehn, 2006; Aleven et al., 2003). A STEP SCAFFOLD breaks the next step into smaller actions (Wood et al., 1976). An E XPLICIT EXPLA - 

> NATION

states the relevant rule and the reasoning behind it (Wittwer and Renkl, 2008). A W ORKED EXAMPLE demonstrates the same reasoning on an analogous problem (Salden et al., 2009). SELF - 

> EXPLANATION

asks the student to justify their own reasoning (Chi et al., 1989). C ONTRASTIVE FEEDBACK compares the student’s incorrect rea-soning with a correct alternative (Daheim et al., 2024). These strategies span low and high levels of assistance, which reflects the assistance dilemma (Koedinger and Aleven, 2007). They also address different sources of misconceptions. Appendix A provides definitions and examples.

Supervised targets: We retain a training exam-ple when one strategy has a positive score strictly higher than every other strategy. Let a⋆t denote this strategy. The training input is ct, and the target contains the strategy tag a⋆t followed by its feed-back u(a⋆t )

Multi-turn Supervision: To train adaptation over multiple turns, we continue the interactions to collect examples from different strategies. We choose the highest-scoring strategy with probabil-ity 1 − ε, and with probability ε, we randomly choose from other strategies. We deliver its feed-3back ut to the student and obtain a fresh reply vt.We append both messages to Ht to form Ht+1 , then compare strategies again at the updated context 

ct+1 . The supervised target still uses the highest-scoring strategy, even when a different strategy continues the multi-turn interaction. This separates the choice of training target from the choice of which tutoring situations to encounter next. Ap-pendix E examines target selection, exploration, and the number of student attempts used for com-parison. 

Training and inference: We fine-tune M0 on the resulting tutoring contexts and their strategy– feedback targets. At inference, CPS receives ct and generates a strategy tag followed by tutor feedback 

ut in one call. Only the feedback is delivered to the student, and the tag is retained for analysis. After the student replies, CPS selects the next strategy using the updated dialogue. AdaTutor thus adapts as the student progresses. 

## 4 Implementation Details 

Stateful Student Simulator: Prompted LLM stu-dents can abandon an assigned misconception af-ter feedback that does not address it (Do et al., 2026). This makes it difficult to attribute apparent progress to the tutor’s assistance. Motivated by prior work advocating explicit representations of student knowledge (Yuan et al., 2026), we use a stateful LLM-based student simulator. We sepa-rate knowledge updates from response generation, drawing on the approach of Chang et al. (2026b). This lets us compare alternative tutor responses from the same dialogue and recorded knowledge state. The student uses knowledge component (KC). It is a reusable knowledge or concept needed for a reasoning step (Corbett and Anderson, 1994). We build a separate KC library for each domain from training problems and reference solutions, draw-ing on previous work on automated KC generation and tagging (Duan et al., 2026). For this, we split solutions into reasoning steps and cluster similar steps. We then identify KCs within each cluster and merge overlapping candidates. For selected KCs, we generate incorrect beliefs to construct the mis-conception students (Sonkar et al., 2024a; Do et al., 2026). The library remains fixed during tutoring. We assign each student a simple profile specifying which KCs are mastered, unmastered, or associated with an incorrect belief. Knowledge-gap students have unmastered KCs, misconception students hold incorrect beliefs about particular KCs, and mixed 

students have both knowledge gaps and misconcep-tions. The student’s state records the current status of the KCs needed for a problem. For example, it may mark addition as mastered but interpreting ”twice as many” as a misconception, with the belief ”twice means two more.” We initialize this state from the student’s profile. After tutor feedback, two LLM calls run sequentially. The first proposes an up-dated state based on the feedback. The second gen-erates an answer and a public response conditioned on the state. The state remains fixed during the sec-ond call, and only the public response is added to the tutor’s dialogue. We check whether responses reflect the assigned state and how they change un-der different feedback. Appendix B provides more details on KC library construction, misconception generation, and student profiles. 

Models and interaction protocol: 

We use Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct , and 

Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025) for student and teacher respectively. All methods begin from the same saved initial student response for each profile and problem, with subsequent responses sampled independently. Each interaction allows at most five tutor turns and ends when the student correctly answers the original problem or reaches the turn limit. 

Data collection and training: We sample K =10 independent student attempts to score each can-didate response and use ε = 0 .5 for exploratory continuation. We train a separate CPS tutor for each dataset using LoRA. We provide all prompts in Appendix G. 

## 5 Experiments 

Datasets and student profiles: We evaluate on math reasoning on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021). ScienceQA (Lu et al., 2022) contains MCQ’s in natural science, social science, and language. We construct 5 stu-dent profiles for each of the three student types, amounting to 15 profiles for each dataset. 

Baselines and evaluation metrics: We compare CPS with six Fixed-Strategy Tutors that always use a fixed strategy irrespective of the tutoring con-text. We also compare with P ROMPT SELECTOR ,where the tutor is provided all the six strategies and 4prompted to select the most appropriate strategy for the context. We also compare with Socrati-cLM (Liu et al., 2024), and TutorRL (Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025). We report Cumulative accuracy ( At) to measure the fraction of student–problem pairs solved by turn 

t. We also report Correction rate, defined as the fraction of initially incorrect pairs that are solved by five turns. See Appendix C for additional details on the experimental setup for reproducing the results. Appendix F evaluates student responses for con-sistency with assigned knowledge, tests whether student knowledge updates improve performance on new problems, and tutor responses for strategy adherence, correctness, and answer disclosure.

6.6 Transfer Across Datasets and Tutors 

The previous analyses examine tutoring within each dataset. We now test whether the learned tutoring behavior transfers to another dataset and whether the strategy choices can guide a different tutor model. 

Cross-dataset transfer: We evaluate each trained CPS tutor on the other two datasets with-out further training or checkpoint selection on the target dataset. For each target, all tutors use the same student profiles, problems, initial replies, and five-turn budget. Table 6 reports final accuracy. Transfer is strongest between GSM8K and SVAMP. The SVAMP-trained tutor reaches 75.5% on GSM8K, compared with 72.7% for the GSM8K-trained tutor and 66.4% for the strongest fixed strat-egy. In the reverse direction, the GSM8K-trained tutor reaches 78.7% on SVAMP, 3.8 points be-low the in-domain tutor and 1.2 points below the strongest fixed strategy. These arithmetic datasets also share the same KC library and student profiles. Transfer between arithmetic and ScienceQA yields lower accuracy. Tutors trained on GSM8K and SVAMP reach 49.1% and 51.1% on Sci-enceQA, compared with 64.1% for the ScienceQA-trained tutor. The ScienceQA-trained tutor reaches 33.5% on GSM8K and 31.1% on SVAMP. This dif-ference is expected given the differences between the domains. Arithmetic and science involve dif-ferent reasoning patterns, student difficulties, and misconceptions, which may require different peda-gogical strategies. The stronger transfer between GSM8K and SVAMP suggests that the learned tu-toring behavior transfers best when the source and target domains share similar student needs.

6.7 Response Quality Evaluation 

We use an LLM Judge to evaluate 270 initial stu-dent replies, 180 replies during tutoring, and 180 fixed-strategy tutor responses on GSM8K. It judges 95.0% of tutor responses mathematically correct and identifies no correct final-answer disclosure. On examples also rated by humans, the agreement is 84.0% for student-KC judgments and 95.0% for tutor strategy and correctness judgments. We also test whether student-state updates help on new problems. Using the state after relevant feedback raises accuracy on 300 new GSM8K problems that need the same KCs, from 5.0% to 67.7%, with the teaching dialogue removed and model parameters fixed. ## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced CPS, a framework to train a tutor to adapt its pedagogical strategy to the student’s work and the preceding dialogue. We train it by com-paring feedback that uses different strategies at the same point in a tutoring session. The selected strat-egy and feedback provide supervision for a tutor that chooses a strategy and generates its response in one call. Experiments on GSM8K, SVAMP, and ScienceQA show significant improvements over the strongest alternative tutor after five turns. First-turn comparisons show contributions from both learned strategy choices and feedback generation. Its learned strategy choices also improve tutoring with GPT-4.1-mini over fixed strategies. These findings support adapting the form of assistance to the current tutoring context rather than retaining one strategy throughout a session. 8Limitations 

Our experiments provide an efficient testbed for comparing pedagogical strategies with simulated students. Current results do not demonstrate human learning.

## B Stateful Student Simulator 

The simulator uses a KC library, a small library of screened misconceptions, synthetic student profiles, and a two-call LLM interaction. These components create controlled student conditions for tutor train-ing and evaluation rather than attempting to model the full behavior of human students. 

KC library and misconception construction: 

Knowledge components have long been used to represent fine-grained student knowledge (Corbett and Anderson, 1994). We construct them automati-cally from training solutions, following the general LLM-based KC generation and problem-tagging approach of Duan et al. (2026). We decompose each reference solution into small reasoning steps. We then cluster steps with similar meaning, pro-pose local KC candidates, and merge synonymous or redundant candidates. We retain recurring KCs and review the resulting library before using it for problem annotation. We then annotate each prob-lem with the KCs it needs using the reference solu-tion. Figure 6 summarizes this procedure. Not every KC used for problem annotation is used to define students. We retain KCs that occur often enough and are suitable for controlling stu-dent knowledge. For these KCs, an LLM proposes several reusable incorrect beliefs. We remove mal-formed, duplicate, or overly specific candidates and test the remaining beliefs on problems requiring the corresponding KC. Following prior work on simulated students with misconceptions (Sonkar et al., 2024a) and concerns about misconception faithfulness (Do et al., 2026), we retain a miscon-ception only when the student actually follows the intended incorrect belief and its performance de-creases relative to a mastered control on the same problems. 

Student profiles: A student profile assigns one of three states to the KCs used in the experiment: mas-tered , unmastered , or misconception . A knowledge-gap student has one or more unmastered KCs, a 

misconception student has one or more assigned incorrect beliefs, and a mixed student has both. We use a fully mastered profile when screening gaps and misconceptions. A profile can contain several deficits, but for a particular problem the student receives only the states of the KCs needed by that problem. State changes persist during that tutoring interaction, and the profile is used again to initialize the next problem. Dataset-specific KCs and profile counts are reported in Appendix C. 

Student responses: The student uses two sequen-tial LLM calls, similar in spirit to prior work that separates student state from response generation 10 Problem: Mia has 6 marbles. Leo has twice as many. Each person receives 3 more marbles. How many marbles do they have altogether?

E.1 Outcome Construction 

We compare three approaches for estimating the outcome of each strategy-conditioned response. The first directly evaluates whether the student can solve the original problem after receiving the can-didate response, using ten independently sampled attempts. The other two approaches introduce an interme-diate student response before evaluating comple-tion. The first samples one student response fol-lowed by ten completion probes. The second sam-ples ten student responses, scoring each directly when it answers the problem or using a paired com-pletion probe otherwise. Direct completion produces unique positive win-14 ners in 30.24% of collection histories, compared with 18.00% and 20.87% for the two alternative approaches. It also produces substantially fewer tied maxima: 8.96%, compared with 68.94% and 66.43%. The resulting supervision yields considerably higher downstream tutoring accuracy. With iden-tical initial student responses, direct completion reaches 88.55% validation accuracy, compared with 70.37% and 71.72% for the alternatives. Direct completion also reduces collection cost. It requires 191,391 model requests, compared with 239,414 and 432,345 for the other approaches. These findings motivate using direct completion value as the supervision signal in CPS. 

E.2 Exploratory Trajectory Continuation 

We next examine the effect of exploration during data collection. The supervised target at each tutor-ing context remains the highest-scoring candidate. The continuation policy determines which candi-date response advances the public dialogue and consequently which tutoring contexts are encoun-tered at later turns. We compare four values of ε, ranging from greedy continuation ( ε = 0 ) to exploration with 

ε = 0 .5.Increasing exploration exposes the tutor to ad-ditional histories following responses that do not achieve the highest observed direct completion value. At ε = 0 .5, the collection produces 1,048 supervised examples, compared with 773 under greedy continuation. The resulting tutor achieves 90.24% validation accuracy, the highest observed result among the evaluated configurations. This represents an im-provement of 1.68 percentage points over greedy continuation. We adopt ε = 0 .5 based on its validation per-formance and increased coverage of subsequent tutoring contexts. 

E.3 Supervised Targets and Sampling 

We investigate whether modifying the supervised target selection criterion or training-data sampling improves the distilled tutor. Our default approach retains comparisons with a unique positive maximum. We compare it with three alternatives: including one seeded candidate from positive ties and requiring winner margins of at least 0.2 or 0.5. Including positive ties increases the training set from 1,048 to 1,399 examples but reduces valida-tion accuracy from 90.24% to 86.20%. Requiring larger winner margins also reduces the number of available supervised examples. A margin of 0.2 produces 89.56% validation accuracy, while a mar-gin of 0.5 produces 86.53%. We additionally compare ordinary shuffled sam-pling with strategies that balance the frequency of pedagogical strategies or jointly balance strategies and tutoring turns. Ordinary shuffled sampling reaches 90.24% val-idation accuracy. Strategy-balanced and turn– strategy-balanced sampling achieve 79.80% and 75.42%, respectively. These results favor retaining unique positive win-ners without an additional margin threshold and training on their observed distribution. This con-figuration preserves the contextual distribution of successful strategy-conditioned responses during distillation. 

E.4 Interaction-Based Checkpoint Selection 

Finally, we examine checkpoint selection using downstream tutoring performance. We evaluate saved training checkpoints through complete five-turn validation interactions and select the check-point using final cumulative accuracy. On GSM8K, the interaction-selected checkpoint at step 220 achieves 91.92% validation accu-racy, compared with 88.55% for the minimum-validation-loss checkpoint at step 120. The cor-responding mean accuracy across all five tutoring turns increases from 85.72% to 87.81%. The difference highlights the value of selecting the deployed tutor using the downstream objective. We therefore use interaction-based checkpoint se-lection for the final CPS tutors.

F.4 Response to Different Feedback 

We examine whether the student’s state changes when feedback addresses its assigned difficulty, rather than merely asking it to reconsider. Do et al. (2026) show that simulated students can abandon a misconception after feedback that does not ad-dress it. Inspired by their comparison, we evalu-ate five feedback conditions. Relevant feedback addresses the assigned knowledge gap or miscon-ception. Retry only asks the student to attempt the problem again. Irrelevant feedback supplies information unrelated to the problem. Misaligned feedback addresses a different KC. Answer reveal provides the final answer without explaining the method. In this last condition, we expect the stu-dent to report the supplied answer while retaining its KC state, since reporting an answer alone does not establish mastery of the method. We select 40 GSM8K problem–profile pairs: 20 with a knowledge gap and 20 with a misconception. Each selected problem activates exactly one of the profile’s assigned deficits. Both initially correct and initially incorrect responses are included. We apply each feedback condition separately to the same initial response and state, then record the student’s answer, public response, and updated KC state. Table 18 reports changes to the targeted KC. The student’s state changes only after relevant feedback. Of the 40 target-state changes, 39 mark the target KC as mastered, while one does not mark the KC as mastered. An LLM judge con-siders all 40 changes supported by the feedback and local to the targeted KC. Answer revelation raises immediate answer accuracy to 82.5%, while leaving every KC state unchanged. When the stu-dent is subsequently evaluated from that unchanged state without the revealed answer, accuracy is 7.5%. This comparison distinguishes using the supplied answer from changing the student’s represented knowledge. We additionally assess whether the public re-sponses follow the resulting states. Across the ini-tial response and five feedback branches for each pair, yielding 240 judged responses, consistency is 90.4% over all affected KCs and 88.9% over KCs reached during reasoning. The structured answer agrees with the public response in 100.0% of cases. Thus, the evaluation checks both whether feedback changes the state selectively and whether the stu-dent’s subsequent reasoning reflects that state.
