# The Computational Assessment Stack: An Architecture for AI-Native Measurement

## Duolingo 

## Abstract 

Test development is shifting toward automation and agentic artificial intelligence (AI), in which autonomous systems draft items at scale, assem-ble test forms, administer adaptive assessments, score complex responses, and monitor test qual-ity. As autonomous systems take on more of this work, the architecture underlying test de-velopment must change to preserve assessment quality under the new paradigm. This paper proposes the Computational Assessment Stack 

(CAS), a four-layer architecture for AI-native measurement where every stage of the assess-ment lifecycle shares a unifying computational construct representation as its base layer. This representation is a machine-readable, modular, and versioned knowledge graph. Three lay-ers are built above it: computational content generation that builds on the knowledge graph; 

computational psychometrics that derives sub-stantive structure directly from the knowledge graph; an orchestration layer in which agentic systems operationalize the stack under human governance. The paper’s core contribution is architectural. Building on foundational psycho-metric frameworks, the CAS provides a persis-tent representation that unifies the four layers. Unlike the descriptive prose traditionally used to define constructs in test development, the proposed architecture renders measurement as-sumptions inspectable and their consequences empirically testable. 

Keywords: computational psychometrics, con-struct representation, knowledge graphs, Q-matrix, explanatory item response theory, evidence-centered design, validity argument, modularity, agentic AI, AI-native assessment 

## 1 Introduction: From Capabilities to Architecture 

Educational measurement has entered an era in which artificial intelligence (AI) with human gover-nance can reliably conduct many of the test devel-opment processes. AI systems are used to generate test items, score complex constructed responses, deliver adaptive assessments, and synthesize diag-nostic feedback at scale. Even though each capa-bility has been rigorously studied (Hao et al., 2024; von Davier et al., 2024; von Davier, 2026), they have not as of yet been part of a unified architec-ture for AI-native assessments. What reliably links the target construct to content generation, content to psychometric calibration, calibration to score inference, and inference to the ultimate claims re-ported about a test taker? This progression has been presented as the chain of inferences in an argument-based framework for test validity (Kane, 1992, 2006). This paper investigates what sustains 

and preserves that inferential chain when core life-cycle processes become automated. In current operational practice, humans and prose-based documentation are the connective tis-sue that machines can act upon. Construct frame-works, test specifications, alignment studies, and calibration models are each written as descriptive prose, typically kept in separate documents, and maintained on their own, in formats machines can-not act on. Each artifact is relevant to its own stage of development, but there are not necessar-ily links to artifacts across stages. Humans make the connections by interpreting each document and links. As descriptive prose, these artifacts cannot easily be queried by a machine, validated system-atically, version-controlled, or enforced computa-tionally (for example, to constrain AI content gen-erators). As AI capabilities are introduced, often unsystematically, a shared representation becomes necessary to keep the validity argument intact. An AI-native measurement system is one de-signed from the ground up for AI. It assumes that AI participates in every stage of the assessment lifecycle, autonomously or not. This includes ev-erything from generating content to delivering tests, scoring responses, and monitoring quality. AI ap-1arXiv:2610.33901v1 [stat.AP] 24 Oct 2026 plications are not retrofitted onto a workflow built for human-only operation. This paper proposes an architecture, the Com-putational Assessment Stack (CAS), in which ev-ery stage of the measurement lifecycle is repre-sented computationally and designed to work to-gether. The CAS has four layers: a computational construct representation at its base; computational content generation ; computational psychometrics 

(delivery, estimation, and scoring); and an orches-tration layer in which agentic AI coordinates the system under human governance. The CAS is gov-erned by a single principle: every consequential measurement decision should have an explicit computational representation before it is auto-mated. 

The term computational requires precise defi-nition here. A construct specification is recorded 

when written down in a document, and made ex-plicit when its claims are stated rather than implied. A construct becomes computational when an as-sessment system can evaluate, execute, and enforce it programmatically. The system can query con-struct coverage (e.g., identifying subskills lacking items), validate content against blueprints, drive tar-geted item generation, and maintain version control over dependent validity claims. Unlike continuous embeddings or vector spaces, this representation is symbolic (composed of discrete, typed statements programmatically evaluated as database queries). Put simply: documented constructs can be read, explicit constructs can be understood, and compu-tational constructs can be executed. In short, the computational construct representation serves as a machine-readable test blueprint. The CAS responds to a broader shift in how assessments are built and run. Automated item pro-duction is already established in operational pro-grams (e.g., the Duolingo English Test’s item fac-tory; von Davier et al., 2024). The more consequen-tial change, however, is the rise of agentic AI sys-tems that act with increasing autonomy across the entire assessment lifecycle (von Davier, 2026). It is this shift that makes a shared, machine-actionable construct representation necessary. Delegating workflow autonomy requires explicit, machine-readable construct representations, task models, and blueprints that agents can programmatically query, satisfy, and be held accountable to. Accountability, in turn, requires a provenance record indicating what agents actually did. When agents generate and route content, provenance 

becomes central to validity. Provenance is a machine-readable record that captures authorship, version, grounding evidence, and empirical status (expert-asserted, data-confirmed, or data-contested) and provides a chain of custody for measurement claims. Due to the stochastic nature of AI-based content generation, we cannot fully explain why a model produced a particular item or item component. We can, however, log everything around the generation, such as the inputs and model version used, the item that was produced, the checks it passed or failed, and the decision to admit or reject it. That log is what makes each item’s path into the pool traceable. The computational construct representation makes this traceability possible, serving simultaneously as the AI agent contract, operational substrate, and audit record.

## 2 What Already Exists: The Lineage of Each Layer 

A proposal for an “AI-native architecture” invites the question of whether the field has already built formal, machine-actionable structure into assess-ment. It has, repeatedly and successfully. But each prior line of work formalizes one layer, or one link between layers, and none provides a persistent rep-resentation shared across the whole lifecycle. This section reviews that work, both to credit it and to locate what the stack adds. 

2.1 Design and validity frameworks: ECD, PADI, e-ECD, and Kane’s validity approach 

Evidence-centered design (ECD; Mislevy et al., 2003) and Kane’s validity framework (1992; 2006) provide the logical foundation for connecting claims, evidence, and validation warrants. While projects like PADI (Principled Assessment Designs for Inquiry; Mislevy and Riconscente, 2006) en-coded ECD design patterns as structured, machine-readable objects, these frameworks lack a persis-tent operational object, that is, a machine-readable construct representation that a running system can version, query, and enforce. 

2.2 Machine-actionable skill structures: knowledge space theory and learning maps 

Knowledge space theory (KST; Doignon and Fal-magne, 1985; Falmagne and Doignon, 2011) and learning maps (Kingston et al., 2016) established that formal, machine-actionable skill structures can operate at scale in programs like ALEKS and Dy-namic Learning Maps assessment. While these sys-3tems prove feasibility of a formal skill structure for operational tests, they lack generality and modular-ity. Their underlying structures are single-purpose or bespoke, treat alternative decompositions as mu-tually exclusive, do not account for representation uncertainty, and were not designed to constrain AI generation. Recent work extends this line to the language domain. Ribeiro-Flucht and Chen (2026) encode CEFR A1–A2 English grammar as a typed graph of forms and functions, validate its mappings with expert annotation, and use it to guide LLM-based tutoring. Such resources supply the content of a construct representation but not its governance, meaning they are not versioned and not linked to a measurement model. Moreover, they treat their consensus graph as a single decomposition. 

2.3 Diagnostic structures: Q-matrices, attribute hierarchies, and diagnostic classification models 

The Q-matrix (Tatsuoka, 1983) bridges construct and model by specifying which skills each item re-quires, while attribute hierarchies (Leighton et al., 2004) and hierarchical diagnostic classification models (DCMs; Templin and Bradshaw, 2014) add prerequisite relations that are included in a con-struct graph. Rather than introducing a new sta-tistical model, Section 5 proposes an architectural generalization. Classical Q-matrices and attribute hierarchies become views of a richer construct rep-resentation in a graph rather than hand-written ta-bles. 

2.4 Constrained generation: cognitive design systems and automatic item generation 

Embretson’s (1998) cognitive design systems and the early work on automatic item generation (AIG; Bejar, 2002; Gierl and Haladyna, 2013) pio-neered item generation constrained by formal mod-els, where cognitive templates generate families of items whose psychometric properties are pre-dictable from design features. The CAS’s gen-eration layer (Section 6) inherits this lineage but expands its scope. While a template constrains a single item family, the construct representation con-strains every family and remains attached through calibration and reporting. Consequently, graph-constrained generation can target new subskills without new templates and enforce global rules like blueprint coverage. Furthermore, the stack strictly separates item generation from item pool admission, the governed step where items enter operational use with validated construct links. 

2.5 Feature-explained parameters: explanatory IRT 

Explanatory item response theory, from Fischer’s (1973) linear logistic test model (LLTM) through the general framework of De Boeck and Wilson (2004), models item parameters as functions of item properties rather than estimating them freely. Section 7’s proposal, in which item parameters are functions of an item’s position in the construct graph, sits squarely in this tradition, as does re-cent work that models item difficulty from item content (Netík and Martinková, 2026). The ex-tension is representational, where the LLTM ex-plains difficulty via hand-engineered features, the graph-explanatory model explains item parameters through a structured representation of the construct itself. 

2.6 Computational psychometrics 

Computational psychometrics (von Davier, Mis-levy, and Hao, 2021) blends data-driven computer science methods with theory-driven psychomet-ric modeling across digitally mediated learning and assessment systems (see also von Davier and Yan, 2026). Recent applications include LLM-based scoring, automated coding of communication data, and machine-learning parameter calibration (Sharpnack et al., 2025, Sharpnack et al., 2026; Hao et al., 2026), including scalable Bayesian ex-planatory IRT engines designed for large banks of sparsely linked, automatically generated items (Ny-dick et al., 2026). We use the term computational psychometrics both for the discipline and, narrowly, for the CAS’s third layer, where its models reside. The stack as a whole can be read as computational psychometrics made architectural. 

2.7 What is missing 

The paper’s novelty can be defined by five increas-ingly demanding capabilities: (i) specifying rela-tionships among assessment components; (ii) stor-ing and acting on formal representations in an operational system; (iii) versioning those repre-sentations and tracking provenance; (iv) carrying a single representation through generation, cali-bration, inference, and reporting; and (v) treat-ing the representation itself as uncertain and eval-uating competing versions. Comparing existing frameworks reveals structural asymmetry—ECD and Kane reach (i), PADI reaches (ii) at design 4time, ALEKS reaches (iii) for a closed structure, DLM sits between (ii) and (iii), and the L2 Net-work (Ribeiro-Flucht and Chen, 2026) reaches (ii) operationally and (iii) only partially, through its re-leased endorsement data. To our knowledge, none combines lifecycle persistence, versioned prove-nance, modular revision, and representation uncer-tainty. The CAS claims this complete combination of a persistent, machine-actionable construct rep-resentation defined by modularity and provenance across the entire assessment lifecycle, as shown in Figure 1. 

## 3 Layer 1 Foundations: What Is a Computational Construct Representation? 

This section defines the foundational object of the CAS, the Computational Construct Representation .

3.1 Definition 

We define a computational construct representation as a formal object consisting of: 1. Entities. Constructs, subskills, facets, text types, proficiency levels, task features, response features, scoring rules, evidence types. 2. Attributes. Properties of entities, such as level, domain, language, or a distributional parameter. 3. Relations. Typed, directed edges between enti-ties, such as requires , prerequisite-of , aligned-to , elicits , evidences . A typed relationship is an edge that includes a label saying what type of connection it is. 4. Constraints. Formal conditions on the graph, such as the condition that every item must be aligned to at least one descriptor. For example, every B1 item (in a CEFR sense) must use a text type permitted at B1. 5. Provenance. For each entity, relation, or con-straint, a record of who or what introduced it, when, and on what basis. 6. Versions. The representation changes over time, under versioning (downstream inferences refer-ence the version that justified them). The first three characteristics (entities, attributes, relations) describe the content of the representa-tion, and most of the prior structures reviewed in Section 2 possess them. A Q-matrix has entities and relations, a learning map has all three. The last three (constraints, provenance, versions) describe the governance of that content, and they are what distinguish a computational construct representa-tion from both a static specification document and its formal predecessors. The six properties charac-terize the representation as a whole, and the two graphs and the alignment introduced in Section 3.3 all carry them. A useful way to understand the construct graph is to think of it as a machine-readable test blueprint. Where a table of specifications records the intended test structure for human readers, the graph captures those same commitments, along with their prove-nance and versions, in a form the system can query and enforce. Every statement in the graph can be read as a subject–relation–object triple. In the machine learning literature model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) represent a similar concept. The con-struct representation plays a similar documentary role for the construct, and the system itself can enforce what the record says. A fair question is whether a graph can capture everything a careful prose specification conveys, let alone everything experts know. It cannot. Any rep-resentation is a simplification. But a prose specifi-cation is a simplification too. The difference is that the graph’s simplifications are explicit, inspectable, and revisable, and what the representation omits can be named, debated, and added, module by mod-ule. Some constructs may be harder to represent as a graph altogether, as we discuss later in the paper. 

3.2 Psychometric consequences 

Each group of properties has a direct psychomet-ric consequence. Entities, attributes, and relations make the representation queryable , and coverage and gap analyses become computations rather than manual compilations. Constraints make it enforce-able , and a misaligned item is caught at screening instead of discovered retrospectively. Provenance makes it auditable , and the basis for any alignment judgment or decomposition can be examined and challenged. Versions make it traceable , and there-fore, every claim links to the representation and model versions that supported it, which matters in high-stakes programs where frameworks, specifi-cations, and models change over time. The case study presented later in this paper illustrates each capability in turn. None of the consequences represents validity ev-idence. Queryability tells us where the gaps are, 5not whether the categories are right. Enforcement tells us an item conforms to the blueprint, not that the blueprint is sound. Auditability and traceabil-ity tell us which claims were made under which 

assumptions, not that the assumptions were war-ranted. The properties create the objects on which validation can operate and the validation remains to be done. 

3.3 Two graphs, one relation 

Throughout the rest of the paper we distinguish two graphs. The construct graph , GC , contains the entities, attributes, and relations that constitute the construct, that is, what is to be measured. The 

assessment/evidence graph , GA, contains the en-tities that constitute the assessment (items, task features, response features, scoring rules, psycho-metric parameters, observations, evidence, infer-ences), what is administered, observed, and in-ferred. Alignment is a relation between the two graphs, representing a set of links, each pairing an element of GC with the elements of GA that are claimed to instantiate it. We write A ⊆ GC × GA.The relation is not intrinsic to either graph. It is a set of claims about how the assessment instantiates the construct. A construct graph can be specified before any item exists, and an assessment graph accumulates content as items are written, admin-istered, and calibrated. Alignment is the mapping between them. 

What the alignment contains. The links in A

are typed, and they are of two kinds. Construct-alignment links connect an item to the parts of the construct it is claimed to measure (an item targets 

a subskill, or is aligned to a framework descriptor). 

Operational-conformance links connect an item to the specifications it is built to satisfy, that is, an item instantiates a task template, or uses a text type permitted at its target level. The classical Q-matrix is a slice of the first kind. Write s1, . . . , s K for the subskill nodes of the active Stage 1 module. Restricting A to subskill–item pairs gives qik = 1 

exactly when (sk, i ) ∈ A under the targets rela-tion (Section 5). Relations that stay within one graph are not alignment, meaning that a prerequi-site between two subskills belongs to GC , and the response features of an item belong to GA.

Asserted and empirical alignment. Every link in A begins as a claim, asserted by an expert panel or proposed by an AI generator and accepted in review. Response data can then confirm or contest it. We therefore distinguish the asserted alignment, which the system enforces at screening and which can fix the structure of a psychometric model (the hard constraints of Section 7.1), from its empiri-cal status , which records whether the data support each link (the provenance labels of Section 5.1 and the soft constraints of Section 7.1). In these terms, empirical Q-matrix validation (de la Torre, 2008; Chiu, 2013; de la Torre and Chiu, 2016) is empiri-cal validation of one slice of A.

Alignment as a measurement problem. Many alignment questions can be formulated as graph comparison problems. Alignment questions arise at two levels. At the level of a single link, the ques-tion is whether a claimed correspondence holds: whether an item meets with the specification it was written to, or whether its content corresponds se-mantically to the descriptor it is aligned to. At the level of a pool or a form, the question is how well GA as a whole instantiates GC , as in blueprint coverage. For the second level, given a target con-struct graph GC , an assessment graph GA, and the alignment A, one can define a family of similar-ity functions combining node coverage, relational agreement, prerequisite-path agreement, and distri-butional agreement over content categories, with different weights. Any such function is a candidate metric . Before interpretation, its reliability, sen-sitivity to relevant graph changes, and invariance across populations need to be established. 

3.4 Design decisions 

Building a computational construct or assessment representation requires several decisions. 

Implementation technology. Several standard technologies can host the computational representa-tion. One is an RDF (Resource Description Frame-work) knowledge graph (Cyganiak et al., 2014), a web-standard format in which every statement is a subject–relation–object triple. RDF comes with SHACL (Shapes Constraint Language; Knublauch and Kontokostas, 2017), a language for automated checks on graph structure that acts as automated blueprint enforcement, and SKOS (Simple Knowl-edge Organization System; Miles and Bechhofer, 2009), a vocabulary for recording correspondences to external frameworks. Alternatives are a property graph in a graph database, or a relational database schema with integrity constraints. The machine-level encoding of the case study for implementers is given in the Appendix. The paper’s argument rests 6on the general claim that the construct becomes a computational object inside the assessment sys-tem (stored, queried, checked, and versioned by the same infrastructure that manages items, param-eters, and scores) rather than a document consulted alongside it, hence it is independent of any one of these technologies. 

Authoring procedure. How the graph is au-thored (how its content is elicited and encoded) is itself part of the validity argument. Candidate procedures include consensus-based expert decom-position, independent-then-aggregated expert de-composition, and LLM-extracted decomposition (with or without expert validation). Each has dif-ferent properties with respect to reliability, cover-age, and defensibility, and the authoring procedure used should be documented in the representation’s provenance. 

Grain size. A finer-grained representation is more informative but harder to link to standard sta-tistical models. While a binary Q-matrix is coarse and easy to model, it discards substantive infor-mation. This is the familiar question of attribute grain size from the diagnostic-modeling literature, extended to the whole representation. This paper takes no position on the optimal grain size. The claim here is that the modular architecture proposed in the next section makes the trade-off explicit, in-spectable, and revisable. 

## 4 Modularity: Designing Against Ossification 

4.1 The ossification problem 

Once a construct is formalized as a graph, three risks emerge: provisional claims harden into struc-tural commitments, alternative decompositions be-come invisible, and low-level revisions trigger cas-cading invalidations across items, calibrations, and reports. Relying solely on human governance (“hu-mans approve changes”) does not solve how to make revision affordable. Without explicit architec-tural design, a computational representation risks becoming less flexible than the prose it replaces. This is a neglected risk that partly explains why be-spoke operational graphs remain difficult to main-tain and compare. 

4.2 A modular architecture 

We propose a modular architecture for GC , GA,and A, organized into four stages, each indepen-dently replaceable, governed by a design princi-ple that dependencies flow downward. Higher stages commit to less. Lower stages are replace-able without invalidating the stages above them. The architecture maps onto Cronbach and Meehl’s (1955) construct–observable distinction and Kane’s argument-based validity, where each stage transi-tion represents an inference with a corresponding warrant. Figure 2 summarizes the four stages. • Stage 0: Core construct ontology. The schema defining the kinds of entities and relations that 

GC , GA, and A may contain, not any specific content. For example, defining allowable entities, relation types (including the types of alignment link), and formal axioms (e.g., that every con-struct has at least one facet). This stage commits to almost nothing, remaining stable across frame-work revisions. • Stage 1: Framework decomposition modules. 

It includes decompositions of a core construct into framework-specific subskills (e.g., CEFR B1 Reading, or Intermediate Reading in the Profi-ciency Guidelines of the American Council on the Teaching of Foreign Languages, ACTFL, 2024). Multiple modules may coexist, each ver-sioned and validated separately. Stage 1 modules belong to GC .• Stage 2: Operationalization modules. It in-cludes context-specific task templates, text types, lexical bands, and delivery constraints that de-pend on Stage 1 modules without being entailed by them. Stage 2 modules also belong to GC be-cause they specify what an admissible item must look like, while the items themselves belong to 

GA.• Stage 3: Empirical modules. It includes GA

(the items as written, administered, and cali-brated, with their parameter estimates, evidence records, and posteriors) together with the empir-ical status of each link in A, such as an item-to-subskill link that the data confirm or contest. Stage 3 instantiates the upper stages without ever modifying them. Formally, let G(l) denote the set of modules at stage l, each module carrying its own version. The construct graph is the union of the active Stage 1 and Stage 2 modules, GC = G(1) ∪G(2) , written in the schema G(0) ; Stage 3 holds GA and the empir-ical status of A. Dependencies flow GA → A →

7GC , and within GC , G(2) → G(1) → G(0) , that is an item depends on the links that align it, and those links depend on the construct elements they point to, never the reverse. Three properties follow: • Local revision. Replacing a Stage 2 module leaves Stage 0 and Stage 1 in place; only the links in A that point to the replaced module, and the items and estimates in GA that depend on those links, are flagged for re-validation. The cost of a revision is bounded by the stage in which it occurs. • Composability. Multiple modules can coexist at each stage; the graph is a lattice, not a tree, mean-ing that a subskill or operationalization may serve several parents, and alternatives may sit side by side rather than one decomposition excluding all others. Each coexisting Stage 1 module m car-ries its own alignment Am, which is what allows inferences to be compared, or combined, across modules (Section 9). • Traceability. Every claim is traceable to a spe-cific configuration of module versions in GC , the links in A it relied on, and the objects in GA that supplied its evidence. 

## 5 The Q-Matrix as a View of the Alignment 

The Q-matrix is part of the alignment A (Sec-tion 3.3), being the slice of A that pairs items with subskills. The graph representation therefore gen-eralizes the representational scope of the Q-matrix rather than replacing it. It embeds item–skill in-cidence within a richer set of typed relations, and a Q-matrix can then be derived as a view of that representation and supplied to a compatible psycho-metric model. A Q-matrix, a prerequisite hierarchy, and an item-to-domain mapping can coexist in one representation. However, this generalization is rep-resentational, not statistical. The classical Q-matrix (Tatsuoka, 1983) is a bi-nary incidence structure Q = ( qik ), with qik = 1 

if item i requires skill k and 0 otherwise. It bridges the construct and the psychometric model. The representation embeds this incidence matrix within a family of typed relations, which differ in where they sit: • Item–subskill incidence (in A). qik ∈ { 0, 1}:whether item i requires subskill sk; these are the targets links between GA and the Stage 1 modules of GC .• Item–domain incidence (in A). did ∈ { 0, 1}:whether item i belongs to content domain d.• Skill prerequisite structure (within GC ). pjk ∈{0, 1}: whether subskill sj is a prerequisite for subskill sk — the relation modeled by the at-tribute hierarchy method (Leighton et al., 2004) and hierarchical DCMs (Templin and Bradshaw, 2014). This structure will need both feasibil-ity checks and structural constraints to keep the dependencies computable. A graph can better handle dependencies. • Item–evidence-type incidence (within GA). 

eie ∈ { 0, 1}: whether item i elicits evidence type e (selected response, constructed response, process). The classical Q-matrix is the special case in which P , E, and D are trivial, and the attribute hierarchy is the special case in which E and D

are trivial. The generalization is that the Q-matrix is one relation in a family of typed relations, the family is the substantive specification, and each relation carries provenance and a version. Where a relation sits matters. For example, Q and D are claims that cross from the assessment to the con-struct and inherit the measurement status of A, P is a claim about the construct alone, and E describes the assessment alone. Because graph relations could serve different statistical roles, the mapping from graph to model must be explicitly declared. A graph-derived Q-matrix inherits standard identifiability challenges, such as the completeness and non-redundancy con-ditions established in the DCM literature (Gu and Xu, 2021), and must be validated against expert-derived and empirically derived alternatives (de la Torre, 2008; Chiu, 2013). Such validation asks whether the targets links in A are supported by re-sponse data. Ultimately, the graph’s contribution is not to replace these established methods, but to provide a richer, relational, provenance-bearing specification against which they can be rigorously compared.

## 6 Layer 2: Computational Content Generation 

With the construct graph in hand, item generation becomes a constrained generation problem in the lineage of cognitive design systems and AIG (Em-bretson, 1998; Gierl and Haladyna, 2013), with the shared construct representation replacing the per-family template as the constraining object. Genera-tion proceeds by querying the graph for the target descriptor, subskills, and permissible task features, then prompting a generator (an LLM, an agentic workflow, or a template-based script) with the re-sulting subgraph as context. The pipeline is: Specify → Generate → Struc-tural screening (checks of form and metadata) →

Semantic evaluation (checks of meaning) → Hu-man review → Pilot → Calibration → Admission to the item pool. Screening and semantic evaluation run automatically, before any human review. This ordering is the design proposal, and operational programs already implement parts of it. We emphasize the distinction between genera-tion and admission . Generation is unconstrained production, meaning that a generator can produce any string, and prompting alone does not reliably 9yield proficiency-aligned content (Benedetto et al., 2025). Admission is governed measurement, that is, the step by which an item enters the operational pool with explicit, validated links to the construct. The construct graph constrains admission, not gen-eration. Structural screening runs the graph’s ad-mission constraints mechanically on every candi-date as soon as it is generated. Admission proper is the final step, after review, piloting, and calibra-tion. The consequence is that generation can be fast, exploratory, and cheap, while admission is slow, constrained, and documented, and the two can be run by different models, different agents, and different governance. The Item Factory (von Davier et al., 2024) pro-vides an operational workflow for this separation of generation and admission. Subject-matter experts design tasks, AI generates items at scale, humans review for quality and fairness, differential item functioning (DIF) analyses monitor for bias, and monitoring continues after launch. The construct graph leaves this workflow intact. It makes the links between generation, review, and calibration explicit and machine-traceable. When an admitted item later exhibits DIF or misfit, the graph makes it possible to ask which construct claims the item was admitted under, and whether other items admitted under the same claims share the problem. Over a graph, that question is a simple query, while over a traditional document trail, it is a complicated and time-consuming process. 

## 7 Layer 3: Computational Psychometrics in the CAS 

The construct graph specifies the substantive struc-ture, which is what the psychometric work should be about. This section proposes graph-constrained models, graph-explanatory models, and a model comparison that makes the framework falsifiable.

7.2 The graph as an explanatory representation 

Graph-constrained IRT specifies which dimensions an item may measure but leaves its permitted dis-crimination parameters and difficulty to be esti-mated from response data. A stronger formulation uses the construct graph to explain the item param-eters themselves. In this formulation, the graph is not merely a constraint on the psychometric model, but it is the representation from which item param-eters are predicted. Let Gi denote the graph-based representation of item i. It includes the relevant construct nodes and relationships activated by the item, such as the depth and specificity of the skills involved, their prerequisite relationships, and the item’s position relative to proficiency levels. The baseline model should be a hierarchical explanatory IRT model using prespecified graph-derived features. For example, item difficulty might be modeled as 

bi = β0 + β1 Depth( Gi)+ β2 PrerequisiteComplexity( Gi)+ β3 SkillDistance( Gi) + εi,

where the graph-derived predictors are defined before model fitting and εi captures residual item-level variation. The three predictors pro-posed here are standard node-level graph mea-sures. Depth( Gi) is the level of the item’s targeted subskills in the prerequisite hierar-chy; PrerequisiteComplexity( Gi) is the num-ber of distinct subskills required by the targeted subskills (Falmagne and Doignon, 2011); and 

SkillDistance( Gi) is the graph distance (shortest-path length) between the item’s targeted subskills, capturing how dispersed the item’s demands are 11 across the construct graph. These are illustrative operationalizations computed from GC and the item’s targets links in A; the exact definitions are themselves design choices to be recorded with the model’s provenance. Other graph-measures may be considered and tested. Discrimination parameters can be modeled analogously, subject to appropriate positivity, dimensionality, and identification con-straints. This baseline is interpretable and can be estimated using standard psychometric methods. This formulation extends the explanatory IRT tradition. The Linear Logistic Test Model (LLTM; Fischer, 1973) is a special case in which item dif-ficulty is expressed as a linear function of coded cognitive operations. De Boeck and Wilson (2004) provide a broader framework for explanatory item response models. The proposed extension is to use a structured, versioned representation of the construct graph as the explanatory basis for item parameters, rather than relying exclusively on man-ually engineered item-feature vectors. Three distinct prediction tasks should be distin-guished: 1. Parameter prediction for calibrated items: 

estimating item parameters from explanatory information and evaluating them against param-eters obtained from response data. 2. Parameter recovery from a reference calibra-tion: assessing whether the explanatory model can recover parameters established by an inde-pendent or reference calibration. 3. Prediction for genuinely new items: predict-ing item parameters before any response data are available for those items. The third task is the most demanding deploy-ment scenario. Strong performance on held-out items from familiar templates does not establish that the model can generalize to genuinely novel items or item families. The proposed architecture is therefore 

Gi → (ai, b i) = fϕ(Gi) → IRT model → Xpi .

The graph explains the item parameters, and the IRT model retains the measurement structure that relates those parameters to person abilities and ob-served responses. This differs from directly pre-dicting item difficulty or response accuracy from the graph without an explicit measurement model. In the latter case, the graph may support prediction, but its role in explaining the measurement process is less clearly specified. For newly generated items, graph-predicted pa-rameters should initially be treated as provisional estimates or as the basis for informative priors, not as established calibration results. As response data accumulate, the parameters can be updated through a hierarchical model, consistent with item-family calibration approaches (Glas and van der Linden, 2003; De Boeck and Wilson, 2004). This approach is also consistent with the proposal that predicted parameters for new items serve as provisional pri-ors that are subsequently updated with calibration evidence (Sharpnack et al., 2026), and with op-erational calibration engines built for this setting, in which feature-informed priors for automatically generated items are updated as sparse response data accumulate (Nydick et al., 2026). 

7.3 A falsifying comparison: four models 

The explanatory claim becomes testable through a comparison of four models. The comparison asks whether the construct graph provides sufficient in-formation to explain item parameters, and whether it adds value beyond conventional item features. • M0 — Conventional IRT: Item discrimination and difficulty parameters are estimated freely from response data. This model provides a ref-erence for fit and predictive performance when item parameters are calibrated directly, rather than predicted from explanatory variables. • M1 — Feature-explanatory IRT: Item param-eters are modeled as functions of conventional item features, (ai, b i) = fϕ(zi), where zi is a vector of conventional item features (linguistic, content, format). • M2 — Features-plus-graph explanatory IRT: 

Item parameters are modeled using both con-ventional features and graph representations, 

(ai, b i) = fϕ(zi, G i).• M3 — Graph-only explanatory IRT: Item pa-rameters are modeled from the graph representa-tion alone, (ai, b i) = fϕ(Gi).Here fϕ denotes an explanatory mapping in the sense of Section 7.2, with parameters ϕ estimated separately under each model. The models differ only in what information the mapping is allowed to use, and ai and bi denote its outputs, the item parameters used in the response model. 12 The principal comparison is M3 against M2, with M1 establishing the predictive value of con-ventional explanatory features and M0 providing a reference based on freely estimated item parame-ters. If M3 performs comparably to M2 on appro-priate out-of-sample evaluations, this would sup-port the claim that the graph captures much of the information in conventional item features that is relevant to the modeled item parameters. It would not, by itself, establish that the graph captures every construct-relevant property of an item. If M3 underperforms M2, the difference is diag-nostically useful. Conventional features may con-tain construct-relevant information missing from the graph, suggesting that the representation needs to be enriched. Alternatively, their apparent pre-dictive advantage may reflect construct-irrelevant properties, suggesting a need for tighter feature screening. The gap may also reflect limitations of the mapping function fϕ, rather than deficiencies in the graph itself. These possibilities require a separate investigation. For newly generated items, predicted parame-ters should be treated as uncertain and updated as response evidence accumulates. The graph can record each item’s parameter provenance and cal-ibration status. For example, it can record graph-predicted , provisionally calibrated , or empirically calibrated item parameters along with the model and graph versions used to obtain the estimates. These labels describe the evidence supporting the parameters, not guaranteed levels of accuracy. 

## 8 Layer 4: Agentic Orchestration and Governance 

The orchestration layer coordinates assessment workflows, such as content generation, item recali-bration, and quality review, using agentic AI under human governance. The principles underlying the orchestration are: 1. Construct-Authority Principle: Agents may query the representation and propose changes to the construct graph GC and the alignment A, but cannot autonomously authorize them. Human approval remains mandatory for changes to what is measured and to what counts as measuring it (Figure 3). 2. Modular Governance: Agents propose ver-sioned module updates across stages (e.g., alter-native decompositions or operationalizations), which require appropriate human approval be-fore becoming operational: module version v → (agents query and propose) 

→ module version v + 1 → (human approval) 

→ operational. 3. Centralized Provenance: Every reported claim (e.g., mastery classifications) is fully recon-structible, linking active representation versions, measurement models, item administrations, and decision rules. 

## 9 Construct-Representation Uncertainty 

The modular architecture reveals a new source of uncertainty. This is the uncertainty about the con-struct representation itself. Different expert panels may propose different decompositions of a compe-tency, different frameworks may identify different subskills, and the literature may support more than one interpretation. This uncertainty occurs at two levels. 

Edge-level uncertainty. A single relationship in the graph (a prerequisite between two subskills, say) may or may not be supported by the avail-able evidence. This level is handled by machin-ery already introduced. The provenance labels of Section 5.1 (expert-asserted, data-confirmed, data-contested) record each edge’s current status, and the evidence trail records how that status was reached and revised. A contested edge triggers expert review rather than silent deletion. 

Module-level uncertainty. The harder case arises when whole decompositions disagree. Two Stage 1 modules may differ in the number of sub-skills, their definitions, or their organization, and both may be admissible , that is grounded in the-ory, endorsed by experts, and consistent with the data so far. When admissible modules coexist, the system has three responses, in increasing order of ambition. First, report per module . The default is that ev-ery claim already carries, in its provenance, the module version and alignment Am under which it was computed (the Centralized-Provenance princi-ple of Section 8), so claims from different modules can coexist without being merged. Second, report what is robust . Sensitivity analy-sis recomputes consequential claims (proficiency classifications, decisions about individuals) under each admissible module and asks whether they 13 Figure 3: The Computational Assessment Stack with human governance and data flows. change. Hence, the recommendation is to report conclusions only at the level of detail that the avail-able representations support consistently. If the modules agree on an overall proficiency classifi-cation but differ in how they define particular sub-skills, the overall classification is defensible while subskill-level claims require qualification. Third, combine the modules’ predictions . Com-bination is legitimate only for a claim that means the same thing under every module being combined. This means that there must be an explicitly defined decision rule that can be applied to each module’s posterior distributions, together with a documented mapping from each module’s outputs to the com-mon claim. An overall proficiency classification may qualify, if each module supports the same classification and the thresholds have a defensible shared interpretation. A claim about a particular subskill generally does not qualify, when the mod-ules define their subskills differently. Whether the meanings correspond is an expert judgment, not a computation, and where no correspondence can be established the modules are evaluated separately. When the condition holds, the combined proba-bility of a claim Cp about person p is a weighted average over the candidate modules Mm:

where P (Cp | Xp, M m) is the probability of the claim computed under module m’s alignment and measurement model, and the weights wm are non-negative and sum to one. Following predictive stacking (Yao, Vehtari, Simpson, and Gelman, 2018), the weights are estimated by optimizing the combination’s out-of-sample predictive perfor-14 mance. The right analogy is weighting forecasters by their track records, not voting on which fore-caster is right. Bayesian model probabilities ad-dress which candidate model generated the data. Stacking weights measure how much each module contributes to prediction (and a module can receive a large weight while remaining substantively wrong about the construct). 

What remains subjective. The formula does not remove expert judgment. The weights can only redistribute belief among the modules that panels actually wrote, and no mixture can represent a de-composition nobody proposed. Whether Cp means the same thing under M1 and M2 is decided by expert review, and combining without that warrant produces a number with no interpretation. And the weights do not indicate the probability that a module is correct. Evaluating representation un-certainty therefore requires complementary analy-ses, where experts establish which representations are defensible, predictive evaluations measure how well each performs, and sensitivity analysis deter-mines which conclusions depend on the choice. 

Cross-framework comparison. A more difficult case arises when assessments use different frame-works, such as the CEFR and ACTFL. If the frame-works define different constructs or make different claims, their outputs cannot simply be combined using the mixture above. Comparison first requires a crosswalk that specifies how claims in one frame-work correspond to claims in the other. One pos-sible approach is to estimate a mapping between their Stage 1 construct graphs, allowing correspon-dences to vary in strength rather than assuming exact equivalence. Such a mapping would require substantive validation and, where available, empir-ical evidence from a linking sample. Developing and validating cross-framework mappings remains an open problem. 

Invariance of the representation. A parallel question arises across populations and language versions. Measurement invariance asks whether a psychometric model behaves equivalently across groups; construct-representation invariance asks whether the representation itself does. If construct graphs for the reading inference exist in English and in Spanish, GC (English ) and GC (Spanish ),are they structurally equivalent? Semantically? Measurement-equivalent? These are distinct questions with direct fairness implications (Sec-tion 11.1), and the sensitivity analysis above com-plements traditional measurement-invariance test-ing by asking whether the representation, both its dimensions and relationships, remains sufficiently consistent for the intended comparisons.

## 11 Discussion 

11.1 Fairness considerations 

We distinguish five sources of bias in a graph-driven assessment system, the first two made visi-ble by the representation layer. They are construct representation bias (what is included or omitted), 

decomposition bias (whose structure is adopted), 

generation bias (whether generated content disad-vantages subgroups), measurement bias (traditional differential item functioning), and feedback bias 

(discrepant diagnostic pathways driven by model error). While formal representations make certain bi-ases auditable in ways prose cannot, they also in-troduce risks at the ontology and decomposition layers. Auditing such systems connects to a grow-ing literature on algorithmic audits (Raji and Buo-lamwini, 2019; Raji et al., 2020). This indicates that fairness should be evaluated not only at the response-to-score layer (AERA et al., 2014), but also at the construct-representation layer. Because culturally specific decompositions can embed bias before items are ever generated, the representation layer must be inspected directly, with construct-representation invariance serving as the formal instrument for that evaluation. Such checks might also help with the design of socioculturally respon-sive assessments (Bennett, 2023). Here, we propose a framework for investi-gation that distinguishes three forms of equiva-lence. They are structural (same nodes and re-lations), semantic (same meanings), and measure-ment (same parameters; Meredith, 1993; Millsap, 2011). While traditional measurement invariance assumes a single shared representation across sub-groups, computational-construct-representation in-variance extends this hierarchy to the representa-tion itself. For example, subgroup differences in prerequisite edges constitute structural evidence rather than item-level Differential item function-ing (DIF; Holland and Wainer, 1993). In cross-language adaptation (International Test Commis-sion, 2017), structural equivalence can be evaluated pre-calibration using graph metrics (e.g., edit dis-tance, subgraph isomorphism). Graph structure and measurement parameters can sometimes dissociate, which is why both structural and psychometric evi-dence must be evaluated together. 

11.2 Validation agenda and open problems 

The CAS generates testable hypotheses (H), and it identifies problems that are not yet testable. We organize both by the object they concern (the con-struct graph GC , the alignment A, and the assess-ment/evidence graph GA) followed by the genera-tion and governance layers that act on all three. 

The construct graph, GC . H1a, inter-expert agreement. Can expert construct specifications be represented computationally with acceptable inter-expert agreement? Given a set of CEFR descrip-tors, do independent experts working with a shared schema produce decompositions whose nodes and relations agree well above chance? In the closest published precedent, expert form–function map-pings for CEFR A1–A2 grammar reached mod-erate agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0 .58 ), with disagreement mostly additive rather than contradic-tory (Ribeiro-Flucht and Chen, 2026). Note that “experts” could be highly trained AI agents in the 19 future. 

H1b, empirical distinguishability. Can compet-ing decompositions be distinguished empirically? Agreement is not correctness. Do alternative Stage 1 modules produce measurably different down-stream results in alignment scores, difficulty pre-dictions, model fit, or reported classifications? If the results are indistinguishable, the mixture of Section 9 collapses harmlessly, and if they differ, the choice of module is consequential and must be defended. 

Open problems. a) Some constructs are harder to represent as static graphs (interactive, collabora-tive, dynamic constructs, and process constructs). They require representations that capture temporal and interaction structure, not merely node–edge structure. b) H1a and H1b are a beginning, but the field lacks a general framework for evaluating alter-native decompositions and for validating a module 

rather than a whole graph. 

The alignment, A. H2, alignment. Does graph-derived alignment agree with independent expert judgments, and are misalignments detected with ac-ceptable sensitivity and specificity? The alignment metrics discussed earlier need to be validated. 

Open problems. The conditions under which a graph-derived Q-matrix — the item–subskill slice of A — is identifiable for a given DCM or MIRT model need to be established, extending Gu and Xu (2021) to the derived-view setting. 

The assessment/evidence graph, GA, and infer-ence. H3, psychometric structure. Does graph-derived structure explain empirical item-response behavior? The test is the four-model design of Sec-tion 7.3, with performance on novel AI-generated items the most diagnostic criterion. 

H5, inference and reporting. Do graph-linked diagnostic claims show greater construct traceabil-ity and interpretive validity than conventional sub-scores, without losing psychometric precision? The graph may improve interpretability and traceabil-ity without improving reliability, since the two are distinct. 

Open problems. The graph-to-parameter map-ping f of Section 7.2 is a potential source of sys-tematic error distinct from the graph. The trade-off between interpretability, flexibility, and misspecifi-cation risk needs to be further explored. 

Generation and governance. H4, generation. 

Do graph-constrained generation systems produce items with better construct coverage and fewer sub-stantive misalignments than unconstrained genera-tion? A strong experiment compares unconstrained, prompt-constrained, and graph-constrained genera-tion on expert alignment, quality, diversity, psycho-metric characteristics, and rejection rate at screen-ing. 

Open problems. What is the right division of labor between human and AI agents in construct revision, and how should agentic decisions be au-dited? 

## 12 Conclusion 

The Computational Assessment Stack unifies an AI-native measurement system around a foundational, machine-readable construct representation. By in-tegrating modular knowledge graphs with content generation, explanatory psychometrics, and agen-tic orchestration with human governance, the stack transforms disparate assessment components into a single, traceable architecture. While building on established theory, from evidence-centered design to explanatory IRT, CAS’s core contributions are architectural: a persis-tent, versioned representation across the lifecycle, a modular design preventing ossification, and a for-mal treatment of representation uncertainty. Ulti-mately, AI optimizes within the layers, but humans govern what it is. The next frontier in assessment is not simply automating item generation or scoring, but build-ing the computational representations that connect construct theory, task design, evidence, statistical inference, and reporting, and then validating them.
