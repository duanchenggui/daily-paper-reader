Abstract 

Modeling the latent, multi-step reasoning processes of learners is a core chal-lenge for adaptive tutoring systems, which often rely on simple right/wrong answers rather than understanding how students think. Instantiated here for grammatical reasoning, this study introduces a diagnostic framework that makes students’ step-by-step problem-solving visible by analyzing their ac-tion sequences in an interactive learning game. The framework computes five interpretable strategy dimensions (e.g., systematic error correction, random guessing), estimates coordination demand from task structure and behav-ioral patterns, and classifies reasoning into four distinct states. Analyzing 36,234 interactions from 234 students working on 42 morphosyntactic exer-cises, we found that reasoning strategies vary primarily by context rather than stable traits (intraclass correlation = 0.14), and students show high arXiv:2610.35609v1 [cs.CL] 26 Oct 2026 persistence in unproductive states; specifically, 74.6% of failures driven by high coordination demand (rather than simple knowledge gaps) are followed by another failure without intervention. Our early warning model predicts validation success with strong accuracy (AUC = 0.826 using strategy pat-terns alone; 0.902 with additional sequential context) while avoiding circular reliance on current error counts. These findings demonstrate that making latent reasoning processes visible enables earlier, more targeted scaffolding that responds to how students are thinking rather than simply whether they succeed. While demonstrated in language learning, the framework provides a generalizable, interpretable architecture for diagnosing complex, structured reasoning in any domain. 

Keywords: diagnostic modeling, adaptive scaffolding, grammatical reasoning, strategy detection, early warning prediction, interpretable machine learning, learning analytics 

1. Introduction 

Intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) have fundamentally transformed educa-tional technology by providing personalized, step-by-step guidance. Grounded in cognitive architectures like Adaptive Control of Thought—Rational (ACT-R) and formalized through inner-loop (step-level feedback) and outer-loop (task selection) designs, these systems excel in well-defined procedural do-mains where errors map directly to missing knowledge components [1, 2]. In parallel, recent advances in learning analytics and stealth assessment have begun to infer latent competencies from clickstream data, demonstrating that action sequences, timing, and choice patterns can reveal underlying rea-2soning strategies [3]. Together, these approaches have established a robust foundation for tracking what a student knows and whether they can apply it correctly. However, these frameworks struggle when applied to complex, non-linear reasoning tasks like morphosyntax. Recent sequence-based learning analyt-ics have revealed that, in this domain, learners do not simply retrieve static rules; rather, they treat every individual action as a dynamic hypothesis test about linguistic structure, converging toward a solution through highly in-dividualized paths [4]. Because language is a fundamentally nonalgorithmic domain—where the inherent irregularity of spelling or morphosyntax means no single strategy guarantees a correct outcome [5]—students are forced to constantly navigate multiple competing hypotheses, consistent with Siegler’s overlapping waves theory [6]. In this context, learners are juggling proba-bilistic, uncertain approaches rather than executing deterministic production rules. Consequently, traditional ITS models cannot reliably map these fluid actions to static knowledge states, and standard outcome-based metrics fail to capture the rich reasoning dynamics that precede a final answer. Scaf-folding systems are thus left blind to whether a student lacks foundational knowledge or is simply overwhelmed by the coordination demands of the task, risking interventions that are misaligned with the student’s actual thought process and disruptive to productive struggle [7]. To address this gap, we introduce a theory-driven diagnostic framework that makes hidden reasoning processes observable and actionable in real time. Rather than relying on opaque black-box predictors that lack pedagogical transparency [8] or retrospective outcome metrics, our pipeline transforms 3raw interaction sequences into interpretable, pedagogically meaningful sig-nals. Specifically, the framework concurrently computes five strategy dimen-sions (e.g., systematic error correction, random guessing) and estimates a Coordination Demand Index that captures both structural task complexity and behavioral struggle. These signals are integrated into a transparent, state-aware classification system that maps each reasoning cycle to distinct cognitive states. By explicitly excluding direct error counts from its predic-tive logic to avoid circularity, the framework provides a non-circular early warning mechanism. This enables a shift from uniform, outcome-based feed-back to real-time truly adaptive scaffolding that responds to how a student is reasoning, preserving learner autonomy during stable states while triggering targeted support during unproductive persistence. We validate this framework through a large-scale analysis of 36,234 inter-actions from 234 students across 42 distinct morphosyntactic exercises. Our empirical findings demonstrate that reasoning strategies are fundamentally state-dominant rather than trait-like (mean intraclass correlation = 0.14), justifying the need for real-time, context-aware adaptation over static profil-ing. Furthermore, we reveal that students exhibit high persistence in unpro-ductive states—specifically, 74.6% of failures driven by high coordination de-mand are followed by another failure without intervention. Our early warning model predicts validation success with strong accuracy (AUC = 0.826 using strategy patterns alone; 0.902 with sequential context), substantially outper-forming conventional ITS baselines. Ultimately, while instantiated here for language learning, this study provides a generalizable, interpretable archi-tecture for diagnosing complex, structured reasoning. The remainder of this 4paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews related work; Section 3 de-tails the framework; Section 4 presents empirical results; Section 5 discusses theoretical implications and scaffolding design; and Section 6 concludes. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Intelligent Tutoring Systems and Adaptive Learning Foundations 

Intelligent tutoring systems have long relied on structured architectures to diagnose learner states and deliver targeted support. Model-tracing ap-proaches rooted in cognitive architectures like ACT-R pioneered step-level diagnosis, demonstrating how systems can map discrete student actions to specific knowledge components for precise error remediation [1]. Expanding on this foundation, VanLehn’s [2] influential framework formalized the dis-tinction between the inner loop (step-level feedback) and outer loop (task se-quencing), establishing a comprehensive blueprint for real-time pedagogical decision-making. Meta-analytic evidence confirms that intelligent tutoring systems can substantially improve learning outcomes compared to traditional instruction, particularly in well-defined domains [9]. More recently, adaptive learning technologies have expanded beyond knowledge tracing to incorpo-rate pedagogical design principles that tailor instruction to individual learner trajectories [7]. While highly effective for procedural and well-structured do-mains, these frameworks primarily track the accumulation, application, or decay of discrete knowledge components. They are less equipped to handle reasoning processes characterized by non-linear hypothesis testing, compet-ing cognitive demands, and rapid strategy switching. In morphosyntactic tasks, learners frequently revise partial solutions, backtrack across agreement 5chains, and balance accuracy against working memory constraints. Captur-ing these fluid cognitive dynamics requires moving beyond static component tracing toward a diagnostic architecture that continuously evaluates strategy configurations and coordination demand in real time. 

2.2. Feedback Theory and Formative Assessment 

Effective scaffolding is fundamentally grounded in feedback theory and formative assessment practices. Hattie and Timperley’s [10] meta-analytic framework establishes that high-impact feedback must address three ques-tions: Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next? Similarly, Shute [11] distinguishes between directive feedback (providing explicit cor-rections) and facilitative feedback (prompting self-regulation), emphasizing that the latter better supports long-term retention and transfer. Sadler’s [12] seminal work further argues that formative assessment is only effective when learners can perceive the gap between current and target performance and possess actionable pathways to close it. Empirical studies in online tutoring confirm that immediate feedback during problem solving improves learning outcomes [13]. Yet, these models implicitly assume that the performance gap is either observable or easily articulated. In grammatical reasoning, the gap is often hidden behind unproductive strategy configurations, elevated cognitive load from managing multiple interdependent constraints, or flawed hypothesis testing (formalized as the Coordination Demand Index in Sec-tion 3.7). Students may possess the requisite grammatical knowledge but fail to apply it due to working memory constraints or misdirected attention. Our framework addresses this by diagnosing the gap in real time from pro-cess telemetry, transforming latent reasoning states into quantifiable signals 6that trigger context-aware, facilitative interventions precisely when the gap becomes pedagogically actionable. 

2.3. Stealth Assessment and Configuration-Aware Diagnosis 

The rise of game-based and digital learning environments has catalyzed stealth assessment methodologies, which infer learner competencies from in-game behaviors without disrupting the learning flow [3, 14]. By mapping clickstreams, response times, and choice patterns to latent traits, researchers have successfully quantified agency [15], modeled probabilistic relationships between actions and competencies using Bayesian networks [16], and tracked learning curves to identify knowledge acquisition bottlenecks [17]. Process mining techniques have further enabled the discovery of sequential behavioral patterns that predict learning styles and strategy preferences [18, 19, 20], while educational data mining reviews have highlighted the importance of distinguishing productive struggle from unproductive wheel-spinning [21]. Despite these advances, most stealth assessment approaches aggregate perfor-mance over extended periods or infer static competency profiles. They rarely capture the moment-to-moment interplay of multiple reasoning strategies or the dynamic shifts in coordination demand that precede validation outcomes. Our framework advances this tradition by modeling strategy configurations as concurrent, continuously updated signals. Rather than inferring whether a student “knows” a grammatical rule, we diagnose how they are reasoning about it in real time, enabling scaffolding that responds to transient cognitive states rather than retrospective proficiency estimates. Nevertheless, most current detectors of unproductive persistence rely on coarse behavioral aggregates and black-box models. For instance, Liu et al. 7[22] trained a struggle detector for an open-ended science game, achieving AUC = 0.635, insufficient for individual intervention. Karumbaiah et al. [23] predicted level quitting in a physics game with AUC = 0.81 using gradi-ent boosting and 101 features aggregated over 60-second windows, but the model required post-hoc partial dependence plots for interpretation. Within grammar ITSs, Deininger et al. [24] predicted first-attempt correctness on individual exercise fields in an English tutoring system. Their best model (XGBoost) reached AUC = 0.68, while logistic regression yielded only 0.57; the features were limited to prior performance, time, and attempt count, without any representation of reasoning strategies. These findings indicate that even in structured domains, standard behavioral features leave consid-erable predictive power untapped, and that transparent models often lag behind complex ensembles. Our framework directly addresses this gap by replacing opaque outcome-based features with five theory-driven strategy di-mensions and a coordination demand index, all computed in real time, and by showing that an inherently interpretable logistic regression can outperform the black-box alternatives. 

2.4. Metacognition, Self-Regulated Learning, and Cognitive Variability 

Metacognitive awareness and self-regulated learning (SRL) are critical drivers of complex reasoning performance. Flavell’s [25] foundational work established metacognition as the active monitoring and regulation of cogni-tive processes, while Winne and Hadwin’s [26] COPES model operational-ized SRL as a cyclical process of condition evaluation, strategy selection, and outcome monitoring. A large-scale meta-analysis has confirmed a con-sistent, positive relationship between self-regulated learning and academic 8achievement across childhood and adolescence [27]. In grammatical reason-ing, these processes manifest as learners’ ability to detect mismatches, adjust chain-completion priorities, and abandon unproductive hypotheses. How-ever, traditional SRL measurement relies heavily on self-report inventories or think-aloud protocols, which are intrusive, retrospective, and unable to capture rapid strategy shifts during task execution. Moreover, Siegler’s [6] overlapping waves theory demonstrates that cognitive variability is not noise but a signature of learning: students simultaneously hold multiple strate-gies and switch between them based on perceived efficacy and task demands. Our framework operationalizes metacognitive regulation directly from action sequences, translating theoretical SRL constructs into observable, validation-level metrics. By quantifying strategy stability, error-targeting precision, and adaptive displacement, we provide a real-time lens into how learners monitor and adjust their reasoning without interrupting the natural flow of practice. 

2.5. Interpretable Artificial Intelligence for Educational Decision-Making 

As machine learning becomes increasingly embedded in educational tech-nologies, the tension between predictive accuracy and pedagogical trans-parency has intensified. Rudin [28] argues forcefully against post-hoc expla-nations of black-box models in high-stakes domains, advocating instead for inherently interpretable algorithms whose decision logic can be audited and trusted by practitioners. In education, this transparency is essential: teachers and system designers must understand why a scaffolding trigger was activated to align interventions with pedagogical goals and avoid hint fatigue [29, 30]. Surveys of interpretability methods emphasize that feature importance, co-efficient transparency, and decision boundary clarity are prerequisites for 9educational deployment [31, 32]. Yet, many learning analytics pipelines still prioritize complex ensembles or deep architectures at the expense of explain-ability. Our framework explicitly adopts interpretable logistic regression with constrained feature engineering, ensuring that every prediction is traceable to specific strategy scores and coordination demand estimates. This design choice prioritizes pedagogical actionability over marginal gains in predictive performance, enabling educators to audit, adapt, and trust the scaffolding logic. 

2.6. Grammatical Reasoning and Intelligent Language Tutoring 

The acquisition of morphosyntax is governed by frequency effects, delib-erate practice, and the gradual automation of rule application [33, 34]. In-telligent language tutoring systems (ILTS) have historically relied on natural language parsers to analyze open-ended learner input, diagnosing errors in agreement, tense, and reference with high granularity [35, 36]. While parser-based approaches yield rich linguistic diagnostics, they are computationally intensive, domain-specific, and difficult to scale across diverse classroom con-texts. Recent curriculum analyses in French education further emphasize that morphosyntactic agreement remains a high-stakes bottleneck, requiring targeted intervention that moves beyond binary correctness scoring [37, 38]. Recent work in English grammar ITSs further supports a configuration-aware perspective: Deininger et al. [39] demonstrated that the same learning behav-iors (e.g., corrections after feedback) were positively associated with achieve-ment for some learners and negatively for others, depending on their prior knowledge and motivation. This underscores the risk of static, aggregate behavioral profiles and the need for real-time diagnosis that accounts for 10 the learner’s current cognitive and strategic state. Moreover, studies of au-tomated corrective feedback reveal that learner uptake depends heavily on cognitive readiness and motivational state, not merely error detection [40]. Our framework bypasses the scalability limitations of parser-based ILTS by leveraging structured game telemetry to extract grammatical reasoning pat-terns at scale. By quantifying chain competition, error-targeting efficiency, and validation pressure, we provide a computationally lightweight yet ped-agogically precise alternative that aligns with frequency-based acquisition models and contemporary language curriculum demands.

3.2. Learning Environment & Data Collection 

Data were collected from an interactive grammatical reasoning game de-ployed on the publicly funded, non-commercial platform [Reference blinded 12 for review], developed in partnership with cantonal educational authorities in French-speaking Switzerland. The game asks players to build grammatically correct sentences by moving sliders, each containing several words or different inflections of the same word. By repositioning sliders, players align words to respect agreement chains (e.g., subject-verb, determiner-noun, adjective-noun) and produce syntactically valid sentences. When a player believes a sentence may be correct, they can submit it for validation and receive binary feedback (correct/incorrect). The game de-liberately withholds explicit information about underlying agreement chains and does not localize errors (e.g., indicating which sliders are incorrect). By providing only binary outcomes, the design compels students to observe individual slots and infer syntactic dependencies through systematic trial, reflection, and hypothesis testing. This approach intentionally mirrors au-thentic language acquisition, where morphosyntactic rules are internalized through pattern recognition rather than explicit instruction, and we have consistently observed progressively deeper attentional engagement with this gameplay mechanic. Data were collected over a one-year period (April 2025–March 2026) from 11 classes across 8 schools in French-speaking Switzerland with primary school students aged 8-11, where the game was freely available for class-room use alongside other platform activities. The research was conducted with full informed consent from parents and teachers, in compliance with all applicable ethical guidelines; a complete ethics statement and study proce-dure description will appear in the final publication and are omitted here to preserve double-blind anonymity. This particular game was selected for anal-13 ysis due to its fine-grained decomposition of morphosyntactic reasoning task, yielding high-resolution action sequences suitable for diagnostic modeling. Each logged interaction includes: anonymized student identifier, class identifier, session start/end timestamps (enabling duration calculation), val-idation outcome (correct/incorrect), and a temporally ordered sequence of actions. Actions are of two primary types: tirette (slider movement, record-ing which slider was moved and to which position) and validate (submission attempt, recording the full sentence state at validation time). This teleme-try enables reconstruction of the student’s reasoning trajectory at the level of individual grammatical decisions.

This concur-rent computation approach aligns with process-mining methodologies that extract pedagogically meaningful patterns from sequential educational data [19], while extending linguistic pattern discovery techniques to grammatical reasoning contexts [20]. Each strategy captures a distinct pedagogical con-struct, is normalized to a [0,1] scale for direct interpretability in scaffolding decisions, and is computed in real time from validation-level action windows. Full mathematical specifications, edge-case handling, and normalization pro-cedures are provided in Appendix A (Equations 1–20). The five dimensions are defined as follows: • Verb-First targeting (Sverb ) quantifies whether students prioritize the verb slot early in their reasoning cycle and maintain sustained at-17 tention on its agreement chain. Sequence-based analysis in morphosyn-tactic games reveals that learners frequently anchor on the verb be-fore adjusting preceding elements, a non-linear processing pattern that aligns with pedagogical recommendations for identifying the syntactic pivot in French [4]. This strategy reflects early functional category ac-quisition and efficient working memory allocation during agreement chain resolution [36]. The dimension rewards systematic anchoring rather than peripheral tinkering (Equations 1–6). • Chain-Aware progression (Schain ) measures whether students com-plete or make measurable progress on one agreement chain before switch-ing to another. Cognitive load theory posits that sequential depen-dency resolution minimizes extraneous load when working memory is taxed, whereas frequent context-switching fragments attention and in-flates coordination demand [42]. This dimension captures adherence to sequential resolution principles, operationalizing systematic hypothesis testing across morphosyntactic structures (Equations 7–10). • Error-Focus (Sfocus ) computes the proportion of actions that actively reduce structural mismatch (Hamming distance) relative to the closest valid configuration. This operationalizes targeted error correction as a metacognitive monitoring strategy, directly linking to self-regulated learning frameworks where productive struggle is characterized by de-liberate mismatch detection and feedback utilization rather than ran-dom adjustment [26, 7]. By quantifying the precision of error-targeting, this dimension distinguishes systematic correction from superficial trial-18 and-error (Equations 11–12). • Randomness (Srand ) captures undirected exploration by combining chain-switching entropy with low error-reduction rates. While some exploration is necessary for hypothesis generation (Siegler’s overlap-ping waves theory), excessive randomness indicates disorientation or unguided guessing. This dimension explicitly distinguishes productive hypothesis testing from counterproductive thrashing, enabling scaffold-ing systems to intervene when exploration ceases to yield structural progress [6] (Equations 13–15). • Strategic Commitment (Sstrat ) identifies sustained pursuit of a non-initial candidate solution until it becomes at least as close as the previ-ously optimal path. This operationalizes hypothesis persistence, cap-turing whether learners commit to a structural hypothesis and system-atically refine it rather than prematurely abandoning viable configura-tions. High commitment scores reflect resistance to impulsive switching and align with convergence patterns observed in expert-like problem-solving trajectories [6] (Equations 16–18).

3.9. Personalization Potential (Variance Decomposition) 

To determine whether strategy use reflects stable learner traits or context-dependent states, we compute intraclass correlation coefficients following Shrout & Fleiss [47] for ICC(1) one-way random effects (Equations 42–45). 24 Variance is decomposed into student-level, session-level, and residual compo-nents (Equations 46–47). An ICC < 0.2 indicates state-dominant variation, where within-student variance across sessions substantially exceeds between-student differences. This statistical property directly informs scaffolding de-sign: low ICC values justify context-aware, real-time adaptation over static learner profiling, as reasoning strategies fluctuate significantly based on ex-ercise structure and immediate coordination demand. 

3.10. Early Warning Prediction Model 

To enable proactive intervention, we train a logistic regression model to forecast validation success using pre-validation features. We evaluate a hi-erarchy of nested models (M0–M3) to isolate component contributions; full results and ablation details are provided in Appendix B, Section 4.1. For the main model, the feature vector xv comprises the five concurrent strategy scores, two context variables (actions since last validation, prior quadrant), and two temporal derivatives: Hamming distance trend since the previous validation ( δh) and strategy stability over the last five validations ( σS ), com-puted as the mean standard deviation across all five strategy dimensions (Equations 48–51). Critically, current Hamming distance is explicitly ex-cluded to prevent circularity, ensuring predictive power derives from reason-ing patterns rather than direct error counts. We deliberately select interpretable logistic regression with L2 regulariza-tion over complex black-box architectures. As Rudin [28] argues, high-stakes educational decisions require transparent models whose decision logic can be audited, trusted, and aligned with pedagogical goals. Logistic regression provides coefficient-level interpretability, enabling educators and system de-25 signers to trace every prediction back to specific strategy configurations and demand estimates. This choice is further justified by evidence that sequential behavioral patterns reliably predict learner states when properly engineered [18], making non-linear complexity unnecessary for achieving robust discrim-inative performance. The model is trained via stratified 5-fold cross-validation with class-weight balancing to address the 38.1% success rate imbalance. Performance is evaluated using AUC, Average Precision, and accuracy at threshold 0.5. Feature importance is reported via standardized coefficients ( ˆwj /std (xj )), ensuring comparability across heterogeneous feature scales. Threshold op-timization for scaffolding triggers (balancing early detection with hint effi-ciency) and comprehensive robustness checks (generalization, signal stability, calibration) are documented in Appendix B, Sections 4–5 and 6.1. 

3.11. Scaffolding Policy Integration 

Diagnostic signals feed directly into a transparent, validation-state deci-sion policy for adaptive scaffolding. While this section outlines the intended mapping from diagnostic states to hint types, the pedagogical efficacy of these mappings has to be empirically validated in a controlled trial. The policy operates on two complementary layers: (1) a state-based trigger that evalu-ates concurrent strategy configurations and coordination demand, and (2) a 

trajectory modulator that incorporates temporal features ( δh for error-trend direction, σS for recent strategy stability) to optimize intervention timing. Concretely, when structural interference exceeds τ = 0 .35 and Error-Focus drops below baseline, the system identifies a High-Demand Failure state and can trigger facilitative hints that prompt re-evaluation rather than 26 direct correction. The trajectory modulator refines this logic: if δh indi-cates an accelerating error trend or σS signals erratic strategy switching, the trigger threshold could be adaptively lowered in future implementations to enable earlier, proactive support. Conversely, stable reasoning with low de-mand ( τ < 0.35 , high Sfocus , low σS ) preserves learner autonomy, avoiding hint fatigue and supporting productive struggle. This dual-layer architecture operationalizes stealth assessment principles, inferring latent reasoning states from observable telemetry without interrupting the natural learning flow [3]. Crucially, all temporal features are inherently interpretable: δh captures recent error-accumulation direction (not current error counts, preserving non-circularity), and σS reflects short-term strategy consistency. Because both are computed within the same validation-level window as core strategy scores, every trigger remains auditable and traceable to concrete reasoning dynamics. The framework is designed for teacher-AI complementarity rather than full automation; transparent trigger logic enables educators to audit, override, or refine scaffolding decisions based on classroom context and pedagogical priorities [29]. The conceptual decision logic is detailed in this section and Section 3.10, while threshold optimization results supporting risk-stratified intervention are documented in Appendix B, Section 6.1.

Variance decomposition con-32 firms that within-student, across-session variation accounts for the majority of total strategy variance. This statistical property has direct implications for scaffolding design: low ICC values justify context-aware, real-time adaptation over static learner profiling. Because strategy configurations fluctuate significantly based on exercise structure, immediate coordination demand, and validation history, effective support must respond to the student’s current reasoning state rather than historical averages. 5.2. Scaffolding Policy Design: Transparent, Configuration-Triggered Inter-vention 

The diagnostic signals can be mapped to a decision logic that prioritizes interpretability and learner autonomy. For example, when structural inter-ference exceeds τ = 0 .35 and Error-Focus drops below baseline, the system would identify a High-Demand Failure state and could trigger facilitative hints that prompt re-evaluation rather than direct correction. When prior quadrant classification indicates Low-Demand Failure (low demand, repeated failure), directive hints target specific rule application. Stable reasoning with low demand preserves autonomy, avoiding hint fatigue and supporting pro-43 ductive struggle. This architecture operationalizes Hattie and Timperley’s [10] feedback model: Feed Up (where is the learner in their hypothesis test-ing?), Feed Back (what error or demand pattern emerged?), and Feed For-ward (what to test next?). For grammatical reasoning, this translates to monitoring Error-Focus ( β = +1 .25 in strategy-only model) to detect when students stop targeting mismatches, then selecting hint type based on coordi-nation state. Following Shute’s [11] distinction between directive and facilita-tive feedback, the framework reserves explicit correction for knowledge gaps and employs metacognitive prompts for coordination bottlenecks. Threshold optimization (Appendix B, Section 6.1) ensures that intervention timing bal-ances early detection with hint efficiency, preventing over-scaffolding while maintaining reliable struggle detection. It is important to emphasize that this policy architecture is theoretically motivated but not yet empirically validated. Randomized controlled trials are needed to determine whether hints delivered at these diagnostic thresholds improve learning outcomes, reduce unproductive persistence, or accelerate error recovery relative to unassisted practice. 

5.3. Methodological Rigor, Interpretability & Computational Efficiency 

The framework’s design prioritizes pedagogical transparency over marginal predictive gains. By selecting logistic regression with L2 regularization and explicitly excluding current Hamming distance, we ensure that every pre-diction is traceable to specific strategy scores and demand estimates. The ablation analysis confirms that the strategy-only model (no sequential con-text) achieves AUC = 0.826, while the full model (+ sequential context) attains AUC = 0.902, yet strategy configurations alone sustain strong dis-44 criminative performance. This validates the non-circular design principle: predictive power derives from reasoning dynamics, not direct error deriva-tives. Comprehensive robustness checks (generalization across 42 exercises, temporal stability, noise resilience) confirm that signals are stable under re-alistic telemetry variability. From a comparative standpoint, these AUC values place our detector at the upper end of reported performance for real-time disengagement and fail-ure prediction. Liu et al. [22] obtained AUC = 0.635 in an open-ended science game, and Karumbaiah et al. [23] reached 0.81 in a physics game. Within grammar ITSs, Deininger et al. [24] predicted field-level correctness with AUC = 0.68 using XGBoost, and only 0.57 with logistic regression. Even when excluding sequential context and Hamming trend, our strategy-only model (AUC = 0.826) surpasses those benchmarks, and the full model (AUC = 0.902) achieves a level of discriminative power that is rare in educational early warning systems. Crucially, this is accomplished with a transparent logistic regression whose coefficients are directly interpretable, without re-course to post-hoc explanation methods. The comparison suggests that do-main structure, when explicitly leveraged through theory-grounded feature engineering, can simultaneously improve predictive accuracy and maintain full pedagogical transparency. While deep sequence models achieve strong predictive performance in educational data mining, we deliberately selected an interpretable architec-ture because grammatical reasoning involves pedagogically meaningful pat-terns (chain completion, error targeting, hypothesis persistence) that should be measured, not only inferred from raw sequences. In structured domains 45 where theoretical constraints are strong, forcing the model to respect ped-agogical categories ensures that every scaffolding trigger is traceable to a specific reasoning configuration. This design stance prioritizes human-AI complementarity over automation: educators can audit, override, and refine triggers because the decision logic is transparent, not latent. Furthermore, because the framework outputs low-dimensional, interpretable diagnostic sig-nals rather than opaque latent states, it is designed to interface with arbitrary feedback generation layers—including large language models—while preserv-ing full auditability of the trigger logic. The deeper rationale for selecting logistic regression is thus epistemic rather than computational. Neural sequence models may achieve marginal predictive gains, but they impose hidden costs that are particularly acute in classroom contexts: opacity under real-time constraints, data hunger in cold-start scenarios, and brittleness under distribution shift when task interfaces evolve. Theory-driven features preserve educator trust and diagnostic ac-tionability in ways that post-hoc explanations of neural architectures cannot replicate. That said, the pipeline is also computationally efficient enough for real-time classroom deployment. Benchmarking on researcher hardware (Apple M2/8GB RAM) establishes an upper-bound performance profile: the full diagnostic pipeline (feature computation, CDI estimation, and logistic re-gression inference) completes in 2.2 ± 0.7 ms per validation event (median:

5.4. Limitations & Future Directions 

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the current valida-tion focuses on French morphosyntax within a single educational context. While the game is deployed in over 20 languages—suggesting architectural reproducibility and cross-linguistic potential—formal validation across typo-logically distinct languages remains pending. Morphosyntactic complexity, agreement chain density, and orthographic transparency vary significantly across languages; future work will test whether strategy dimensions and CDI thresholds generalize beyond Roman languages. Second, the framework currently models cognitive and strategic signals, and the CDI already incorporates behavioral struggle indicators such as pause duration, rapid guessing, slot cycling, and validation pressure. However, it does not explicitly detect or respond to affective states. Control-value theory suggests that prolonged High-Demand Failure states likely co-occur with frus-tration [52, 53], and affect-aware systems can provide motivational support when such states are detected [54]. Future iterations will therefore integrate explicit affect-detection channels—for instance, by linking the existing CDI 47 behavioral signals to validated frustration labels or by incorporating multi-modal sensors—to deliver scaffolding that balances cognitive challenge with emotional regulation. Third, the study employs an observational design; while robustness checks confirm predictive validity, causal effects of hint timing and type on learning gains require randomized controlled trials. Fourth, the ICC = 0.14 finding is among the most theoretically conse-quential results of this study, as it challenges trait-based assumptions under-lying much of adaptive learning. Future work should treat this as a priority. Specifically: (1) Do these low ICCs replicate in other structured reasoning domains, or are they specific to morphosyntax? (2) What is the tempo-ral structure of within-student variation—do strategies fluctuate randomly, or do they follow systematic patterns tied to fatigue, exercise sequencing, or prior success? (3) Under what conditions (e.g., high task complexity) do stable traits re-emerge, as suggested by our complexity-band analysis? A dedicated follow-up exploring the boundary conditions of state-dominant versus trait-dominant variation would have significant implications for how the field conceptualizes learner modeling. Finally, the linear additive assumption in CDI and logistic regression captures primary effects effectively but may not fully model complex config-urational dynamics. Future work will explore targeted pairwise interaction terms and generalized additive models (GAMs) to capture non-linear rela-tionships while preserving the interpretability and computational efficiency required for classroom deployment. 48 5.5. A Generalizable Architecture for Structured Reasoning 

While the framework is instantiated here for French morphosyntax, its un-derlying architecture is domain-agnostic. It provides a transferable blueprint for structured reasoning tasks that produce sequential telemetry over a con-strained state space. Such tasks can be diagnosed through the same pipeline: raw actions are mapped to state vectors; strategy dimensions quantify the-oretically defined reasoning patterns; a coordination demand index captures structural and behavioral interference; and quadrant states enable trans-parent, real-time intervention triggers. For example, in algebra equation solving, state vectors represent term assignments, chains represent balanc-ing operations across the left and right sides, and CDI captures interference from competing transformation rules. In programming debugging, states represent variable assignments, chains represent data-flow dependencies, and strategy dimensions distinguish systematic hypothesis testing from random line-tweaking. 5.6. Broader Impact on AIED: Toward Hybrid Intelligence in Language Learn-ing 

The framework positions adaptive scaffolding within a hybrid intelligence paradigm, where machine precision complements human pedagogical exper-tise [30]. AI reliably detects morphosyntactic patterns, quantifies coordina-tion demand, and forecasts struggle probability; teachers interpret creative intent behind “errors”, adjust hint tone to classroom culture, and override triggers when pedagogical context warrants flexibility. Methodologically, the pipeline demonstrates that concurrent strategy min-ing, dynamic demand estimation, and non-circular prediction can be inte-grated into a single, auditable architecture. While the specific features (verb chains, morphosyntactic slots, slider telemetry) are domain-bound, the un-derlying approach—extracting latent reasoning configurations from sequen-tial interaction logs and mapping them to interpretable diagnostic states— offers a transferable blueprint for other structured reasoning tasks. Formal 50 cross-domain validation will be required to adapt the framework to mathe-matics, programming, or scientific inquiry, where constraint navigation and hypothesis testing follow different structural logics. By making reasoning vis-ible, auditable, and actionable, this work advances AIED toward transparent, theory-grounded, and educator-trustworthy design. 

6. Conclusion 

This study introduces a diagnostic framework that transforms opaque grammatical reasoning into observable, actionable signals for adaptive scaf-folding. By computing five interpretable strategy dimensions from action sequences, estimating coordination demand in real time, and mapping val-idations to four distinct reasoning states, the pipeline shifts diagnosis from binary success metrics to dynamic, configuration-aware personalization. Em-pirical analysis across 2,144 sessions and 42 morphosyntactic exercises demon-strates that strategy use is fundamentally state-dominant (mean ICC = 0.14), with students exhibiting high persistence in unproductive states (74.6% High-Demand Failure self-loop) and predominantly adaptive strategy transitions (45.9%). An early warning model forecasting validation success achieves robust discriminative performance (strategy-only AUC = 0.826; full + se-quential context AUC = 0.902) while explicitly excluding absolute current error counts to preserve non-circular interpretability. The framework’s strength lies in its commitment to pedagogical trans-parency: linear logistic regression, ablation-validated feature engineering, and calibrated probability thresholds ensure that every scaffolding trigger is auditable and educator-trustworthy. Computational efficiency ( < 12 ms 51 per validation on classroom hardware) guarantees seamless real-time deploy-ment without disrupting gameplay flow. Extending prior sequence-mining approaches to structured language tasks, this work demonstrates that latent reasoning dynamics can be reliably extracted from fine-grained telemetry, by-passing the computational bottlenecks of parser-based systems while preserv-ing diagnostic precision. Ultimately, by making reasoning visible, contextual, and interpretable, this framework advances the broader AIED vision of ar-tificial intelligence that augments rather than replaces human pedagogy. By providing a diagnostic engine capable of distinguishing how students reason from whether they succeed, it lays the groundwork for scaffolding systems that can respond to reasoning processes in real time.
