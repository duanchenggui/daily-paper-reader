## Abstract 

We evaluate whether semester-level interaction features from a large language model tutor improve assignment-score prediction beyond constant baselines. StudyChat provides 16,851 student– assistant exchanges from two offerings of an undergraduate ar-tificial intelligence course, with outcomes matched to 175 of 203 student-semester records. Six fitted estimators and training-fold mean and median baselines share repeated nested cross-validation. After averaging each student’s held-out predictions across ten re-peats, Gradient Boosting achieves mean absolute error of 3.023 grade points, versus 3.138 for the median baseline. The paired dif-ference is −0.114, with a conditional 95% bootstrap interval of 

[− 0.245 , 0.013 ]. Representation changes and a semester-specific median yield similarly small gains. In the lowest observed-score quartile, however, boosting’s error is 7.30 points, versus 8.07 for the median and 6.67 for the mean; Ridge has the lowest fitted-model error at 6.37 despite its worse overall error of 3.331. Scores con-centrate near the ceiling: 160 students (91.4%) score above 90, and the lowest quartile contributes 62.1% of boosting’s total absolute error. This retrospective comparison shows how baseline choice and error distribution affect predictive value, informing evaluation of student-support tools without establishing a learning effect.

## 1 Introduction 

Conversational tutoring logs record how students seek help, offer-ing a possible basis for feedback and instructor support. Before such traces inform educational decisions, their predictive value must be separated from the performance of a simple baseline. When scores concentrate near the ceiling, a constant prediction can achieve small average error while poorly representing lower-scoring students. Evaluating only overall accuracy can therefore obscure the errors most relevant to a support decision. 

> ∗Corresponding authors.

We examine this distinction in StudyChat [16], a research release with 16,851 exchanges from two offerings of one undergraduate artificial intelligence course. Semester-level features are available for 203 student-semester records, of which 175 have assignment outcomes. Among these matched records, 160 (91.4%) score above 90. The supervised sample therefore consists of 175 outcome-bearing records, not 16,851 independent learning outcomes. StudyChat’s original assessment-specific regressions add usage and dialogue-act counts to prior assessment scores [16]. Changes in explained variance in that setting do not establish held-out pre-diction of a semester aggregate or describe how prediction errors are distributed. We address that evaluation gap through two ques-tions: do semester-level interaction summaries reduce held-out assignment-score error relative to training-fold constants, and do baseline and estimator comparisons change across observed score levels? This is a complementary analysis of the same corpus, not a test of added value beyond prior academic performance. The main finding is a contrast between small aggregate gains and uneven error. Gradient Boosting’s student-averaged mean absolute error (MAE) is 3.023 points, versus 3.138 for a median baseline, with a conditional 95% interval for the difference crossing zero. In the lowest observed-score quartile, boosting outperforms the median but not the mean baseline; Ridge has the smallest fitted-model error there despite its worse overall rank. The baseline and evaluation population both affect the apparent predictive value. The analysis provides three connected contributions: 

• A repeated nested comparison of six fitted estimators and two constant baselines quantifies the incremental value of 14 interaction features. 

• All-model outcome-stratum contrasts identify where ag-gregate rankings change and separate learner performance from baseline choice. 

• Representation, cohort-transfer, and held-out attribution checks examine the scope of the predictive result and its interpretation. For learning analytics, these results establish evaluation require-ments before predictions guide support; no support intervention is tested. Full-semester features make this a retrospective study. Prospective evaluation requires verified assessment-relative cut-offs. Figure 1 distinguishes the supervised evaluation from the ex-ploratory descriptions of interaction patterns.

## 2 Related Work 

AI assistance and educational outcomes. Research on AI in edu-cation spans tutoring, feedback, assessment, and learner support. Reviews describe technical adoption alongside uneven educator involvement in evaluation [24]. Kasneci et al. discuss opportunities and risks of language-model assistance, including the need for peda-gogical oversight [12]. Bastani et al. compare tutor designs with and without learning safeguards in high-school mathematics [2]. Lau and Guo interview programming instructors about their planned responses to AI coding tools [13]. These studies distinguish educa-tional practice and learning effects from the prediction of observed assignment scores addressed here. 

Learning analytics and the evaluation gap. Learning analytics relates traces to educational processes and outcomes [8, 21], while educational data mining uses submissions, assessments, and activity records for prediction [19]. StudyChat supplies dialogues, generated dialogue-act labels, and normalized outcomes [16]. Its assessment-specific regressions add utterance or dialogue-act counts to a prior-score baseline and examine explained variance and coefficients. We instead compare held-out MAE for a semester-level aggregate on shared nested splits and examine errors across observed outcomes. The different target, information set, and error estimand distin-guish the two analyses; using the same corpus does not provide independent-cohort replication. 

Prediction and explanation. Nested cross-validation separates parameter search from outer evaluation [5, 23]. Repeated folds remain dependent, so we distinguish descriptive fold averages from fixed-fit student-bootstrap contrasts [7]. SHAP decomposes a fitted prediction into additive contributions [15]; numerical additivity alone does not establish accuracy or educational validity. These

## 4.5 Exploratory Interaction Profiles 

The companion clustering analysis uses eight standardized dialogue-act percentages for all 203 records, separately from supervised predictors. K-means [14] and Ward clustering describe request mixtures. For 𝑘 = 2, . . . , 6, silhouette [20] selects six; extending to twelve selects eight, while Davies–Bouldin [6] selects ten. All evaluated partitions above six contain singletons. Subset sensitivity samples 203 indices with replacement 200 times, discards repetitions, and refits on distinct rows with fixed scaling. All original rows are assigned to the nearest refitted cen-troid; adjusted Rand index (ARI) [11] compares assignments with the full-cohort reference. Reference/subset fits use 20 initializations, versus 100 in selection. This is not a multiplicity-preserving boot-strap. At the operational median-ARI cutoff 0.60, 𝑘 = 2, 9, 10 pass. ## 5 Educational Implications and Scope 5.1 From Prediction to Student Support 

The study supplies an empirical check before interaction traces are used to guide student support. The observed small gains provide little evidence for allocating feedback or instructor attention from this representation alone. Meanwhile, Q1 error concentration shows why population-average error is insufficient for evaluating a tool intended to assist lower-performing students. A support system should be assessed for its intended decision and population, not adopted solely because its average score error is small. For responsible use, educators should define the intended deci-sion, the information available when it is made, and the costs of missed support and unnecessary alerts before fitting a model. Those choices determine which baselines and evaluation populations are relevant. Our result concerns prediction beyond constants; prior academic performance remains an important untested compara-tor. It also does not imply that asking contextual questions harms learning. Such questions may express confusion, task requirements, productive help seeking, or annotation error. Interaction measurement is distinct from learning-process mea-surement. Counts and ratios discard dialogue order, task seman-tics, response correctness, and verified timing. The study does not directly measure motivation, emotion, or self-regulation. An educator-reviewed application could investigate these dimensions, but the present features should not be renamed as psychological constructs. Likewise, outcome-defined error heterogeneity is not a demographic fairness audit. Absence of protected-group evaluation precludes a claim of bias-free predictions.

A completion manifest reports Keras 3.15.1, whereas a separate environment snapshot records it as unavailable. Later versioned code documents search design, not exact historical implementation. These limitations are distinct from numerical agreement with the saved outputs. Replay checks all 400 model-fold target/semester orders; raw fold values are averaged before rounding. A separate source-frozen check retained the same cohort, splits, and search budgets. In 90 minutes, both constants, Ridge, Elastic Net, and Random Forest completed 50 folds each and matched point errors and paired intervals to four decimals. Extra Trees was interrupted; boosting and cohort MLP were not reached. This partial same-cohort check does not replace the historical comparison or provide independent validation. 

## 5.3 Future Work 

Future evaluation should first fix prediction times before assign-ment deadlines and restrict features to information available by each cutoff. Comparing interaction summaries with prior assess-ment and course-context predictors on held-out courses would test added value beyond existing academic records. Richer representa-tions could retain dialogue sequence, task content, and response

Educators and learners should then help define actionable sup-port and acceptable error costs. A controlled study of instructor-reviewed support could assess whether earlier feedback improves subsequent unaided performance, retention, or self-regulated learn-ing. Prespecified measures of missed support, unnecessary alerts, and instructor workload would distinguish predictive improve-ments from educational benefit. None of these intervention out-comes is established by the present retrospective evaluation. 

## 6 Conclusion 

Semester-level StudyChat features yield small overall assignment-score gains over training-fold medians, while baseline and estimator rankings vary across observed outcomes. Boosting’s overall gain is 0.114 points with a conditional interval crossing zero; its Q1 advantage over the median reverses against the mean. All-model reporting and explicit error populations are therefore necessary to interpret incremental predictive value in this cohort. For learning analytics, the contribution is a baseline-referenced assessment of this representation and the students who carry its error. It informs the design and evaluation of support tools without demonstrat-ing a learning effect. Prospective cross-course prediction and an educator-informed intervention study are the next tests of practical educational value.
