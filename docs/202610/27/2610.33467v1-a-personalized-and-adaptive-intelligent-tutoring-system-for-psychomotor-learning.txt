# A Personalized and Adaptive Intelligent Tutoring System for Psychomotor Learning 

Madeleine S. Yuh and Neera Jain 

Abstract —Intelligent tutoring systems (ITSs) have advanced learner motivation and personalization in cognitive domains, but extending these gains to psychomotor tasks remains challenging, particularly for maintaining motivation and tailoring instruction to individual learners. Existing psychomotor ITSs typically rely on learner models built before instruction or updated only once per module, leaving open the question of how an ITS should use ongoing, real-time assessments of learner cognitive state to drive finer-grained personalization. More specifically, during psychomotor learning, multiple tutoring objectives should be met, including calibrating the learner’s self-confidence to their performance, calibrating their workload according to the Yerkes-Dodson Law, and driving their skill progression towards higher learning stages. However, how to balance these tutor-ing objectives for individual learners, and how to adjust that balance as a learner progresses, remain open questions. This study proposes and evaluates an online personalization algorithm that continuously reprioritizes tutoring objectives to match the evolving needs of individual learners during training. Rather than treating tutoring objectives as equally weighted throughout instruction, the proposed actor-critic-inspired algorithm dynami-cally updates reward weights based on recent measures of learner self-confidence, workload, and performance. These personalized weights inform a multi-input, multi-output cognitive control framework that synthesizes learner-specific tutoring policies via model-based planning. The approach was evaluated in a human-subject study ( N = 40 ) using a 2D quadrotor landing task. Clustering analysis of the learned reward weights revealed distinct prioritization patterns across learners, indicating that the algorithm captures heterogeneity in learner needs and provides a principled mechanism for individualized instructional adaptation. Learners whose reward weights shifted more substantially over training tended to achieve higher final performance, and those whose weights consistently emphasized workload calibration achieved the strongest outcomes overall. These findings contribute new technical knowledge on online learner modeling and control-based personalization in psychomotor ITSs, while demonstrating how dynamically adaptive tutoring technologies can better sup-port learning by aligning instructional decisions with learners’ evolving cognitive and performance states.

ITSs have been successful in closing the feedback loop by considering learner behavior in cognitive learning domains such as language acquisition, computer programming, and mathematics [3]. In particular, many of these consider increasing learner motiva-tion by supporting self-efficacy, a learner’s self-perception of their competence and achievements [4], as increased learner self-efficacy has been consistently associated with improved learning performance and heightened learner motivation [4]. While ITSs have predominantly been used for cognitive learning domains, there have been several recent developments to advance ITSs designed for psychomotor tasks. A psychomo-tor task is defined as any motor action that is dependent on conscious mental processes [5]–[7]. Recent psychomotor ITS development has largely focused on structured knowl-edge representation and the design of adaptive, self-improving tutoring systems that enhance tutor–learner interaction. Selfit [8] enables users to self-report fatigue, motivation to train, sleep quality, and stress levels before each training session. During training, users may manually record exercise failures and report whether additional repetitions could have been performed, including the number of additional repetitions. After completing a session, users can evaluate the perceived difficulty of the training session. This information is then used by Selfit to update the training curricula. Another example is GIFT, the generalized intelligent framework for tutoring [6], which updates a model of the student using historical learner information, self-reports, performance data, and sensor-derived behavioral and physiological measures. In addition to this, the framework leverages a motivation assessment tool (MAT) over longitudinal training. Together, these measurements are utilized to inform GIFT on how to adapt via tutoring strategies according to the INSPIRE (Intelligent, Nurturant, Socratic, Progressive, Indirect, Reflective, Encouraging) model of tu-toring success [9]. In prior work [10], an alternative approach is proposed, in which learner engagement and motivation are decomposed into the underlying cognitive states of self-confidence and workload. Seminal learning literature indicates that self- confidence and workload directly influence self-efficacy. Additionally, these cognitive factors are widely recognized as key determinants of human–automation interaction quality [11]–[13]. Therefore, to model evolution during learning, a Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework is used to proba-bilistically capture the dynamics of self-confidence, workload, and learning stages characterized in by the authors in prior work [14]. The action space includes assistance provided during task execution and the tone of the formative feedback delivered after each task attempt. Despite these advancements, existing psychomotor ITS are not fully realizing the goal of personalization, particularly when it comes to adaptation to achieve cognitive objectives. Supporting learner motivation in this setting requires online assessment of learner self-efficacy along with targeted inter-ventions that can effectively influence the learner. Although researchers have recognized the need for intelligent tutoring systems (ITSs) to adapt to human cognitive behavior in cog-nitive learning domains [1], [3], [15], [16], an open question remains: how should an ITS use these ongoing assessments to improve personalization strategies in psychomotor domains? 

Currently, personalization requires an initial model of the individual student, like that provided by the aforementioned motivational assessment tool in GIFT [9] and the self-reports collected by Selfit [17]. Both are implemented before instruc-tion, or can also be implemented once per distinct module or task. However, the personalization of psychomotor ITSs could benefit from more frequent online updates to the model of student behavior , especially as psychomotor tasks increase in complexity. Furthermore, the tutoring policies employed by these psychomotor ITS should be updated according to the evolving needs of a given student by leveraging adaptive con-trol methods. This allows online updates to both the model and the policies tutoring agents use when helping the learner and would further enhance the tutoring strategies chosen by current ITSs. One example is adapting how and when information is presented by the ITS according to an individual’s learning style [18]. The contribution of this paper is an adaptive algorithm that updates the a psychomotor ITS tutoring policy via reprioriti-zation of tutoring objectives during the learning process for each learner. The actor-critic algorithm from reinforcement learning inspires the algorithm and considers both performance and cognitive states (self-confidence and mental workload) to determine how well the student and ITS interaction is meeting tutoring objectives. This is then used to solve for reward weights, in which the weights represent the prioritizations of each tutoring objective. The proposed algorithm leverages a learner model and multi-objective tutoring policy designed for psychomotor ITS described in the authors’ prior work [10], [19]. The main research questions we answer are: (RQ1) Does the algorithm give rise to distinct patterns in the tutoring objective weights across all learners? (RQ2) Do the reward weights dynamically change to adapt to the learner’s needs? 

(RQ3) Is the personalization algorithm successful in prioritiz-ing the tutoring objectives learners need? Our findings show that the personalization algorithm produces distinct patterns in the tutoring objective prioritization across learners. Through statistical analysis, the algorithm is shown to appropriately prioritize tutoring objectives according to the learners’ needs. The paper is organized in the following order. A. Psychomotor Task: 2D quadrotor landing simulator 

The psychomotor task considered in this work is a 2D quadrotor landing simulator. The learner’s objective is to safely guide the quadrotor onto a landing pad while satisfying constraints on its speed ( |v| ≤ 15 ms ) and roll angle ( |ϕ| ≤ 5◦), using the desktop simulator shown in Fig. 1a. The quadrotor’s state consists of its position ( x, y), velocity ( ˙x, ˙y), roll angle ϕ,and roll angular velocity ˙ϕ. Participants control the quadrotor through a T.Flight Hotas 4 controller, using the throttle to command thrust force, u1, and the joystick to command roll angular acceleration, u2. A landing is classified as safe if the quadrotor reaches the pad within the speed and roll angle limits and as unsafe if it reaches the pad but violates one or both of these conditions. If the quadrotor crashes before reaching the pad, the attempt is considered unsuccessful .During each trial, the participant either lands the quadrotor entirely on their own ( manual control mode, θof f ) or receives automation assistance ( shared control mode, θon ). In manual mode, the participant has full control over the quadrotor’s thrust force and roll angular acceleration inputs. Conversely, in shared control mode, the participant’s inputs are supplemented by inputs from automation consisting of a linear-quadratic regulator that tracks a reference trajectory derived from expert demonstrations, using a linear time-invariant model of the quadrotor dynamics together with a quadratic cost function that drives all quadrotor states toward zero [20]. After each landing attempt, learners receive negative, neu-tral, or positive formative feedback generated using GPT-4o [21], following the approach of Jensen et al. [22], who showed that AI-generated formative feedback can meaning-fully improve learning outcomes. The automation assistance and feedback tone are controllable inputs that the ITS can adjust before each trial of the task.

1) Discrete-valued Learning Stage Classification: The Dreyfus model of skill acquisition learning stages was first proposed by [23] as a means of characterizing learning pro-gression in psychomotor tasks by outlining five sequential stages of learning a skill and has evolved in the decades since. Prior work [14] developed a classifier for determining a participant’s learning stage after completing a trial of the quadrotor landing task based on trajectory data associated with task execution. Specifically, the first four stages of the five-stage learning theory model proposed by Dreyfus [23] are applied to the quadrotor landing task: novice (learning stage 1), advanced beginner (learning stage 2), intermediate (learning stage 3), and competent (learning stage 4). Features of the quadrotor dynamics as well as feasible landing types for each learning stage are summarized as follows for the reader’s benefit.  

> •

Learning stage 1 corresponds to the novice learner, who is still decomposing the task and developing an understanding of the overall goal. At this stage, the learner struggles to stabilize the quadrotor, and feasible landings are limited to unsuccessful or unsafe outcomes.  

> •

Learning stage 2 corresponds to the advanced beginner ,who begins developing strategies, identifying sub-tasks, and recognizing cues that indicate instability. The learner can now stabilize the quadrotor and complete landings, though the resulting trajectories remain inefficient and inconsistent. Feasible landings at this stage include un-successful, unsafe, and safe landings.  

> •

Learning stage 3 corresponds to the competent learner, who refines their strategy, prioritizes among sub-tasks, and shows increasing consistency, though still under a high attention load. The learner can control the quadro-tor and produces less variable trajectories, with feasible landings limited to unsafe and safe outcomes.  

> •

Learning stage 4 corresponds to the proficient learner, whose strategy has become fixed and continues to im-prove with practice, accompanied by a decreasing atten-tion load. The learner maintains control and lands the quadrotor consistently, producing efficient trajectories, with safe landings as the only feasible outcome. Each learning stage definition is quantified using a repre-sentative distribution of trajectories associated with that stage; example trajectories for each learning stage are shown in Fig. 1b. These trajectories capture the full quadrotor states of position ( x, y), velocity ( ˙x, ˙y), roll angle ϕ, and roll angular velocity ˙ϕ together with the thrust force input u1 and roll angular acceleration input u2. Each of these distributions is represented as a kernel embedding within a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, from which a learning stage classifier is built. For a detailed description of the development of the classifier, see [14].

C. Human Learner Model 

To design an optimal tutoring policy and adapt it online, we require a model of the human learner. Given that the tutoring objectives of interest rely on knowledge of the learner’s self-confidence, workload, and learning progression, we utilize a Markov decision process (MDP) model that captures stochastic transitions between these learner states. The model is based on a previously developed and validated framework [10] and is summarized here for completeness. PERSONALIZATION 

In order to personalize the psychomotor tutoring policy to individual learners, we need a tutoring policy structured in a way that enables adaptation, as well as the adaptation algorithm itself. In this section, we first introduce the design of a weighted reward function that captures the three tutoring objectives of interest. We then propose an algorithm for adapt-ing the weights in the reward function based on individual performance over consecutive trials. 

A. Reward Function Design and Policy Training 

We aim to achieve the following three tutoring objectives with a psychomotor ITS: (1) calibrating self-confidence to learning stage; (2) calibrating workload in accordance with the Yerkes-Dodson law [25]; and (3) rewarding advancement in learning stages from novice toward proficient. The first two objectives are motivated by the self-efficacy literature [26]– [29], which holds that greater self-efficacy makes learners more realistically self-aware about their progress and accom-plishments, in turn boosting performance and motivation [4], [29]. Maintaining appropriately calibrated self-confidence and workload levels are thus both components of self-efficacy. The third objective explicitly encourages progression through the learning stages. B. Reward Weight Adaptation Algorithm 

To update the reward weights online, one may initially turn to online reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms such as actor-critic [32]. However, in the case of the psychomotor ITS, the available dataset is not well-suited for online reinforcement JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 5

learning. First, the data is sparse and collected in short, fixed-length episodes (e.g., the most recent t trials), which limits the ability to perform long-horizon credit assignment or reliably estimate value functions from interaction data alone. Second, the data are generated under a restricted set of previously deployed policies, resulting in limited state– action coverage and insufficient exploration for stable policy improvement. Third, learner behavior is inherently variable and non-stationary, as performance, self-confidence, and work-load evolve, violating the stationarity assumptions commonly required for RL. Finally, the human-in-the-loop nature of the system makes aggressive exploration undesirable, as frequent or suboptimal policy updates may negatively impact learning outcomes and user experience. To address these challenges, we propose a data-driven reward re-weighting approach that leverages recent observa-tions to adapt the relative importance of tutoring objectives, followed by model-based Q-iteration to compute the corre-sponding policy. This framework can be interpreted as an actor–critic-inspired architecture, in which the reward weights 

α serve as high-level actor parameters that modulate the reward structure underlying policy optimization, while the objective functions (described in Sec. III-B1) computed from recent learner data provide a critic-like signal that evaluates the extent to which the tutoring objectives are satisfied. Unlike classical actor–critic methods, however, policy updates are not performed through online interaction; instead, the policy is recomputed via model-based planning under the learned reward function, resulting in a more stable and data-efficient approach. 

1) Objective Function Definition: To adapt the relative prioritization of tutoring objectives, we define three objective loss functions V1, V2, V3 that correspond directly to the reward components R1, R 2, R 3. We utilize self-reported self-confidence SR SC and self-reported workload SR W and scaled score J. Scaled score 

J is the total score S (Eqn. (1)) divided by 10, such that self-reported self-confidence, self-reported workload, and scaled score are all in the range [0 , 100] . SR W , and empirical mean of scaled score J, together with the scaled score standard deviation σJ .The first objective function aligns learner self-confidence with observed performance and is defined as the squared difference of SR SC and J,

IV. H UMAN USER STUDY 

With the proposed reward-weight adaptation algorithm and associated tutoring policy, we seek to answer the following research questions: (RQ1) Does the algorithm give rise to distinct patterns in the tutoring objective weights across all learners? (RQ2) Do the reward weights dynamically change to adapt to the learner’s needs? (RQ3) Is the personalization algorithm successful in prioritizing the tutoring objectives learners need? To do this, we design and conduct a user study in which participants learn to land the quadrotor in the 2D simulator while receiving tutoring assistance guided by Algorithm 1. 

A. Experiment Procedure 

Participants complete the psychomotor task described in Section II-A. A flowchart depicting the sequence of events in the experiment is shown in Fig. 2. In the first two trials, participants complete the task manually and without formative feedback, providing a measurement of baseline manual perfor-mance. The next 30 trials are divided equally into three phases, where the initial position of the quadrotor changes in each phase; the sequence of the changes in position is randomized to remove ordering effects. At the beginning of Phase 1, the reward weights are initialized uniformly for every participant, and the associated tutoring policy is synthesized. The participant then completes one practice trial in manual mode before the tutoring policy is turned on for the next 7 trials. During these 7 trials, the policy determines whether the automation assistance should be on or off ( aθ ) at each trial as well as what tone of feedback (aτ ) should be used when providing formative feedback to the participant. Recall that after each trial t, participants receive feedback on their previous landing attempt. This includes the time it took to land the quadrotor, the final speed and roll angle, and the achieved landing type. Additionally, the partici-pants receive their total score S out of 1000 points. Participants are then asked to self-report their confidence in achieving a safe landing with assistance together, as well as their perceived mental workload during the landing attempt. For the 9th and 10th trials of Phase 1, the participant again completes two trials in manual mode. At this stage, Algorithm 1 is executed to update the reward weights, after which a new tutoring policy is synthesized for each participant using the Phase 1 data. For this experiment, ζ = 85 in Eqn. (11). This value is chosen based on the experimental design in which a safe landing, with a trajectory that differs substantially from expert behavior, yields at most a score of 750 /1000 . Therefore, a threshold of 850 /1000 indicates that the learner’s trajectory is beginning to resemble that of a proficient operator. The scaling parameter κ of Eqn. (12) is set to 0.25 so that the least satisfied objective receives sufficient emphasis in the reward-weight vector. Parameter β in Eqn. (13) is set to 0.01 to allow the reward weights to adapt more rapidly to each learner’s needs. Phases 2 and 3 are conducted similarly to Phase 1, with the only change being the initial position of the quadrotor on the screen and the implemented policy being based on an updated reward-weight vector. After Phase 3 ends, the participant completes 5 trials in manual mode without formative feedback to provide a measure of their performance manually landing the quadrotor.

D. Data Collection 

For each participant, the collected trial-by-trial data in-cludes self-reported self-confidence, self-reported workload, and achieved total score. Changes in cluster assignment between consecutive phases are interpreted as shifts in the underlying tutoring strategy. The number of clusters k is determined using silhouette analysis [35], with the elbow method [36] used for secondary verification. The optimal k is selected based on the average silhou-ette score, with preference given to the smallest k that achieves near-maximum performance (within the 95% thresh-old of the maximum performance) to balance clustering quality and model simplicity. VII. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented and evaluated an algorithm for personalizing tutoring assistance to individual learners by dynamically updating the relative prioritization of tutoring objectives within a multi-input, multi-output cognitive con-trol framework for psychomotor intelligent tutoring systems (ITSs). The three tutoring objectives considered are aligning self-confidence with performance, calibrating workload to performance, and promoting progression through psychomotor learning stages. We enable the tutoring agent to adapt to changes in a learner’s cognitive states and learning stage via an actor–critic-inspired architecture in which the reward weights serve as high-level actor parameters that shape pol-icy optimization, while tutoring objective-specific functions computed from recent learner data provide a critic-like signal. Unlike classical actor–critic methods, the policy is not updated through online interaction but instead recomputed via model-based planning under the learned reward function. Moreover, the algorithm overcomes limitations posed by the data re-quirements of traditional actor-critic reinforcement learning by using gradient descent to optimize the reward weight parameters. To evaluate the proposed adaptive tutoring algorithm, we designed and conducted a human user study. We showed, through k-means clustering, that the personalization algorithm did result in distinct patterns in the tutoring objective weights across all learners. Using these clusters, we identified which learners’ reward-weight vectors changed sufficiently to move them to a different cluster after each phase of the study. Participants whose learned reward-weight vectors changed sufficiently to transition between clusters generally achieved higher final performance than participants whose reward-weight vectors remained within the same cluster, with the exception of the workload-calibration group. This was followed by a statistical analysis, which showed that the personalization algorithm successfully prioritized tu-toring objectives according to the observed needs of individual learners. A limitation of the results is the number of partici-pants; despite collecting data from a total of 40 participants, we could not control how many people were categorized into each cluster (and hence the groups defined based on these clusters). As a result, some groups contained relatively few participants, with the smallest groups containing only seven participants. Nevertheless, this work provides a computational framework and associated algorithms for achieving personalization during psychomotor learning and demonstrates, quantitatively, the benefits of this personalization. Possible directions for future work include a more extensive user study to further validate the efficacy of the proposed ITS, consideration of inter-dependencies between cognitive states and their influence on tutoring objectives, and personalization of the timescale on which reward weights are updated for individual learners. Consider R(sSC , s LS , a τ ) as an example: a learner who reports high confidence while still at learning stage 1, a case of severe overconfidence, receives a reward of 0.60 for negatively toned feedback versus only 0.20 for positively toned feedback, reflecting that corrective tone is more appropriate in this case. Similarly, leaving automation off yields a higher reward than turning it on, such that the learner is shown that they perform worse without assistance. with learning stage theory from [23], which states that task-related cognitive demand declines as expertise grows. This trend is consistent with the learning-stage characterization reported in [14]. Table VIII implements this logic. At learning stages 3 and 4, low workload paired with no assistance earns the maximum reward of 1.0, reflecting that proficient learners should be able to land the drone manually with minimal cog-nitive demand. Conversely, at learning stage 1, low workload also earns 1.0, since a novice attempting a manual landing should experience an elevated workload, and the reward is highest when the workload is appropriately challenging for their current level. When the learner’s workload is high, assistance is rewarded more than its absence, since the learner likely lacks the spare mental capacity to manage the task unaided. Only the assistance action enters R2, since assistance is provided during the trial, whereas feedback is delivered only after the attempt.

Objective 3: The reward R3(s, a ) incentivizes progression through the learning stages, as shown in Table IX. With automation off, reaching learning stage 3 or 4 earns the maximum reward of 1.0, with progressively lower rewards assigned for stages 1 and 2, encouraging the learner to advance toward expertise in manual mode. With automation on, the pattern inverts: learning stage 1 earns the maximum reward of 1.0, reflecting that a learner at this stage is most likely to benefit from assistance during the trial, while rewards decline across stages 2 through 4 as the need for assistance diminishes. As with Objective 2, only the assistance action enters R3, since it directly shapes the learner’s performance within each trial.
