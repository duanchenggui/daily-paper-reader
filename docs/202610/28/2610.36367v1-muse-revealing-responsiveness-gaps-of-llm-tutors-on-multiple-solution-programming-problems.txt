Abstract 

When a problem admits multiple valid solu-tions, effective tutoring requires building on a student’s strategy while introducing alterna-tives at appropriate points. However, whether LLM tutors can support this flexibility by ap-propriately responding to students’ diverse solu-tion approaches remains largely unexplored. In this work, we introduce MUSE: Multi-solution Understanding and Simulation Environment, a controlled environment for simulating student-tutor conversations on multiple-solution pro-gramming problems. Grounded in learning sci-ence on responsive instruction and multiple-solution learning, we investigate which solu-tions LLM tutors prefer, how they respond to students’ approaches, and how they transi-tion between solutions. Experiments suggest that LLM tutors exhibit systematic preferences among valid solutions, often favoring brute-force approaches when not providing general advice. More concerningly, tutors often impose these preferences, redirecting students toward their own preferred approach rather than sup-porting the student’s. Yet this tendency reverses once a solution is completed: tutors often re-main anchored to the existing approach when a stronger alternative could deepen understand-ing, and sometimes even transition to a less optimal approach. Thus, tutors struggle both to build on students’ reasoning when appropriate and to extend their reasoning when beneficial. Promisingly, targeted post-training improves both tutor responsiveness and transitions to-ward more optimal solutions. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as interactive tutors, leveraging their ability to generate explanations, ask questions, provide feedback, and engage learners in dialogue. Re-cent work has explored LLM-based tutoring across dimensions including pedagogical alignment, mis-conception awareness, and metacognitive support (Macina et al., 2023; Scarlatos et al., 2025; Wang, 2026). However, existing LLM tutoring systems and evaluations largely treat the target solution as fixed, or focus on a particular solution even when a problem admits multiple valid approaches. They therefore do not explicitly model the student’s cho-sen solution strategy as a central part of the tutoring interaction. This distinction is especially important for pro-gramming problems, where a single problem can admit multiple correct solutions with different tradeoffs in efficiency, simplicity, and generaliz-ability. A responsive tutor must do more than guide a student toward a correct answer: it must recognize the approach the student is pursuing, de-termine how to build on that reasoning, and decide when and how to transition to an alternative strat-egy (Çatman Aksoy et al., 2026; Dyer and Sherin, 2015). This raises a central question: Can LLM tu-tors effectively support students across multiple-solution strategies? 

In this work, we introduce MUSE (Multi-solution Understanding and Simulation Environ-ment), a controlled environment for simulating tu-toring interactions on multiple-solution program-ming problems. MUSE explicitly controls the so-lution pursued by the student while tracking the solution intended by the tutor at each time step, enabling systematic evaluation of how LLM tu-tors respond to different student approaches. Us-ing this environment, we investigate three research questions: (RQ1) which solution strategies LLM tutors prefer when multiple approaches are avail-able, (RQ2) whether tutors respond to and build upon the student’s proposed solution, and (RQ3) whether tutors appropriately transition between so-lution strategies after the student completes an ap-proach. When tutors provide solution-specific guidance, they exhibit systematic preferences, frequently favoring brute-force or oth-erwise naive approaches. Strikingly, tutors do not simply exhibit these preferences, but often impose them. Even when students pursue valid alternative strategies, tutors frequently redirect them towards the tutor’s pre-ferred approach instead (Figure 1). Our main contributions are threefold: 1. We introduce MUSE together with evaluation metrics for measuring solution preferences, responsiveness, and transitions between solu-tion strategies. 2. We systematically evaluate open-source LLM tutors on multiple-solution programming problems, revealing limitations in their respon-siveness to students’ proposed solutions and their transitions between solution strategies. 3. We demonstrate that targeted post-training can mitigate these limitations, leveraging MUSE to generate synthetic data at scale. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 LLMs for Tutoring 

Prior work has investigated LLM tutoring through pedagogical alignment (Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025; Scarlatos et al., 2025), misconception awareness (Macina et al., 2023; Otero et al., 2025), metacog-nitive development (Liu et al., 2026; Wang, 2026), and personalized instructional support (Reddig et al., 2025). Despite these advances, existing evaluations commonly focus on whether a tutor provides correct or pedagogically appropriate guid-ance toward a target solution, even when the un-derlying problem admits multiple valid approaches (Macina et al., 2023; Reddig et al., 2025; Scarlatos et al., 2025). As a result, relatively little attention has been given to whether a tutor recognizes and builds upon the particular solution pursued by a student, or how it should transition between alter-native solution strategies. Our MUSE allows for evaluating tutors’ solution preferences, responsive-ness, and transitions between solution strategies by explicitly controlling the student’s proposed solu-tion. 

2.2 Multiple-Solution Problems 

Educational research shows that engaging with multiple-solution strategies can promote learning, conceptual understanding, and procedural flexibil-ity (Große and Renkl, 2006; Rittle-Johnson and Star, 2007, 2009). Because multiple approaches can provide distinct learning opportunities, under-standing how tutors navigate this solution space is an important first step. We therefore examine which valid solutions LLM tutors prefer ( RQ1 ). However, simply presenting multiple approaches may overwhelm students (Lynch and Star, 2014), making it important for instructors to recognize and build on students’ existing reasoning (Çatman Ak-soy et al., 2026; Choppin, 2011; Dyer and Sherin, 2015). This motivates RQ2 : whether LLM tutors support students’ proposed solutions rather than redirecting them toward another approach. Multiple-solution instruction also creates oppor-tunities to introduce alternatives after students com-plete an initial solution. Because explanations are most effective when learners have reached an ap-propriate point for receiving them (Schwartz and Bransford, 1998), we investigate whether LLM tu-tors capitalize on these opportunities by transition-ing toward a more optimal solution, continuing with the existing approach, or redirecting students toward a less optimal solution ( RQ3 ). These findings suggest that effective tutoring across multiple solutions requires navigating stu-dents’ reasoning and solution strategies throughout the interaction. However, LLM tutoring research has not systematically studied these behaviors. We therefore study whether LLM tutors can effectively support students across multiple-solution strategies. A detailed discussion of related work on multiple-solution instruction is provided in Appendix A. 

3 MUSE: Multi-solution Understanding and Simulation Environment 

MUSE provides a controlled environment for sim-ulating LLM tutoring interactions on programming problems with multiple-solution approaches. It controls the simulated student’s pursued solution and intuition, as well as the tutor’s conversation initialization, enabling systematic evaluation of tu-tor behavior across different learning trajectories at scale. Using these controls, we define a response taxonomy and evaluation metrics for tutor’s so-lution preference, responsiveness, and transitions between solution strategies (Figure 2).

3.4 Conversation Initialization 

When simulating conversations, the LLM tutor will often open up the conversation by guiding the stu-dent towards a specific solution or by offering gen-eral advice. Alternatively, MUSE also allows for the LLM tutor to take a probe-first approach and open with a leading question (Cole et al., 1978). More specifically, after the student asks for help with a problem, the tutor may respond with a ques-tion such as: “What have you tried so far?"

5 Experiments and Results 

5.1 Dataset and Experimental Setup 

We collect 150 programming problems from the NeetCode 150 collection (NeetCode, 2026), span-ning diverse topics and three difficulty levels: easy, medium, and hard. For each problem, we collect its title, description, and three valid solutions. Each so-lution includes an explanation, algorithm steps, and time complexity, and is categorized as brute-force ,

suboptimal , and optimal based on time complexity. For each solution, we use GPT-OSS 120B (Agar-wal et al., 2025) to generate a correct intuition and a plausible but incorrect intuition that a student pur-suing the solution may have (Appendix E.2, E.3). Using MUSE, we simulate one conversation per problem and conversation type with four LLMs as tutors: GPT-OSS 120B, Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026), Llama 3.3 70B Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Gemma 4 12B IT (Team, 2026), yielding 150 conversations per type. GPT-OSS 120B serves as the LLM judge, classifying tutor responses according to the response taxonomy de-scribed in § 3.2 (Appendix E.1). For each LLM tutor, MUSE generates conversations under four conditions: probe-first or direct opening, crossed with a correct ( I2 = 1 ) or false ( I2 = 0 ) student intuition. When presenting an approach, the simu-lated student draws on the corresponding generated intuition. For SFT experiments, we randomly split the problems into 80% training and 20% test sets, using the training split for synthetic dataset generation and post-training and reserving the held-out test split for evaluation. To assess the robustness of SFT results, we repeat the training and evaluation process across three random problem splits gener-ated using different seeds and report the mean of each metric (Appendix C, F).

For fine-tuned models, we evaluate only the probe-first setting, since they are trained on this opener. Table 2 shows that probe-first openings generally increase SSR, with effects varying substantially across models. GPT-OSS, Qwen, and Gemma ex-hibit higher SSR under probe-first openings for correct student intuitions, with improvements of

Post-Training Improvements. We find that tar-geted SFT improves solution responsiveness. Com-pared with base Gemma, Gemma SFT1, trained and evaluated without explicit solution informa-tion, increases SSR from 0.507 to 0.606 for correct intuitions and from 0.419 to 0.596 for false intu-itions, corresponding to improvements of 9.9 and

This suggests that candidate solutions alone are insufficient to improve tutor responsiveness, as tutors must also learn to adapt their guidance based on the student’s reasoning. 5.4 RQ3: Post-Solution Transitions 

We examine whether LLM tutors take advantage of the opportunity to introduce a more optimal solu-tion after students complete their approach. We report the Post-solution Transition Score (PTS) (Equation 11) for each conversation type across all four models. For fine-tuned models, we evaluate only the probe-first setting, since they are trained on this opener. Additionally, we present the transi-tion distributions for correct intuition, probe-first approach (Figure 4) and other conversation types (Appendix Figures 5, 6, 7). Table 3 reveals substantial differences in LLM tutors’ tendencies to transition toward more opti-mal solutions. All base models exhibit positive PTS across conditions, indicating a general tendency to-ward more efficient solutions, but the magnitude varies considerably. Llama shows the strongest preference for post-solution optimization, while Qwen remains more anchored to the student’s solu-tion. Despite Qwen’s relatively high SSR in RQ2, its low PTS in RQ3 shows that supporting a stu-dent’s approach does not necessarily lead to intro-ducing a more optimal alternative after the student finishes their approach. More concerningly, tutors can steer students toward less optimal solutions around 6.1% to 7.1% of the time, potentially un-dermining their existing progress by encouraging less efficient approaches.

0.343 to 0.570 for correct intuitions and from 

0.349 to 0.625 for false intuitions. Combined with the SSR improvements in RQ2, these results sug-gest that SFT can improve tutor responsiveness while subsequently introducing more optimal ap-proaches. 0.936 for correct intuitions and from 0.625 to 0.903 

for false intuitions, while eliminating transitions toward less optimal solutions for correct intuition conversations (Figure 4). Because the SFT data already contains examples of optimal transitions, these results suggest that inference-time solution information does not teach the transition behavior itself, but helps tutors execute it by identifying available alternatives. Thus, SFT teaches how to transition toward a more optimal approach, while explicit solution information helps determine which 

transition to make. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduce MUSE, an environment for simulat-ing tutoring interactions for multiple-solution pro-gramming problems and systematically evaluating LLM tutors’ solution preferences, responsiveness to students’ proposed solutions, and transitions be-tween solution strategies. Our experiments reveal that LLM tutors often fail to align their guidance with students’ proposed solutions and may steer stu-dents toward less optimal approaches when given the opportunity to introduce more efficient alterna-tives. Supervised fine-tuning on interactions that explicitly demonstrate responsive tutoring moder-ately improves these behaviors, while providing candidate solutions during training and inference further enhances tutors’ ability to transition toward more optimal solutions. Overall, MUSE takes a step toward developing LLM tutors that more effec-tively support students and provides a foundation for studying personalized tutoring, tutor training, and adaptive pedagogical strategies in multiple-solution settings. Limitations 

Our findings should be interpreted in light of two limitations. First, our SFT experiments explore a limited range of fine-tuning configurations. Future work could investigate alternative approaches, such as fine-tuning models to leverage explicit reason-ing or thinking tokens, further improving tutor re-sponsiveness. Second, our evaluation dataset com-prises curated interview-style programming prob-lems, which may not fully capture the diversity of multiple-solution problems. Future work could ex-tend MUSE to broader STEM domains, including mathematical proofs and physics problems.

Appendix A Multiple-Solution Problems Learning Science 

Educational research has long examined the impor-tance of multiple-solution problems in supporting student learning. Asking students to consider or compare multiple solution strategies can promote learning (Große and Renkl, 2006) and has been associated with gains in conceptual understanding and procedural flexibility (Rittle-Johnson and Star, 2007, 2009). Engaging with alternative strategies can require students to identify relationships be-tween approaches and understand why different methods succeed, providing opportunities to de-velop a more flexible understanding of the underly-ing concepts. However, incorporating multiple-solution strate-gies into instruction also presents challenges. Teachers may avoid discussing multiple approaches because doing so can be perceived as time-consuming and may require substantial, multidi-mensional pedagogical content knowledge (Stupel and Ben-Chaim, 2017). Moreover, simply present-ing students with multiple strategies does not neces-sarily produce the desired learning benefits. With-out appropriate instructional guidance, students may become overwhelmed or fail to understand the relationships between alternative approaches (Lynch and Star, 2014). Thus, the pedagogical value of multiple-solution instruction depends not only on exposing students to alternatives, but also on appropriately connecting and navigating be-tween them. This challenge relates closely to research on re-sponsive teaching, which emphasizes building in-struction around students’ existing reasoning rather than immediately redirecting them toward a pre-determined solution (Çatman Aksoy et al., 2026; Choppin, 2011; Dyer and Sherin, 2015). Such re-sponsiveness is particularly relevant when students pursue different valid approaches: an instructor must recognize the reasoning behind a student’s strategy, determine how to productively extend that reasoning, and identify opportunities to introduce alternative approaches. Related work also suggests that the timing of explanations can affect their in-structional value, motivating the importance of in-troducing alternative strategies only when students are prepared to benefit from them (Schwartz and Bransford, 1998). Although this literature establishes the potential benefits and instructional challenges of multiple-solution learning, it has largely examined human instruction rather than LLM-based tutors. Con-versely, existing LLM tutoring work has not sys-tematically evaluated how tutors behave when stu-dents pursue different valid solution strategies. We bring these two lines of research together by treat-ing solution strategy as an explicit component of the tutoring interaction and evaluating whether LLM tutors appropriately support, extend, and tran-sition between students’ proposed approaches.

F SFT Details 

We fine-tune Gemma-4-12B-IT using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). We load the model and its associated pro-cessor using the Hugging Face Transformers li-brary (von Werra et al., 2020) and apply LoRA to the query, key, value, and output projection mod-ules ( q_proj , k_proj , v_proj , and o_proj ) in the attention layers. The LoRA configuration uses a rank of 32, an α value of 64, and a dropout rate of 0.1, with no additional bias parameters trained. The base model weights are loaded in bfloat16 pre-cision, and the model is automatically distributed across available devices. We train for 3 epochs using a per-device batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation over 16 steps, yielding an effective batch size of 16 examples per device before accounting for distributed training. We use a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, a cosine learn-ing rate schedule, and 10 warmup steps. Training uses bfloat16 and TF32 computation, with gradi-ent checkpointing enabled to reduce memory con-sumption. We disable sequence packing and use a random seed of 1. For a fair comparison, both SFT models (standard SFT and solution-informed SFT) are trained using identical hyperparameters and seeds.

We use GPT-OSS-120B as the LLM judge across all configurations. Each tu-tor and judge instance runs on a separate NVIDIA H100 GPU, with tensor parallelism set to 1. The exact compute cost depends on the tutor model. Generating the synthetic dataset for SFT requires 3 GPU-hours on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. Fine-tuning each SFT model requires 2 GPU-hours, resulting in a total of 4 GPU-hours for both models. Evaluating the fine-tuned models requires 1–2 hours of wall-clock time on two NVIDIA H100 GPUs, corresponding to a total of 2–4 GPU-hours, with one GPU allocated to the tutor and the other to the judge. The solution-informed SFT model requires more compute time due to its longer input context.
