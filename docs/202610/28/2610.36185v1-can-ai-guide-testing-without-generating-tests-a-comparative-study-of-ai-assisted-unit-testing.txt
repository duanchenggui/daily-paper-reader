# Can AI Guide Testing Without Generating Tests? A Comparative Study of AI-Assisted Unit Testing 

Zihan Fang, Ron Asoulin-Handelman, Helen Wu, Yu Huang, Gina R. Bai 

Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA 

{zihan.fang, ron.asoulin-handelman, helen.wu, yu.huang, rui.bai }@vanderbilt.edu 

Abstract —Recent advances in generative AI have enabled assistants to generate increasingly sophisticated executable unit tests. However, novices may accept these outputs without indepen-dently identifying what to test, evaluating test-suite completeness, or verifying correctness, potentially limiting opportunities to develop essential testing skills. Although prior work has explored AI-assisted test generation and feedback-oriented programming tutors, little is known about whether AI can support novices’ testing practices without generating tests for them. To investigate this, we introduce TestCompass, a generative AI tutor that provides targeted feedback on weaknesses in students’ test suites without generating executable tests. In a classroom study with 18 students, we compared TestCompass with a general AI assistant across two Java unit-testing tasks, examining students’ testing behaviors, test-suite quality, and subjective experiences. We found that participants using the general-purpose AI assistant produced more tests, whereas TestCompass participants spent more time developing and verifying tests themselves. However, most test-suite quality measures were comparable between conditions, and TestCompass participants achieved similar requirement coverage with significantly fewer test methods ( p = 0 .012 ) in one task. Neither group reported short-term improvements in perceived testing knowledge or confidence. These findings suggest that feedback-oriented AI can support meaningful testing outcomes while preserving opportunities for hands-on testing practice, offering a complementary approach to AI-assisted testing ed-ucation. 

Index Terms —Software Testing, Computer Science Education, AI-assisted Programming, AI-assisted Testing 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Generative AI is reshaping both programming education and real-world development workflows, changing how pro-grammers write code and the skills they need [1], [2]. As AI-generated code becomes increasingly common, students must also learn to critically evaluate and verify code they did not write themselves [3], [4]. Software testing plays a central role by providing a systematic way to determine whether generated code behaves as intended [5], rather than accepting AI-generated solutions without adequately verifying their correctness [6]. However, students can now also rely on generative AI to produce the tests used for verification, creating a new challenge: learners must evaluate not only AI-generated code but also the quality of AI-generated tests [7]. This is particularly concerning because software testing often receives limited instructional emphasis in computing curric-ula [8], and novices already struggle to decide what to test, determine whether a test suite is sufficient, and evaluate the correctness of their tests [9]–[11]. Prior work has explored how general-purpose AI assis-tants, such as ChatGPT, can support students in software testing [12]–[14]. These tools can explain testing concepts, suggest test cases, and generate executable tests, making testing more accessible [15]. However, prior studies have raised concerns about incorrect or incomplete AI-generated tests, insufficient verification, and students’ reliance on AI-generated solutions [12], [13], [16]. By providing ready-to-use tests alongside explanations, general-purpose AI assistants may encourage students to adopt generated solutions without critically evaluating their correctness or reasoning through testing decisions themselves [13]. This motivates exploring AI as a tutor that guides students’ testing decisions rather than providing ready-to-use solutions. An important question is whether such an approach can provide meaningful testing support while keeping students actively engaged in designing and verifying their own test suites. Prior work has explored feedback-oriented AI tutors for programming that provide hints and explanations without revealing solutions [17], [18], as well as conceptual feed-back and structured scaffolding for software testing [16], [19], [20]. Other studies have compared general-purpose AI assistants with testing checklists or pedagogically designed tutors [14], [21]. However, evidence remains limited on how testing-specific, feedback-only generative AI compares with general-purpose AI assistants that generate executable tests, particularly in terms of novices’ testing behaviors and test-suite quality. Understanding this distinction can inform the design of AI tools that support effective testing while keeping students actively engaged in test design and verification. We investigate this gap in the context of unit testing, a foundational practice in programming and software engineer-ing education [22], [23] that requires students to understand expected behavior, identify edge cases, and assess whether their tests adequately cover intended functionality [24]. We introduce TestCompass, a testing-specific generative AI tutor that provides targeted feedback on weaknesses in students’ test suites without generating tests for them. In a classroom study with 18 students, we compare TestCompass with a general AI across two Java unit-testing tasks, examining students’ testing behaviors, test-suite quality, and experiences with the tools. General AI participants spent more time interacting with AI assistants, whereas TestCompass participants devoted more time to developing and verifying tests themselves based on AI-generated feedback. Most test-suite quality measures were arXiv:2610.36185v1 [cs.SE] 26 Oct 2026 comparable between conditions, while TestCompass partici-pants achieved similar requirement coverage with significantly fewer test methods. We make the following contributions:  

> •

TestCompass, a feedback-oriented generative AI tutor that guides novice unit testing without generating executable tests.  

> •

An empirical comparison of feedback-only and general-purpose AI assistants in terms of students’ testing behav-iors, test-suite quality, and perceptions.  

> •

Design insights for future AI tutors in software testing education. Overall, TestCompass demonstrates the potential of AI to support effective testing without taking test development away from students, while highlighting the need for future AI tutors to provide greater guidance on test implementation and verification. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. LLM-Based Support for Software Testing 

Large language models (LLMs) have increasingly been used to automate software testing, particularly the generation of unit tests. Prior studies have evaluated the effectiveness of different LLMs and compared them with traditional automated testing techniques, including search-based software testing and symbolic execution [25]–[27]. These studies demonstrate the potential of LLMs for generating executable tests while identifying limitations in coverage and correctness. To improve test generation, researchers have proposed frameworks that combine LLMs with additional program context, iterative refinement, and automated validation [28]– [31]. For example, CodaMOSA [29] combines LLM-generated tests with search-based testing, while ChatUniTest [28] uses contextual information and iterative validation to improve gen-erated tests. Yuan et al. [7] systematically evaluated ChatGPT-generated unit tests and identified correctness issues, including compilation errors and incorrect assertions. Their findings highlight the importance of validating AI-generated tests rather than relying solely on their apparent plausibility. While these approaches primarily aim to improve the quality of automat-ically generated tests, our work examines a different role for generative AI: providing targeted feedback that helps students develop and verify their own test suites. 

B. Learning and Teaching Software Testing 

Learning to write effective unit tests remains challenging for novice programmers. Prior research has identified difficulties in selecting appropriate test cases, understanding coverage criteria, and determining whether tests adequately capture program requirements [32]–[34]. These challenges highlight the need for instructional support that helps students reason about test design and evaluate the quality of their test suites. Researchers have explored different forms of feedback to support students in developing better tests. Cordova et al. [19] compared inquiry-based conceptual feedback with tradi-tional detailed coverage feedback. They found that conceptual feedback was associated with higher coverage and fewer redundant tests. More recently, Perretta et al. [20] developed an automated feedback system that provides instructor-written hints about mutants not detected by students’ tests. Their classroom evaluation found a small but statistically significant improvement in students’ ability to detect mutants. These studies demonstrate the potential of targeted feedback to guide test improvement without providing complete test implemen-tations. With the growing availability of generative AI, recent stud-ies have also investigated how students use general-purpose AI assistants for software testing. Jalil et al. [12] examined Chat-GPT’s ability to answer software testing questions, identifying both opportunities and limitations for testing education. Fang et al. [14] compared ChatGPT, testing checklists, and their combination, finding comparable testing performance across conditions while raising concerns about students’ limited ver-ification of AI-generated responses. Ardic et al. [13] identified different strategies through which students collaborate with generative AI in test ideation and implementation, highlighting concerns about trust, test quality, and ownership. These studies demonstrate the importance of both feedback and students’ engagement in testing. However, they provide limited evi-dence on how dynamically generated, testing-specific feedback compares with general-purpose AI assistance that can directly generate executable tests. 

C. Pedagogical AI Support in Programming Education 

Generative AI has increasingly been integrated into pro-gramming education to provide students with accessible, per-sonalized assistance. Prior studies have examined how novices interact with general-purpose AI coding assistants [35], while educational deployments such as the AI-powered tools intro-duced in CS50 [36] have explored course-specific assistance at scale. To address concerns about students’ reliance on AI-generated solutions, researchers have developed pedagogically designed AI tutors that emphasize guidance rather than direct code generation. CodeHelp [17] uses prompting strategies and guardrails to provide explanations and programming guidance without revealing complete solutions. Similarly, CodeAid [37] provides conceptual explanations, pseudocode, and sugges-tions for improving students’ code while restricting direct solutions. Iris [18] further demonstrates how context-aware AI tutors can provide assistance tailored to programming coursework. Recent research has also compared pedagogically designed AI tutors with general-purpose AI assistants. Bassner et al. [21] compared a context-aware AI tutor, ChatGPT, and no AI assistance. They found no significant differences in learning gains or task performance, but observed differences in students’ preferences and verification behaviors. Building on this work, we investigate how feedback-only generative AI compares with general-purpose AI assistance in unit testing, focusing on students’ testing behaviors, test-suite quality, and experiences. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. TestCompass Design 

TestCompass is an IntelliJ IDEA plugin designed to help students identify what to test without generating test code for them (Fig. 1). The tool combines coverage analysis with generative AI: coverage data identifies untested portions of the code, while the LLM uses this information to gener-ate targeted, actionable feedback. Following prior work on conceptual feedback in testing education [19], TestCompass aims to guide students’ testing decisions while keeping them actively involved in writing and verifying their own tests. After students run their tests with coverage analysis, Test-Compass ranks production methods by the number of un-covered lines, using lower line coverage to break ties. When students request feedback, the tool selects all ranked methods with uncovered lines. For these methods, TestCompass collects coverage information, relevant production code, and existing test files to provide the LLM with context about the program’s behavior and what students have already tested. The prompt also includes a structured testing checklist that instructs the LLM to identify meaningful untested behavior, focus each recommendation on a single behavior, specify an action and its expected outcome, and avoid duplicating existing tests. This checklist guides the feedback generation process but is not shown directly to students. Based on this context, TestCompass generates recommendations describing three el-ements: the behavior to test, the action needed to exercise that behavior, and the expected observable outcome. The prompt explicitly prohibits generating executable test code. Students then translate the recommendations into JUnit tests, implement and execute them, and rerun coverage analysis. The updated coverage results allow TestCompass to identify remaining testing gaps, supporting an iterative feedback process. Before presenting recommendations to students, TestCom-pass applies a multi-stage validation process. An initial LLM call generates the recommendations, followed by a second LLM pass that reviews them against the production code, existing tests, and coverage context. The reviewed recommen-dations then undergo deterministic checks for the required feedback structure and, for the study tasks, task-specific properties. Recommendations that fail these checks trigger a correction pass before being displayed. This process aims to reduce incorrect or internally inconsistent feedback and improve the reliability of the generated recommendations. 

B. TestCompass Feedback Evaluation 

To assess TestCompass’s feedback, we developed a rubric informed by prior work on agent feedback evaluation [38], conceptual feedback in testing education [19], and devel-oper expectations of AI coding assistants [39]. It covers five dimensions: (1) Correctness , whether recommendations accurately describe program behavior and expected outcomes; (2) Relevance , whether they address meaningful testing gaps without duplicating existing tests; (3) Actionability , whether they give enough guidance to construct appropriate tests; (4) 

Clarity , whether they are unambiguous for novices; and (5) 

Pedagogical Usefulness , whether they encourage independent testing decisions rather than provide ready-made solutions. We applied the rubric through expert review and three pilot sessions. Each dimension was rated on a five-point scale. Two researchers independently checked the recommendations against the task specifications, production code, existing tests, and coverage data, resolving disagreements through discus-sion. Three pilot sessions with participants experienced in software testing and computer science teaching followed. TestCompass averaged above 4 on every dimension across both evaluations.

For both tasks, participants received a complete implementation, behavioral specifications, and minimal starter JUnit tests. They were instructed to modify only the designated test files and test the provided code as thoroughly as possible. D. Participant Recruitment and Group Assignment 

We initially recruited participants from a sophomore-level computer science course at our institution and later expanded recruitment to a senior-level course due to limited participa-tion. Participation was voluntary, with four extra-credit points awarded toward a course assignment upon study completion. Eligible participants were required to be at least 18 years old, enrolled in one of the courses, have prior Java programming experience, and have received introductory instruction in unit testing and JUnit. The study was conducted outside regular class activities, and non-participation carried no penalty. A total of 18 students participated and were randomly assigned equally to TestCompass (TC) or general-purpose AI (AI). However, one participant assigned to TC used only general-purpose AI and was analyzed according to the tool used, resulting in eight TC and ten AI participants. No statistically significant differences were observed between the analysis groups in education, gender, programming experience, or Java experience. All participants reported less than one year of unit-testing experience. Most self-identified as novices, while two identified as advanced beginners. E. Study Protocol 

Our study was reviewed and approved by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB). Before beginning, partic-ipants provided informed consent and completed a pre-study survey through Google Forms. They then received a condition-specific GitHub repository containing the project template, requirements specification, and instructions for using their as-signed AI tool. Participants in the TC condition were instructed to use only TestCompass for AI assistance, whereas those in the AI condition could use a general AI assistant (e.g., Chat-GPT) of their choice. Participants could install TestCompass directly from the JetBrains Marketplace or manually using a local plugin file. We recorded the specific assistant used by each participant in the AI condition. Participants completed both tasks in IntelliJ IDEA, where they inspected the provided source code and starter tests and developed JUnit tests based on the requirements. They could iteratively execute tests, examine failures and coverage results, revise their test suites, and consult permitted online resources. Screen recording was required throughout both tasks to capture testing activities and interactions with AI tools. After each task, participants submitted their completed project files and screen recordings. Upon completing both tasks, they completed a post-study survey. The study procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2. Each study session lasted approximately 1.5 hours. 

F. Data Analysis 

We analyzed participants’ screen recordings, submitted test suites, and survey responses to examine how they interacted with different forms of AI support, their testing performance, and their perceptions of the tools. The dataset included 18 participants (eight TC and ten AI), 36 test suites containing 798 identified test methods, 36 screen recordings, and matched pre- and post-study survey responses from all participants. 

1) Interaction Analysis: We analyzed 22.7 hours of screen recordings, dividing them into event-based segments repre-senting observable activities. Idle, off-task, and unclassifiable intervals were excluded. Activities included reading task in-structions and requirements, inspecting source code, writing or revising tests, running or inspecting JUnit results, con-sulting TestCompass or general-purpose AI, using reference materials, performing web searches, and setup-related activi-ties (Table II). One researcher annotated all recordings with assistance from GPT-6 to extract on-screen text, timestamps, and supporting evidence. The researcher manually determined segment boundaries and activity labels, verifying the AI-assisted information against the original recordings. A second researcher independently reviewed annotations for half of the participants against the recordings. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. For each participant and task, we calculated the frequency and cumulative duration of each activity. To account for differences in task duration, we calculated each activity’s proportion of classified task time at the participant level and then averaged these proportions within each condition. 

2) Test-Suite Quality Analysis: Following prior work on unit-testing evaluation [14], we evaluated each submitted test suite across four dimensions: completeness , effectiveness ,

We examined how participants engaged with different forms of AI support by analyzing their testing behaviors, test-suite quality, and self-reported experiences. 

A. RQ1: How Do Students Interact with Different AI Tools for Unit Testing? a) Distribution of Observed Activities: Participants in the two conditions showed distinct patterns of time allocation between hands-on test development and AI tool interaction. Across both tasks, TC participants devoted a greater pro-portion of their time to test-related activities. In MarsRover, test-related work accounted for 72.6% of their time, compared with 31.8% in the AI condition. A similar pattern emerged in BowlingScoreKeeper (59.1% vs. 22.9%), where TC par-ticipants also spent significantly more time working on tests (27.1 vs. 8.9 minutes; p = 0 .025 ). In contrast, AI participants spent substantially more time consulting their assistants. Tool consultation accounted for 41.2% and 52.6% of their time in MarsRover and BowlingScoreKeeper, respectively, compared with 9.2% and 8.2% in TC. The differences in consultation time were statistically significant for both MarsRover (13.5 vs. 2.3 minutes; p = 0 .015 ) and BowlingScoreKeeper (12.4 vs. 2.5 minutes; p = 0 .011 ). These findings highlight how different forms of AI support may shape students’ engagement in the testing process, par-ticularly the balance between practicing test development and relying on AI assistance. Table II summarizes the distribution of observed activities. 

b) Patterns of AI Assistance: Participants in the AI con-dition used various general-purpose AI assistants, including ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot Chat, and Gemini, for purposes beyond test generation. In MarsRover and Bowl-ingScoreKeeper, 60% and 80% of participants, respectively, used AI for multiple purposes, including understanding pro-gram behavior, planning and generating tests, resolving con-figuration issues, debugging test failures, and reviewing cov-erage. Notably, one participant used AI exclusively for test planning rather than generating complete tests. This partic-ipant requested a checklist of testing scenarios and a test-file skeleton with unfinished test cases, explicitly asking AI to leave the implementation for them to complete. Other participants primarily used AI to generate tests, with little separate interaction focused on explanation or troubleshooting. These contrasting behaviors suggest that, even without explicit instructional guidance, students adopted different approaches to AI assistance, particularly in deciding which aspects of testing to delegate to AI and which to perform themselves. In contrast, participants in the TC condition primarily used TestCompass to examine coverage information and review recommendations while developing or refining their tests, reflecting the tool’s more specialized functionality.

B. RQ2: How does objective test-suite quality differ between the conditions? 

We evaluated students’ test-suite quality across four di-mensions: completeness, effectiveness, maintainability, and implementation barriers, as summarized in Table III. 

a) Completeness: Across both tasks, participants using general-purpose AI achieved higher mean requirement and code coverage than those using TestCompass, although none of these differences remained statistically significant after multiple-comparison correction. For MarsRover, AI partic-ipants achieved higher mean requirement coverage (90.8% vs. 85.4%), line coverage (98.2% vs. 88.1%), and branch coverage (87.7% vs. 72.3%). Despite these differences, four participants in each condition covered all 12 requirements. A similar pattern emerged in BowlingScoreKeeper, where AI participants achieved higher mean requirement coverage (96.2% vs. 89.4%), line coverage (95.7% vs. 89.4%), and branch coverage (86.8% vs. 80.5%). However, six TestCom-pass participants and seven AI participants covered all 13 requirements. Robustness checks using JaCoCo confirmed that these coverage patterns were consistent across measurement tools and were not substantially influenced by the individual submissions examined. Notably, both conditions achieved similar rates of complete requirement coverage, despite TestCompass providing recom-mendations rather than generating executable tests. These find-ings suggest that while direct test generation may help novices achieve broader code coverage, it is not necessarily required to achieve complete requirement coverage. Recommendation-based support may be sufficient to guide novices toward relevant testing behaviors, even when they are responsible for implementing the tests themselves. 

b) Effectiveness: We assessed test effectiveness using mutation coverage, which measures a test suite’s ability to detect injected faults. General AI participants achieved higher mean mutation coverage than TestCompass participants on both MarsRover (84.9% vs. 79.2%) and BowlingScoreKeeper (91.5% vs. 80.8%), although neither difference was statisti-cally significant. For one TestCompass participant, two tests with compilation errors were commented out to enable mu-tation analysis. Assigning this participant a mutation score of zero did not change the statistical conclusions. Thus, although participants using TestCompass authored all tests themselves, guided only by its recommendations, we found no statistically detectable disadvantage in fault detection. 

c) Maintainability: We assessed test-suite maintainabil-ity through test-name quality and the number of maintainabil-ity issues. Across both tasks, AI participants achieved higher mean test-name quality ratings than TC participants, although neither difference was statistically significant. The number of maintainability issues showed mixed results. AI participants produced more issues in MarsRover (4.8 vs. 2.9), whereas TC participants produced more in BowlingScoreKeeper (6.3 vs. 5.2). However, because AI participants generally developed larger test suites, we normalized issue counts by the number of test methods. After normalization, AI suites exhibited fewer issues per test method in both MarsRover (0.19 vs. 0.36) and BowlingScoreKeeper (0.19 vs. 0.40), although only the latter difference was statistically significant ( p = 0.044 ). Regarding test redundancy, TC participants produced fewer redundant test methods on average in MarsRover (0.5 vs. 1.5), while both conditions showed similar levels of redundancy in BowlingScoreKeeper (0.6 vs. 0.5). Neither difference was statistically significant. Overall, general-purpose AI assistance was associated with higher test-name quality ratings and lower maintainability issue density, although most differences were not statistically significant. These findings suggest that feedback-oriented sup-port could be extended beyond identifying weaknesses in students’ test suites to helping them understand and apply principles of effective test design, including clear test naming and maintainable test structure. 

d) Implementation Barriers: We examined implementation-related challenges through test-suite size and the proportion of erroneous tests. Notably, although TestCompass participants achieved similar rates of complete requirement coverage, they did so with fewer test methods. In MarsRover, TC participants submitted an average of 12.0 test methods, compared with 28.3 for AI participants. A similar pattern emerged in BowlingScoreKeeper (17.9 vs. 27.6), where the difference was statistically significant ( p = 0 .012 ). We also found no statistically significant differences in test correctness. In MarsRover, the mean participant-level confirmed-error rate was 0.0% for TC participants and 0.3% for AI participants. In BowlingScoreKeeper, the corresponding rates were 11.0% and 2.6%, respectively. Overall, these findings suggest that TestCompass’s recommendation-based support can help novices achieve broad coverage with fewer, independently authored tests, potentially improving testing efficiency. Although general AI participants produced larger test suites, these additional tests did not yield statistically significant improvements in coverage or fault detection. This highlights the potential of feedback-oriented support to guide efficient test development while keeping novices actively engaged in designing and implementing their own tests. T HREATS TO VALIDITY 

A. Internal Validity 

Participants were randomly assigned to the TC or AI con-dition, and their self-reported prior programming and testing experience was comparable across conditions. To reduce vari-ation in educational background, participants were primarily recruited from the same course, with one exception from a senior-level course. Nevertheless, unmeasured individual differences in programming proficiency, testing skills, or fa-miliarity with AI tools may have influenced their performance. The general-purpose AI condition was not fully standard-ized, as participants were allowed to use different AI assis-tants due to IRB restrictions. Although initially designated as the ChatGPT condition, participants also used tools such as Claude, Gemini, and GitHub Copilot Chat. Differences in model capabilities, response quality, and interaction patterns may therefore have introduced variation. To account for this heterogeneity in our interpretation, we frame the study as a comparison between two forms of AI support, namely general-purpose AI assistants capable of generating executable tests and a feedback-oriented tutor that provides testing recommen-dations. Nevertheless, differences among general-purpose AI assistants remain a potential source of variability. Additionally, both tasks were completed in a fixed order, making it difficult to distinguish task-specific effects from potential practice, fatigue, or carryover effects. Future studies could counterbalance task order and incorporate breaks be-tween tasks to mitigate these concerns. Finally, screen record-ing may have influenced participants’ behavior by increasing performance pressure, although the same requirement applied to both conditions. To minimize this effect, we informed participants that they should do their best and that their performance would not affect their course grades. 

B. External Validity 

Most participants were recruited from a single sophomore-level computer science course at one institution, with prior exposure to Java, JUnit, and introductory unit testing. The relatively small sample size, partly due to recruitment chal-lenges, may further limit the generalizability of our findings to students from other institutions, with different educational backgrounds or experience levels, and to professional de-velopers. Nevertheless, the design of TestCompass primarily targets novice programmers learning unit testing. To strengthen our findings despite the limited sample, we examined test-ing behaviors and outcomes across two tasks using multiple complementary measures. We also employed statistical tests appropriate for small samples and reported effect sizes to support interpretation. Second, our study focused on two Java unit-testing tasks completed in IntelliJ IDEA. Other testing domains, programming languages, development environments, or testing frameworks may introduce different challenges and interaction patterns. Thus, the findings and the current implementation of TestCompass may not generalize directly to other testing contexts. Finally, the study lasted approximately one and a half hours, capturing short-term testing behaviors, performance, and perceptions rather than long-term learning, retention, or transfer of testing skills. Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine whether different forms of AI support influence students’ testing practices and skill develop-ment over time. 

C. Construct Validity 

We evaluated test-suite quality across four dimensions: completeness, effectiveness, maintainability, and implementa-tion barriers. Although these dimensions capture important aspects of testing performance, they do not fully represent the underlying constructs. Requirement, line, and branch cov-erage measure the extent of testing but do not necessarily reflect test correctness or semantic quality. Mutation cov-erage approximates fault-detection effectiveness but depends on the generated mutants and mutation operators. Excluding tests with compilation errors may affect measured mutation coverage, although assigning the affected suite a zero score did not change our statistical conclusions. Maintainability was assessed using static-analysis issues, test-name quality, and test redundancy, which may not fully capture long-term maintainability. Similarly, the number of submitted tests and confirmed errors provides only a partial view of implementa-tion challenges. Manual coding and qualitative analysis also involved re-searcher judgment. To reduce subjectivity, we applied con-sistent coding criteria and involved a second researcher in reviewing annotations and themes, although some interpretive bias may remain. Survey measures, including self-assessed knowledge, confidence, helpfulness, and willingness to rec-ommend the tool, may be subject to response bias and do not directly measure learning gains. Finally, interaction analysis relied on screen recordings and observable behaviors, which cannot fully capture participants’ underlying reasoning or decision-making processes. VI. D ISCUSSION 

A. Teaching Software Testing in the AI Era 

As generative AI increasingly produces both code and its tests, testing education must place greater emphasis on students’ ability to evaluate whether tests adequately cover intended behaviors and interpret what their results reveal. Our findings highlight the potential value of different forms of AI assistance. Participants using general-purpose AI produced substantially larger test suites and generally achieved higher mean coverage. However, comparable proportions of Test-Compass participants covered all task requirements despite receiving only recommendations rather than executable tests, and no coverage difference remained significant after multiple-comparison correction. Interaction patterns also differed: Test-Compass participants spent more of their time writing, re-vising, and inspecting tests, whereas general AI participants spent more time consulting their assistants. Although these differences do not establish better learning, they suggest that the form of AI support shapes how students engage with testing. These findings suggest that educators could select AI tools based on their instructional goals rather than relying on a single approach. General AI may be useful for exploring unfamiliar concepts and examples, while recommendation-based AI tutors may be better suited to hands-on exercises that require students to implement and evaluate tests them-selves. This approach may help students develop the skills to work effectively with AI tools while remaining actively engaged in the testing process rather than passively relying on AI-generated solutions. Regardless of the tool, educators should encourage students to justify their test choices, explain expected outcomes, and identify weaknesses in AI-generated tests. Such activities may help students develop independent testing judgment while benefiting from AI assistance. B. Designing AI Tutors That Bridge Guidance and Implemen-tation 

Our findings highlight opportunities for improving testing-specific AI support. TestCompass was designed to identify testing gaps without directly generating tests, allowing stu-dents to remain responsible for test implementation. How-ever, participants sometimes struggled to interpret lengthy recommendations, translate them into executable tests, and resolve execution problems. In contrast, general-purpose AI participants produced substantially more tests and rated their tools as more helpful. These findings highlight a challenge for recommendation-based support: knowing what to test does not necessarily mean knowing how to implement it. Rather than choosing between providing recommendations and generating complete tests, future AI tutors could offer dif-ferent levels of assistance based on students’ needs. Building on prior work on conceptual feedback and progressive testing hints [19], [20], a tutor could initially help students identify relevant requirements and reason about expected outcomes. When students encounter difficulties, it could offer more con-crete implementation guidance or help diagnose test failures, such as distinguishing incorrect assertions from implementa-tion defects. This progressive approach could bridge the gap between understanding testing recommendations and putting them into practice, while preserving opportunities for students to develop and verify tests themselves. 

C. Feedback Must Support Oracle and Domain Reasoning 

An important challenge emerging from our findings is that knowing what to test does not mean knowing the correct result. In both conditions, participants struggled to understand task specifications, determine expected outputs, and judge whether their tests were sufficient. This was especially true for BowlingScoreKeeper, where testing strikes, spares, and final-frame behavior requires understanding how rolls contribute to the final score. Although general-purpose AI provides both test code and explanations, students may use the generated tests without examining the reasoning behind their expected out-comes. This distinction matters because identifying a relevant test case does not guarantee a correct assertion. Students may recognize which behavior to test yet misunderstand the speci-fication or compute an incorrect expected result. Similarly, an AI-generated test may run successfully without checking the correct behavior. TestCompass addresses this by including val-idated expected outcomes in its recommendations. However, participants’ difficulties suggest that providing the expected result alone may not help novices understand why it is correct. Future AI testing tutors should therefore help students reason about expected outcomes rather than simply provide them. For example, feedback could explain which requirement a test addresses and how its expected result is derived. Given participants’ difficulty with lengthy recommendations, such explanations should be concise and revealed progressively as needed. This could help students understand the reasoning behind their own tests and critically evaluate AI-generated tests instead of accepting their expected outputs unverified. 

D. Perceived Helpfulness Does Not Necessarily Reflect Learn-ing Support 

Participants rated general-purpose AI as more helpful than TestCompass, yet this difference was not accompanied by a statistically significant advantage in requirement coverage. Neither condition showed consistent improvements in self-assessed testing knowledge or confidence. Willingness to recommend the tools also did not differ significantly, and some AI participants gave low recommendation ratings despite finding the tool helpful. One participant even expressed a desire to learn programming without relying on AI. These findings suggest that perceived helpfulness may reflect the convenience of completing an immediate task rather than the extent to which a tool supports learning. This distinction has implications for evaluating educational AI tools. While general-purpose AI can provide immediate solutions, recommendation-based tutors such as TestCompass require students to interpret guidance and implement tests themselves. However, our single-session study cannot de-termine whether this additional involvement leads to better learning outcomes. Future evaluations should therefore look beyond task performance and perceived helpfulness to exam-ine whether students can independently design tests, justify expected outcomes, and identify errors in AI-generated tests after assistance is removed. VII. C ONCLUSION 

As generative AI increasingly supports both code and test generation, testing education needs approaches that provide meaningful assistance while keeping students actively engaged in designing and verifying their own tests. In this study, we introduced TestCompass, a feedback-oriented AI tutor that provides targeted testing recommendations without generating executable tests, and compared it with general-purpose AI assistance in a study involving 18 novice programmers. We found that participants using general-purpose AI produced larger test suites, generally achieved higher coverage, and perceived their tools as more helpful. However, most dif-ferences in objective test-suite quality were not statistically significant, and both conditions achieved similar rates of com-plete requirement coverage. TestCompass participants spent more time developing tests and were more likely to execute and inspect their results. Neither condition showed consis-tent improvements in self-assessed knowledge or confidence. These findings suggest that feedback-oriented AI can support meaningful testing outcomes while preserving opportunities for students to develop and verify their own tests. However, participants’ difficulties in interpreting recommendations and determining expected outcomes highlight the need for addi-tional implementation guidance. Future work should explore adaptive AI tutors that integrate testing recommendations with more implementation and verification support, while examin-ing their effects on students’ independent testing skills over time. Ultimately, TestCompass demonstrates the potential of AI to support effective testing practice without taking test development away from students. REFERENCES [1] P. Denny, J. Leinonen, J. Prather, A.
