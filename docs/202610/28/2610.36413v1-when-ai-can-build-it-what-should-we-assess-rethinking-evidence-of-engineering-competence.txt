# When AI Can Build It, What Should We Assess? Rethinking Evidence of Engineering Competence 

Ali Shakiba 

School of Electrical and Computer Engineering The University of Sydney 

Sydney, Australia ali.shakiba@sydney.edu.au 

Abstract —Generative AI can produce code, tests, documen-tation, and technical explanations that resemble artefacts tra-ditionally used as evidence of student competence, weakening the inference from artefact quality to the engineering capability behind it. We report the redesign and first deployment of a 13-week, AI-permissive, software-intensive engineering course in which students may delegate substantial work to AI while retaining responsibility for planning, verification, review, inte-gration, and explanation. The redesign couples an engineering accountability workflow , which structures human-AI work and preserves human decision authority, with an assessment evidence architecture comprising six complementary channels: product, process/origin, verification, explanation, reflection, and individual assurance. A collect–cross-check–escalate (CCE) reasoning logic connects these channels rather than treating any single artefact or trace as sufficient evidence of competence. By the end of Week 11, the deployment involved 87 students organised into 17 teams, with 957 individual weekly Git repositories and 34 group-project Git repositories supporting repeated assessment and engineering activity. The first deployment yielded four practical lessons: AI assistance changes where rubric criteria carry evidential weight; multiple evidence channels require deliberate cross-checking and meaningful complementarity; the evidential strength realised in practice may differ from that intended in the assessment design; and automation scales evidence collection more readily than interpretation. The contribution is a conceptually grounded, deployed design for making engineering responsibility and com-petence more visible when AI can perform substantial artefact production. 

Index Terms —software engineering education, generative AI, assessment design, assessment validity, engineering judgement, human-AI collaboration 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Generative AI (GenAI) is challenging a long-standing as-sumption in engineering education: a submitted artefact pro-vides meaningful evidence of the competencies required to produce it. Modern GenAI systems can generate, modify, test, document, and even explain software artefacts. Consequently, a technically correct program, report, test suite, or design document may no longer provide sufficient evidence, on its own, that a student can reason about, verify, integrate, or defend the engineering work embodied in that artefact. Recent studies frame this challenge as an assessment-validity issue rather than just an academic-integrity concern [1]–[3]. Emerging evidence further suggests that AI assistance can improve task completion and the apparent quality of submissions without producing equivalent gains in conceptual understanding or independent capability [2], [4]. One response is to restrict AI use or attempt to detect AI-generated work. Detection-based approaches, however, have significant limitations, particularly in cases of hybrid human-AI authorship and short-form coursework [5], [6]. More fun-damentally, authorship and competence are distinct concerns. Even a reliable estimate that AI contributed to an artefact does not establish whether a student understood, verified, adapted, or could defend the engineering decisions embodied within it. The resulting assessment challenge is therefore not only how to determine whether AI was used, but how to construct credible evidence of engineering competence when AI assistance is permitted. This challenge is particularly consequential in software-intensive engineering education. AI-assisted development in-creasingly resembles a realistic condition of modern engineer-ing practice, making it important for students to learn how to work effectively with AI-supported tools. Yet, permitting AI in open-ended engineering work complicates the attribution of competence: educators still require credible evidence that students can make engineering decisions, recognise failures, test assumptions, integrate components, and take responsibility for the resulting system. The design challenge is therefore not simply whether students should be allowed to delegate engineering work to AI, but how to ensure that delegation does not transfer responsibility for the decisions and evidence on which engineering claims depend. This paper reports the redesign and first deployment of a 13-week postgraduate engineering course around this principle. Rather than treating AI use as an exception to be prevented or detected, the course permits substantial AI-assisted work while retaining human responsibility for planning, verification, review, integration, and explanation. The redesign combines an engineering accountability workflow with an assessment evidence architecture for AI-permissive engineering work. At the end of Week 11, the deployment involved 87 students or-ganised into 17 teams, with 957 individual weekly repositories and 34 group-assignment repositories created to support the course. The redesign builds on established principles of constructive alignment, authentic assessment, evaluative judgement, assess-ment validity, and the use of complementary evidence for arXiv:2610.36413v1 [cs.SE] 26 Oct 2026 complex competence; Section II develops these foundations and considers how GenAI changes their application. The redesign addresses three related design challenges:

In response to these challenges, this paper makes three design contributions. First, we present an engineering account-ability workflow , a six-stage cycle (Frame → Delegate →

Inspect → Verify → Review → Integrate) that structures AI delegation around explicit points of human decision authority. Second, we present an assessment evidence architecture (AEA) comprising six complementary channels: product, pro-cess/origin, verification, explanation, reflection, and individual assurance. Each channel supports different inferences about student competence and has known limitations, allowing evi-dence to be interpreted in combination rather than treating any single source as sufficient. Third, we describe a collect–cross-check–escalate (CCE) 

reasoning logic for interpreting evidence across these channels. Evidence is collected through normal engineering and assess-ment activity, cross-checked against complementary sources, and escalated for targeted follow-up when material inconsis-tencies or gaps remain. Beyond these design contributions, we report four lessons from the first deployment concerning the evidential value of assessment criteria, cross-channel assurance, realised evidence strength, and the human cost of interpretation. The rest of the paper is organised as follows: Section II reviews related work and the design foundations; Section III describes the course context and assessment redesign; Sec-tion IV presents the coupled engineering accountability work-flow, AEA, and CCE reasoning logic; Section V describes their deployment and operation; Section VI reports lessons from the first deployment; Section VII discusses design im-plications and transferability; Section VIII outlines the scope and limitations; and Section IX concludes the paper. II. R ELATED WORK AND DESIGN FOUNDATIONS 

Recent work conceptualises the decoupling of artefact qual-ity and individual capability as an assessment-validity problem arising from human-AI coauthorship. Ebrahimzadeh et al. argue that submitted work may no longer provide sufficient evidence that students understand the content they submit [1]. Empirical evidence from programming education reinforces this concern: Bassner et al. found that AI assistance sub-stantially improved programming-task performance without corresponding improvements in conceptual learning or code comprehension [2]. This problem also increases the importance of evaluative judgement : students’ capability to judge the quality of their own and others’ work. GenAI makes such judgement particu-larly important because students must evaluate not only final AI outputs but also the processes through which those outputs are produced [7], [8]. In software-intensive engineering, this judgement extends beyond identifying incorrect code. Students must evaluate whether generated artefacts satisfy requirements, fit system constraints, integrate with existing components, are adequately verified, and can be justified as engineering decisions. Permitting AI therefore shifts part of the educa-tional challenge from artefact production towards maintaining meaningful human judgement, verification, and responsibility around delegated work. The need to look beyond final products is not unique to GenAI. Constructive alignment requires assessment activities to elicit evidence corresponding to intended learning outcomes rather than relying on convenient proxies [9], while authentic assessment seeks alignment with the tasks, contexts, and criteria of professional practice [10]. Research on professional competence similarly argues that complex capability is better inferred from a programme of complementary observations than from a single assessment instrument [11]. Evidence-Centred Design (ECD) similarly conceptualises assessment as an evidentiary argument connecting observations from assess-ment tasks to claims about student competence [12]. GenAI makes these established principles more consequential because it weakens the evidential relationship between producing an artefact and possessing the capability represented by that artefact. Software engineering education already provides several mechanisms for making development activity and individ-ual contribution more visible. Version-control histories and project-management traces have been used to supplement conventional assessment of individual contribution in team software projects [13]. Automated approaches can additionally combine repository activity, software behaviour, testing, and DevOps practices to provide repeated feedback and assessment evidence [14]. However, such traces require careful interpre-tation: commit counts, repository activity, and automated test results reveal aspects of the development process but are not direct measures of understanding or engineering judgement. Process evidence is therefore most useful when interpreted alongside other evidence rather than treated as a replacement competence proxy. Recent GenAI-aware course designs similarly move towards assessment structures that permit AI while making human rea-soning and responsibility more visible. For example, Fein et al. describe an AI-driven software-development course in which assessment considers the resulting code, the development process, and student reflection [15]. Related GenAI-aware assessment approaches increasingly combine AI-permissive work with mechanisms such as process-focused assessment, reflection, oral explanation, staged work, and controlled indi-vidual assessment. These designs recognise that authentic AI-assisted engineering and independent assurance serve different evidential purposes: the former can reveal how students oper-ate in contemporary tool-rich environments, while the latter can provide stronger attribution of particular knowledge and reasoning to an individual. Contemporary responses to GenAI have introduced frame-works for both regulating AI participation and preserving assurance of individual learning. Task-level frameworks such as the AI Assessment Scale provide a structured means of specifying permissible levels of AI use, while two-lane ap-proaches combine AI-permissive assessment with controlled conditions intended to provide stronger assurance of individ-ual capability [16], [17]. However, permission and assurance structures do not by themselves specify how responsibility should be distributed within an AI-permissive engineering task, nor how evidence generated across such work should be interpreted together. Existing work therefore establishes many of the individual elements needed for assessment under GenAI, including au-thentic project work, process traces, automated verification, reflection, oral explanation, and individual assurance. The gap we address concerns their system-level orchestration when substantial engineering production is explicitly delegable to AI: preserving identifiable human decision authority while constructing and interpreting complementary evidence of com-petence. Section IV presents the resulting coupled design. III. C OURSE CONTEXT AND REDESIGN 

The redesign was deployed in a 13-week course, ELEC5308: Intelligent Information Engineering Practice, at the University of Sydney. The application domain is au-tonomous systems, but the course is software-intensive: stu-dents design modular Python systems, use version control and pull requests, build CI pipelines, test and profile software, manage experiments and data, document interfaces, and inte-grate perception, localisation, control, planning, and learning components. The learning outcomes emphasise analysing and evaluating methods and MLOps strategies, designing and eval-uating intelligent engineering frameworks, and synthesising advanced programming pipelines. The cohort comprised 87 students organised into 17 project teams. Open-ended assign-ments permit AI use subject to disclosure and accountability requirements, while the final written examination is supervised and AI use is not permitted. Assignments 1 and 2 are deliberately open-ended and ill-defined, with assessment rubrics published in advance. As-signment 1 required teams to develop a modular, simulator-agnostic autonomous perception and navigation library as an installable Python package. Assignment 2 required teams to extend, refactor, or deploy that existing baseline rather than restart from scratch. This continuity was a deliberate design choice intended to approximate brownfield software development, in which earlier engineering decisions remain consequential: a well-engineered baseline becomes an asset, whereas weaknesses in architecture, testing, documentation, or integration create downstream cost. Both assignments used a Git-based collaborative workflow involving personal forks, pull requests, peer review, reproducibility requirements, and continuity between project stages. The 2026 offering involved a substantial redesign of the teaching and assessment structure. Table I summarises doc-umented structural differences between the previous offering and the 2026 design. IV. T HE COUPLED DESIGN 

The redesign separates two related questions: How should students work with AI? and what evidence should educators use to assess the resulting engineering competence? The first is addressed by an engineering accountability workflow (Sec-tion IV-A), which structures delegation and preserves human responsibility at consequential decision points. The second is addressed by the AEA (Section IV-B), which specifies the complementary evidence used to support claims about student competence. The CCE reasoning logic (Section IV-C) con-nects these evidence channels and describes how incomplete, inconsistent, or mutually reinforcing evidence is interpreted. Figure 1 summarises the workflow, evidence architecture, and assessment reasoning. 

A. Engineering Accountability Workflow 

The workflow is built on a simple principle: delegate production and facilitation, not decision authority . AI may ask questions, expose assumptions, suggest alternatives, draft plans, generate or modify code, and assist with diagnosing failures. Students, however, remain responsible for interpreting requirements, determining scope and architecture, approving decisions, inspecting generated changes, evaluating verifica-tion evidence, and authorising integration into the shared system. Table II summarises this division of authority. The workflow establishes explicit human decision points around AI-assisted work. During planning, AI is used as a facilitator rather than as the project owner: it may surface assumptions, challenge incomplete reasoning, and suggest alternatives, but teams must review and approve the resulting decisions. During implementation, agentic tools may oper-ate within personal development branches or forks, while changes enter the shared repository through human-reviewed pull requests. This creates a governance boundary between a relatively permissive delegation space and a human-reviewed integration space. Conceptually, the workflow is: Frame → Delegate → Inspect 

→ Verify → Review → Integrate .

B. Assessment Evidence Architecture (AEA) 

The AEA organises assessment evidence across six comple-mentary channels (Table III). These channels are distributed across the course rather than instantiated equally in every as-sessment activity, and no single channel is treated as sufficient evidence of engineering competence. Two distinctions are important. First, process/origin evi-dence is not equivalent to reflection. The AI Journal builds on a previously developed structured framework for making otherwise hidden aspects of AI-assisted engineering activity visible [7]. For this course, it was further integrated into the development workflow as a Visual Studio Code extension, with journal records stored within Git commit bodies. This associates declared AI-assisted activity with a timestamped point in the repository history and the corresponding changes, allowing journal records to be interpreted alongside commits, pull requests, and other development evidence. Within the AEA, these records therefore provide process/origin evidence about where and how AI-assisted activity occurred; they are not treated as direct measures of competence or learning. Second, explanation and individual assurance support dif-ferent inferences. A team demonstration may provide useful evidence that the system and its design can be explained, but it does not establish that every team member possesses the same understanding. Individual assurance therefore requires additional evidence that can be attributed to a particular student under more controlled conditions. 

C. Collect–Cross-check–Escalate (CCE) 

The architecture becomes a coherent assessment system only when evidence from the six channels is interpreted in relation to other evidence rather than treated as a set of independent observations. We describe this assessment reasoning as CCE. CCE is not a scoring formula; it is a reasoning logic for combining complementary evidence with different strengths and limitations. CCE describes the intended reasoning structure of the assessment architecture rather than a fully standardised marking protocol. In this first deployment, evidence collection was operationalised systematically across the course, whereas cross-channel comparison and escalation were not implemented through a formal course-wide pro-tocol with predefined triggers. Assessors nevertheless used additional inspection and targeted questioning when concerns, gaps, or inconsistencies became apparent during routine teach-ing and assessment activities, particularly during the weekly stand-ups. These follow-ups were recorded as part of course operations, but were initiated through assessor judgement rather than systematic CCE triage. We therefore treat CCE as the reasoning structure that emerged from and describes these practices, while recognising that its systematic opera-tionalisation remains incomplete. This distinction between the designed architecture and its operational realisation motivates Lesson L2 (Section VI). 

a) Collect: Evidence is generated through both ordinary engineering activity and formal assessment, including submit-ted artefacts, Git and pull-request histories, test and CI records, demonstrations, reflections, and individual assessment tasks. Collection is distributed across the course so that evidence arises at multiple points in the engineering process rather than only at final submission. 

b) Cross-check: Evidence from one channel is inter-preted in relation to other channels that support different inferences. Strong product evidence does not by itself es-tablish strong process or collaboration evidence; a polished repository does not remove the need for reproducibility; and reflective accounts can be compared with contribution traces and declared AI use. Likewise, performance in open-ended AI-assisted project work is interpreted alongside evidence from more controlled individual assessments elsewhere in the course. 

c) Escalate: Material inconsistencies, gaps, or unex-plained discrepancies may trigger targeted follow-up rather than being absorbed into an overall score. For example, missing or inconsistent AI-use records may lead to targeted questioning; uncertainty about individual contribution can be examined through commits, pull requests, reviews, self-reflections, and peer evidence; and submitted work that cannot be adequately explained may prompt individual oral follow-up or additional inspection. For example, consider a team submission that demonstrates strong technical functionality but is accompanied by sparse pull-request and review activity. Under CCE, the product and process/origin evidence would first be considered together rather than scored as independent observations. The assessor could then examine complementary evidence, such as verifica-tion records, timestamped AI Journal records associated with the development history, and students’ explanations of the af-fected components. If these sources did not adequately explain the discrepancy, the case could be escalated through targeted inspection or questioning focused on the relevant engineering decisions. The purpose of escalation is not to penalise the discrepancy itself, but to obtain additional evidence where the existing channels do not support a sufficiently clear inference about the competence or contribution being assessed. V. D EPLOYMENT AND OPERATION 

A. Repeated Individual Engineering Cycles 

Each week, students receive a private individual reposi-tory containing the laboratory task and supporting materials. Repository provisioning is automated through the GitHub API, substantially reducing the manual effort required to distribute and initialise repeated individual workspaces. Students com-plete their work within these repositories and may use AI tools where appropriate. AI Journal records were captured through the Visual Studio Code extension described in Section IV-B and embedded in Git commit bodies, integrating AI-use declaration into the normal repository workflow. Milestones are submitted through Gradescope, where automated checks provide rapid feedback against specified requirements such as file structure, generated outputs, unit-test behaviour, and required artefacts. The repositories also preserve each student’s development history, providing repeated evidence of process, verification, and individual progress while giving students regular practice with software-engineering and version-control workflows. By the end of Week 11, 957 individual weekly repos-itories had been created, in addition to 34 group-project repositories supporting Assignments 1 and 2. From Week 4 onwards, the teaching team also conducted weekly stand-up-style discussions with each project team. Staff rotated between groups and asked each student what they had completed, what they were currently working on, and whether they were encountering blockers. These discussions typically lasted around 10 minutes per team but could extend beyond 20 minutes when substantial questions or technical difficulties arose. The stand-ups were formative rather than separately marked; their primary purposes were to provide recurring oral visibility of individual progress, surface difficulties early, and create opportunities for timely intervention. Notes from these discussions were retained as supporting evidence when later addressing concerns about group dynamics or contribution. 

B. Project Workflow and Assessment Continuity Assignment 1 is the first major integration point. Teams develop a modular autonomous perception and navigation library designed to serve as the baseline for Assignment 2. The specification requires an installable Python package, a clean API, a simulator-independent core, unit testing, documenta-tion, installation guidance, a worked example, and Git-based collaboration. Reproducibility is operationalised explicitly: a new user should be able to clone the repository and run the submitted demonstration on a supported machine within 15 minutes. The marking process enforces this threshold. Assessors use a shared rubric discussed in a calibration meeting and attempt to reproduce and inspect the submitted system. Failure to achieve successful setup within 15 minutes is penalised in the relevant criteria, although assessors may continue trou-bleshooting to inspect remaining functionality. Process evidence is explicitly assessed rather than collected only for visibility. Collaboration and repository criteria include meaningful commits, pull requests and reviews, evidence of shared contribution, and professional repository organisation. Students work through personal forks and focused branches, with peer review expected before changes are merged into the group repository or the main branch. Weak process evidence can therefore reduce marks even when technical functionality is strong. The assignment concludes with an approximately eight-minute demonstration followed by a two-minute Q&A. Al-though the specification states that every team member should be able to answer questions about any component, the available Q&A time usually allowed only one, and occasionally two, students to respond. We therefore interpret this activity as providing limited team-level explanation evidence rather than systematic individual assurance. Students also submit an individually marked self-reflection covering contribution evidence, a learning or challenge episode, and AI use grounded in specific journal entries. In addition, students provide structured feedback to at least two other teams on strengths, potential improvements, useful questions for further examination, and ideas transferable to their own project. Each team receives this aggregated feedback anonymously as GitHub issues carried forward into Assign-ment 2. 

Assignment 2 deliberately begins from the Assignment 1 baseline rather than allowing a clean restart. Teams must reuse, refactor, or extend the existing library into a more complete autonomous system. The task remains intentionally open-ended: teams select their target capability and may work in different simulation or hardware environments, but must justify scope, design choices, evidence, and limitations. Before development, teams submit a short proposal de-scribing the intended deliverable, platform, reuse of the As-signment 1 baseline, technical risks, and expected demon-stration evidence. Scope feedback is returned before substan-tial implementation begins, allowing feasibility and risk to be addressed prospectively. Assignment 1 feedback is also embedded into the development environment through GitHub issues where appropriate, so identified weaknesses become actionable engineering tasks rather than post-hoc comments. The two assignments therefore create a continuity chain from baseline architecture, through assessment feedback, to open-ended extension. 

C. AI-Practice Feedback and Agentic-Development Instruc-tion 

The AI Journals were used not only to document AI-assisted activity, but also as a source of aggregate evidence about emerging AI-assisted engineering practices. Building on their combination of interaction tracking and reflective auditing [7], the teaching team used recurring cohort-level patterns to inform feedback sessions in Weeks 4 and 8. The Week 4 intervention focused on prompting: providing task context, concrete evidence such as tracebacks or outputs, explicit constraints, expected behaviour, and verification crite-ria rather than relying on broad or context-free requests. By Week 8, the emphasis had shifted from prompt construction toward agentic engineering, including providing agents with relevant artefacts rather than only textual descriptions, review-ing generated changes, testing modifications through available verification mechanisms, and making explicit adopt, override, or could-not-verify decisions. These interventions were aimed to form a course-level formative loop: Observe → Aggregate → Teach Back 

→ Raise Expectations → Observe Again .

The second half of the course extended this loop from for-mative feedback into explicit instruction on agentic software development. Part of the Week 9 lecture addressed task and execution boundaries, code ownership and review respon-sibilities, repository-level agent instructions and skill files, human approval before integration, and systematic inspection of agent-generated changes. A third cohort-level AI-practice reflection is scheduled for Week 13 to revisit how students’ approaches to delegation, verification, review, and agent governance have evolved across the semester. Because this activity had not yet occurred at the reporting point, it is treated as planned course design rather than deployment evidence. 

D. Individual Assurance and Escalation 

The redesign does not attempt to make every assessment activity AI-resistant. Open practical work and team projects assess performance in a modern tool-rich environment, while individual quizzes, weekly milestones, targeted oral follow-up, and the supervised final examination provide evidence that can be attributed more directly to individual students. The final examination accounts for 30% of the unit mark and includes a hurdle requirement: students must achieve at least 30% of the available examination marks to pass the unit. These components support two complementary claims: (1) whether students can engineer effectively when AI tools and collaboration are available, and (2) what knowledge and reasoning can reasonably be attributed to an individual un-der more controlled conditions. The architecture treats these claims as complementary rather than substitutable. Where evidence from open project work is incomplete or inconsistent, targeted inspection or oral follow-up can be used to obtain additional individual evidence. Escalation is therefore selective rather than universal: controlled assessment provides a baseline of individual assurance, while further follow-up is reserved for cases in which other evidence remains insufficient or contradictory. 

E. Operational Scale and Workload 

Automation made repeated repository provisioning, evi-dence collection, and autograding operationally feasible, but it did not make the redesign inexpensive to run. Each tutor was allocated two two-hour practical sessions, two hours of preparation, and a one-hour coordination meeting per week. The coordinator’s workload was not formally time-tracked, but the first deployment required substantial additional effort for lecture redevelopment, laboratory and assessment design, repository automation, autograder development, AI-Journal tooling and processing, coordination, online support, and project feedback. Much of this work was concentrated in a single coordinating role. At this scale, automation supported the management of hundreds of repositories and repeated evidence-collection activities across the 87-student cohort. Assessors were also provided with automated repository-analysis tools developed by the course coordinator that sum-marised commit histories, pull requests, reviews, contribu-tion distributions, document lengths, review behaviour, merge patterns, and selected software-quality indicators used in the assessment rubrics. These tools reduced the effort required to locate potentially relevant evidence, but they did not replace assessor judgement about its meaning or adequacy. VI. L ESSONS LEARNED 

L1: AI Changes Where Assessment Criteria Carry Evidential Weight 

A common assumption in AI-permissive course design is that AI shifts the difficulty of assessment from artefact production towards higher-order reasoning. However, the first deployment suggests a more specific assessment-design chal-lenge: AI assistance does not affect all rubric criteria equally .Criteria that traditionally function as quality floors, such as whether software can be installed, whether a repository is well organised, or whether code exhibits basic structural separation, became comparatively easier for students to satisfy with AI assistance. By contrast, criteria involving system integration, cross-component behaviour, failure diagnosis, and the justifi-cation of engineering trade-offs continued to reveal substantial differences in the quality of students’ work. This matters because rubric weights established for pre-GenAI conditions may no longer reflect where meaningful evidence of competence is most visible. If substantial weight remains attached to criteria that AI can readily help students satisfy, while comparatively little weight is placed on integra-tion, verification, diagnosis, and engineering decision-making, higher artefact quality may contribute disproportionately to the final mark without providing correspondingly strong evidence of student capability. The Assignment 2 rubric was revised in response to this observation. Integration quality and engineering improvement over the Assignment 1 baseline were given greater promi-nence, and students were required to provide explicit evidence of integration-stage decisions, including where AI-generated components required adaptation, what was verified before integration, and what was rejected or overridden. The resulting lesson is not that AI-assisted criteria should be removed, but that rubric weight should be reconsidered according to where human engineering judgement remains visible and consequen-tial. L2: Multiple Evidence Channels Do Not Automatically Create Cross-Checking 

The deployment revealed two related limitations of a multi-channel architecture. First, making several evidence channels available does not ensure that they will be systematically cross-checked. Second, cross-checking is only informative to the extent that the channels provide meaningfully complementary evidence. The CCE logic assumes that evidence from different chan-nels is interpreted together rather than treated as a set of parallel observations. The first deployment exposed a gap between this design intent and operational practice. Cross-channel follow-up did occur: during weekly stand-ups, asses-sors sometimes examined additional evidence or questioned students when concerns or inconsistencies became apparent. However, these cases arose through assessor judgement rather than a systematic course-wide triage procedure. At a cohort size of 87 students, and under a substantial first-deployment workload, evidence collection was therefore much easier to operationalise than systematic cross-channel interpretation. AI Journals, oral Q&As, repository histories, reflections, and product evidence were all available for such follow-up, but relating them systematically across the cohort remained diffi-cult. One consequence was that potentially meaningful config-urations of evidence were difficult to identify systematically. For example, no single channel may indicate a problem, yet a combination of unusually polished product evidence, sparse development traces, highly generic AI-use records, and weak explanation may warrant closer inspection. Detecting such pat-terns requires assessors to compare channels explicitly rather than interpret each one independently. In a future deployment, this comparison should be supported through pre-processing or structured triage so that cross-checking becomes a distinct phase of assessment rather than an implicit expectation placed on individual assessors. Candidate triage rules include flagging strong product evidence accompanied by sparse development or review traces; reflection or AI-use accounts that are difficult to reconcile with observable repository activity; and claims of verification that are not supported by corresponding test, CI, or reproducibility evidence. Such indicators should trigger closer cross-channel inspection rather than directly determine marks, preserving assessor judgement while concentrating limited attention on cases where complementary evidence is most informative. A second lesson concerns the independence of evidence channels. Six channels do not necessarily provide six inde-pendent sources of assurance. A student may use AI to assist with code, journal entries, reflective writing, and prepared ex-planations, making these channels partly correlated outputs of the same AI-mediated process. Evidence is therefore stronger when channels depend on meaningfully different capabilities or operating conditions. The practical implication is that evidence architecture should be designed not only around the number of channels, but around their complementarity and independence. Repli-cation therefore requires explicit resources for cross-channel comparison and deliberate inclusion of evidence sources that cannot all be satisfied through the same AI-assisted workflow. In the next offering, we plan to allocate dedicated time within tutors’ weekly workload to review each team’s repository activity, including commits, pull requests, and review histories, before stand-up meetings. We also plan to provide specialised repository-analysis tools that summarise potentially relevant evidence and help tutors identify issues requiring discussion. In the first deployment, repository activity was reviewed weekly by the course coordinator, leading to targeted interven-tion in three instances; however, the available tutor workload did not support comparable preparation and systematic dis-cussion across all teams. The planned change therefore shifts cross-channel inspection from a coordinator-centred activity towards a resourced part of tutors’ preparation for evidence-informed stand-up meetings. 

L3: Designed Evidence Strength May Differ from Realised Evidence Strength 

The first deployment showed that the intended evidential role of an assessment activity may differ substantially from the evidence it produces under real operating constraints. The As-signment 1 demonstration was designed to provide explanation evidence and to contribute to individual assurance: all team members were expected to understand the complete system and to be able to answer questions about any component. In practice, however, the two-minute Q&A window usually allowed only one, and occasionally two, students to respond. The activity therefore provided useful team-level expla-nation evidence but much weaker individual evidence than intended. This limitation arose from an operational constraint: given the number of teams scheduled within each practical session, substantially more than approximately ten minutes per team could not realistically be allocated to the combined demonstration and Q&A. The available time therefore limited the extent to which individual understanding could be sampled across all team members. This constraint could not be removed within the current offering. As a partial mitigation, the Week 12 practical is repurposed as a formative help-desk session. Students first will complete a 40-minute mock final examination as their individual weekly quiz and receive feedback in preparation for the supervised final exam. During the remaining practical time, each team works with its assigned assessor to walk through its codebase, deliverables, and system components, discuss the rubric, and identify missing or incomplete elements. No marks will be assigned during these interactions, but relevant obser-vations will be logged which can later inform the assessor’s interpretation of the submitted work. For future offerings, the more direct response is to increase the duration of practical sessions during demonstration weeks so that each team can be allocated a longer assessment win-dow. A feasible target is approximately 15 minutes per team, comprising an eight-minute demonstration followed by around seven minutes of questioning. This would provide substantially greater opportunity to sample individual understanding while retaining the team demonstration format. More generally, where individual oral assurance is important, sufficient contact time must be treated as part of the assessment design rather than as an implementation detail. 

L4: Automation Scales Collection More Readily Than Inter-pretation 

Automation was essential to operating the redesign at cohort scale. Repository provisioning, milestone checking, autograding, and repository analysis reduced the manual effort required to create workspaces, collect evidence, and locate potentially relevant process information across hundreds of repositories. Without this infrastructure, repeated individual evidence collection would have been difficult to sustain, if not impossible. However, the first deployment also showed that automa-tion addresses only part of the scaling problem. Determining whether a pull request represents meaningful review, whether a reflection is consistent with contribution traces, whether verifi-cation evidence is adequate, or whether contradictory evidence warrants escalation still requires human judgement. The same applies to formative feedback, oral questioning, scope review, and interpretation of unusual cross-channel patterns. The practical lesson is to distinguish collection scalability 

from interpretation scalability . The deployment demonstrated that substantial parts of evidence collection and aggrega-tion could be automated; however, the architecture remains expensive to operate because interpretation, cross-checking, and escalation are attention-intensive activities. Replication therefore requires explicit workload allocation for these inter-pretive stages, not only technical infrastructure for collecting evidence. VII. D ESIGN IMPLICATIONS AND TRANSFERABILITY 

The experience suggests a reframing of a common assessment-design question. Rather than asking “ How can this assessment be made AI-proof? ”, educators can ask “ What evidence would make the intended competence observable when AI assistance is available? ”. The answer depends on the competence being assessed. Authentic AI-assisted project work may provide strong evidence of integration, collabo-ration, verification, and engineering decision-making, while other outcomes may require more controlled individual evi-dence such as supervised practical work, oral questioning, or written examination. The architecture is therefore not an argument for universally permitting AI, nor does it prescribe a particular technology stack. The essential requirement is not GitHub, Gradescope, or a custom AI-use declaration tool, but complementary evidence with different limitations and identifiable points of human responsibility. VIII. S COPE AND LIMITATIONS 

a) Context and transferability: We described a single of-fering of a postgraduate, software-intensive engineering course at one institution. The course benefits from a technically favourable setting, including access to Git-based workflows, automated assessment infrastructure, and substantial coordi-nator involvement. The exact implementation may therefore not transfer directly to larger cohorts, less software-intensive disciplines, or settings with more limited technical and staffing resources. We consequently frame transferability primarily at the level of design principles rather than specific tools or assessment mechanisms. 

b) Evidence and effectiveness: There is no comparison condition, and no student-level learning outcomes, marks, or longitudinal behavioural change are analysed in this paper as the course is still running. The deployment observations therefore support lessons about the design and operation of the assessment system, but not causal claims that the redesign improved learning, engineering competence, or assessment validity. 

c) Status of the architecture: The AEA six evidence channels and CCE logic constitute a design decomposition rather than a validated measurement model of engineering competence. Each channel has known limitations, and the presence of multiple channels does not guarantee independent or reliable evidence. In particular, AI-assisted students may produce correlated evidence across code, documentation, re-flections, and prepared explanations. The architecture should therefore be understood as a validity-oriented design strategy whose evidential claims require further empirical evaluation. 

d) Operational and reporting boundaries: The first de-ployment required substantial human effort despite extensive automation, particularly for feedback, cross-channel interpre-tation, and targeted follow-up. Visible assessment mechanisms may also influence student behaviour: AI-use records, reposi-tory activity, and reflections can become compliance-oriented when students know that these traces are assessed. Finally, we report the course at the stated reporting point; activities not yet completed are identified as planned rather than treated as deployment evidence. Finally, student-level records are not analysed in this paper. IX. C ONCLUSION 

When GenAI makes substantial software artefact production delegable, assessment can no longer rely on artefact quality alone as evidence of the competence behind it. This paper reports one whole-course response: structuring AI-assisted en-gineering so that students retain decision authority at planning, verification, review, and integration boundaries, while com-petence is inferred from complementary evidence distributed across the engineering and assessment process. The experience suggests that the practical unit of redesign is not the isolated “AI-resistant” task but the assessment system as a whole. Evidence must be collected across the engineering process, interpreted in relation to complementary sources, and followed up when important gaps or inconsistencies remain.
