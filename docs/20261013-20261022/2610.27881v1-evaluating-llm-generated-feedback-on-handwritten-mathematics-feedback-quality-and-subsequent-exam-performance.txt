# ABSTRACT 

We evaluate the quality of feedback and practice exercises generated by GPT-5.1 from handwritten university mathematics answers and their grades, and examine whether receiving this feedback improves subsequent exam performance. We use six weekly bonus tests in an English ( n = 343 )and a Dutch ( n = 236 ) cohort. Tests 1 and 3 used a crossover feedback design; otherwise, all attendees received feedback. To evaluate the feedback, six reviewers assessed 157 messages from three English-cohort tasks using six criteria scored from 0 to 3. The practice exercises received the highest proportion of strong ratings (95.5%), while diagnostic specificity (41.1%) and clarity and tone (33.4%) received fewer. Feedback for the lowest-scoring answers was rated as less actionable, suggesting that these students may need more targeted guidance from instructors. Comparisons of related exam questions showed mixed results, with no statistically significant differences after adjustment across four comparisons. Greater attendance and feedback exposure were associated with higher exam scores, but the contribution of feedback could not be separated from attendance and practice. Our findings show the potential of an integrated framework merging instructor guidance with automated feedback generation to provide personalised learning support at scale. 

Keywords Personalised Feedback · Handwritten assessment · Large language models 

# 1 Introduction 

A grade tells a student how well they performed, but it does not necessarily tell them what went wrong or how to improve. This creates an important gap between a course’s evaluative and educational intent. Feedback is the most common way to close the gap between evaluation and learning, by helping students identify their mistakes and understand what they need to work on [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. However, providing such feedback for handwritten answers is time-consuming and makes short feedback loops non-viable when the number of students increases. Previous work has used data from students’ interactions with online learning activities to provide personalised feedback in large courses [6]. LLMs can assist human instructors by offering a way to quickly generate individual comments and practice exercises after an answer has been graded [7, 8, 9].

First, can GPT-5.1 provide accurate feedback and practice exercises suited to the level demonstrated in a student’s handwritten answer? Second, does receiving this feedback improve performance on related exam questions? A model might correctly explain how to solve an exercise but still provide little guidance to a student who does not know where to begin. It might also, because of hallucinations, offer advice on reasoning the student never used [10, 11, 12]. Additionally, LLMs might provide useful feedback, but the feedback might fail to engage students to use it in the learning process [13, 14]. To investigate the usefulness of the feedback, we therefore need to consider both what students receive and how they perform afterwards. Handwritten mathematics poses an additional challenge compared with typed or structured answers. Before generating feedback, the model must interpret the notation, spatial structure, corrections, and order of the student’s reasoning. Errors in this interpretation can carry into the diagnosis and feedback [15, 16, 17]. Research in this area has mainly focused on recognition, error detection, and grading [18, 19]. We extend this work by using handwritten university mathematics answers to generate individual feedback during a course. We assess the quality of this feedback and use a crossover design to examine whether receiving it improves later performance on exam questions testing the same mathematical skills. We build on the pipeline detailed in [20], in which handwritten assessments were scanned, graded with LLM support, and verified by human assessors. Once the grades were finalised, we used a separate generation step to provide comments on the student’s answer and two practice exercises. The feedback was then sent by email, together with the grade and an image of the student’s work. This allowed us to add individual feedback to the existing assessment process. In this study, we describe how we implemented this approach and evaluate its use across six weekly bonus tests. We first assess a sample of the feedback using criteria that distinguish mathematical correctness from the quality of the diagnosis, guidance, and presentation. We then compare performance on related exam questions between students who did and did not receive feedback on the corresponding bonus test. Finally, we consider how attendance and the number of tests with feedback relate to overall exam performance. 

# 2 Methods 

2.1 Participants and setting 

We collected data from the English- and Dutch-taught cohorts of the same undergraduate mathematics course at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel, with 343 and 236 students with bonus-test records, respectively. Both cohorts shared many similarities; they were taught by the same professor and followed the same course content and structure. However, they also differed in important ways, including teaching assistants and backgrounds: the Dutch-speaking students came from the same schooling system, while the English-speaking students came from a wide range of backgrounds. To investigate performance on related content, we linked specific bonus-test questions to final-exam questions at the start of the semester. Each cohort took a separate exam, but the topics and question types were closely matched. We linked the bonus-test data to exam records for 343 students in the English cohort and all 236 students in the Dutch cohort. Of these, 331 and 236 students participated in the first session exam. 

2.2 Bonus test design and A/B version groups 

Students could participate in six weekly bonus tests during the exercise sessions. Each test lasted 10 minutes, covered material from a previous exercise session, and consisted of two handwritten exercises. Participation was voluntary. The six tests could add up to 1.0 point to the final exam grade, which was graded out of 20. Each test contained a common question (Q1) and a version-specific question (Q2). Within a class group, students assigned to versions A and B received the same Q1. We used this question as an anchor to assess comparability across version groups. Across class groups, the questions covered the same topic at a similar level of difficulty, but could differ in their wording or problem instance. Feedback generation. After verifying the grades, we used GPT-5.1 to generate feedback for each student’s answer. We generated each feedback message only once. The model used the final grade to customize its comments and practice exercises to the student’s performance, without altering the grade itself. For each question, we asked it to identify strengths and mistakes in the answer and provide two practice exercises. To generate this feedback, we provided GPT-5.1 with the question text, a cropped image of the student’s answer, the official worked solution, and the final numerical grade. No names or student identifiers were included. We used the following prompt for both cohorts:     

> You are supposed to give personalized feedback to a student’s solution. Point out what they did well and what they could improve on. Be kind, constructive, and to the point. Use the solution provided to guide your feedback; you should not reference it explicitly. Treat the method used in the provided solution as the preferred method and keep your feedback and practice exercises in line with this method. You are not allowed to mention the final score. You can only use it to tailor your feedback. In this course, we only work with real numbers. So if the student does not mention complex numbers, neither should you. Moreover, it’s important to provide actionable feedback in the form of two sample exercises which they can use to improve their skills and practice more. For students with a score of 10, provide two similar exercises that are slightly more challenging. For students who did not attempt to solve the question, reassure them that it is not a problem and provide two simpler exercises to help them get started. For students who made a different exercise than the one asked, remind them to make the correct exercise in the future and give them two practice exercises. For all practice exercises, include only the final solution. Do not provide hints, explanations, or intermediate steps for these practice exercises. Students will not be able to respond to the feedback, or interact with you in any way. So please don’t prompt them to interact with you.

2.4 Evaluation of the generated feedback 

To evaluate the generated feedback, we sampled messages for three tasks from the English cohort: test 1, version B, Q2; test 4, version A, Q2; and test 5, Q1 in both versions. These tasks provided 82, 108, and 210 eligible messages, respectively. Each message concerned one student’s answer to one question. We stratified the sample by the verified grade to include feedback for students at different performance levels. The score bands were [0 , 1) , [1 , 4) , [4 , 7) , [7 , 10) ,and {10 }, with target counts of 5, 15, 15, 15, and 5 per task. When a band contained fewer messages than the target, we included all of them. This resulted in 51, 51, and 55 messages from the three bonus tests. Reviewers assessed each message alongside the student’s answer, the question, the worked solution, and the verified grade. We used six criteria to distinguish mathematical accuracy, diagnostic specificity, actionability, method alignment, clarity and tone, and the quality of the practice exercises (Table 2). Each criterion was scored from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating deficient, 1 weak, 2 adequate, and 3 strong feedback. The rubric provided a separate description of each category for each criterion. Reviewers also completed a checklist indicating whether the feedback mentioned a strength, proposed an improvement or extension, gave a concrete next step, and included two practice exercises. 2.5 Linking bonus tests to exam questions 

At the start of the semester, we linked specific bonus-test items to final-exam questions covering the same mathematical skill. For example, version B of Q2 in Test 1 concerned irrational inequalities, including domain restrictions and the conditions under which an inequality can be squared. The corresponding exam question assessed these same skills using a different inequality. Similarly, version A of Q2 in Test 3 concerned limits at positive infinity involving fractions with square roots and polynomials. These links allowed us to examine later performance on the specific content addressed by the feedback. The English- and Dutch-language exams were aligned and assessed the same types of mathematical questions, using different expressions. 3.3 Crossover analysis on linked exam questions 

Table 5 reports the primary crossover comparisons for the two linked exam questions derived from tests 1 and 3. The estimated differences are mixed in direction across cohorts and questions, and at the level of individual linked exam questions the randomized evidence does not indicate a stable feedback effect. This pattern is not entirely surprising in light of the broader feedback literature. Feedback effects are known to be sensitive to timing, specificity, and uptake [21, 3], and a single feedback exposure tied to one topic may simply be too light of an intervention to produce a reliable gain on one specific exam question asked months later.

# 4 Discussion 

In this study, we examined both the quality of AI-generated feedback on handwritten mathematics and its contribution to subsequent exam performance. The clearest positive result concerned the practice exercises: 95.5% of ratings placed them in the highest category for relevance, correctness, and calibration to the student’s needs. The feedback itself received more mixed assessments, particularly for diagnostic specificity and clarity. These findings show that the model could provide suitable additional practice even when its explanation of the student’s answer needed improvement. Informal comments collected from students attending the final lecture were consistent with these ratings. Several students reported that the feedback helped them identify what to work on or recognise what they had done well. Others described explanations as unclear or superficial, pointed out errors, or objected to the use of AI. Some also expressed disappointment when feedback was absent. Although these comments come from a self-selected group and cannot be taken as representative of either cohort, they point to the same weaknesses as the reviewer ratings. 7LLM-Generated Feedback on Handwritten Mathematics October 21, 2026 The lower ratings for diagnostic specificity and clarity echo previous trepidations with respect to AI feedback [22]. Reviewers described feedback that attributed reasoning to students which they had not used, proposed unnecessary corrections, or gave overly extensive explanations. These observations are relevant to the prompt, which required the model to identify both strengths and areas for improvement. When a student has answered correctly, this instruction may encourage the model to look for a weakness where little needs to be changed. For students who scored 0 or 1, the problem can be different. As R1 explained, a correct account of how to solve the entire exercise may still leave the student unsure how to proceed. We believe this distinction between correctness and usefulness deserves particular attention when generating feedback for students who struggle with the material. The practice exercises in our setup included only final answers, as the prompt explicitly excluded hints and intermediate steps. Providing a worked example or a first step could help these students begin practising. These observations suggest that the guidance should depend on how much progress the student has made. Students who have barely started may need help with a first step, while those with a correct answer may benefit more from an extension than from suggestions for improvement. Whether adapting the prompt in this way improves the feedback and subsequent performance remains to be tested. The positive relation between attendance, feedback exposure, and overall exam scores may suggest a direct benefit from receiving AI feedback. However, students who attended more tests also practised more, while differences in exposure at a given attendance level depended on which tests they attended. These observations therefore cannot resolve the inconclusive results of the randomised comparisons. We would need a design that varies repeated feedback exposure more extensively to assess whether its effects accumulate across the semester. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Each feedback rubric was graded by only two referees, who mostly aligned, but important differences existed, especially in the specificity and actionability of the feedback. Within the exam analysis, the comparison groups were relatively small and restricted to students who attended both crossover tests and had an observed outcome. Conditioning on attendance may affect the comparability created by random assignment, and feedback on one topic could also directly affect performance on another subject; for instance, feedback on limits could impact students’ ability to recognise oblique asymptotes. Students were informed that the generated feedback was AI-generated, which in turn could have lowered their confidence or reliance on the provided feedback [23, 24]; some informal student comments indicated objections to the use of AI. Our findings show that GPT-5.1 can generate practice exercises from handwritten mathematics answers that reviewers judge relevant, correct, and suited to the student’s level. They also identify where the accompanying feedback needs improvement, particularly in diagnosing mistakes and explaining how to proceed. The crossover comparisons did not establish statistically significant improvements in later exam performance. The positive associations with repeated feedback exposure warrant further investigation in a design that further separates feedback from attendance and practice. Recording whether students read the feedback and attempt the exercises would also help explain when the assessed quality of the material translates into learning.
