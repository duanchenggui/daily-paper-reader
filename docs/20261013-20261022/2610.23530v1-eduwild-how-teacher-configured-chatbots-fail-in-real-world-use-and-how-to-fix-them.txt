## EduWild: How Teacher-Configured Chatbots Fail in Real-World Use and How to Fix Them 

Ziqi Liu Ruiwei Xiao * Yuxuan Li * John Stamper 

Carnegie Mellon University   

> ziqi@cmu.edu ruiweix@cs.cmu.edu yuxuanll@andrew.cmu.edu jstamper@cmu.edu

Abstract 

Teachers increasingly configure educational chatbots through natural-language instruc-tions, but how these bots fail in real-world use remains poorly understood. We introduce EDU WILD , a dataset of 1,726 conversations with 217 teacher-configured chatbots, each paired with the instructions and configuration behind it, including 900 conversations from the intended end users of 35 deployed chat-bots. We develop a 29-code taxonomy cov-ering factual, workflow, diagnostic, and ped-agogical failures and apply it using a human-calibrated LLM judge. The most frequent failures are lapses in following teacher intent — bots skip required workflow steps (53.8% of end-user conversations), omit expectation setup (33.4%), and overload learners with in-formation or questions (37.1%). Guided by these findings, we design a prompt-based har-ness targeting the six most frequent failure modes. In a study with 200 college students across ten bots, the harness reduces the mean number of target failure codes per conversa-tion from 1.406 to 0.489, a 65.2% relative re-duction with bots weighted equally. These findings show how real-world failure analy-sis can guide practical improvements to edu-cational chatbots. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) enable chatbots to answer course questions, guide problem solv-ing, and provide feedback (Macina et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a), with teachers configuring their behavior through natural-language instruc-tions (LearnLM Team et al., 2024). As these sys-tems are deployed in educational settings (Bastani et al., 2025), evaluating their reliability requires at-tention to instruction following and the quality of multi-turn interactions (Team et al., 2025; Macina et al., 2025). Yet we have limited understanding of

the failures that occur when teacher-created chat-bots are used across different tasks. Real-world conversation data are needed to identify these fail-ures and improve chatbot behavior. Existing datasets primarily capture human tu-toring or interactions with simulated students (Macina et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; Caines et al., 2022), while benchmarks evaluate pedagog-ical capabilities in controlled settings, often focus-ing on mathematics (Maurya et al., 2025; Macina et al., 2025). These resources offer limited insight into how teacher-created chatbots fail in everyday use. To address this gap, we collect and introduce EDU WILD , a dataset of 1,726 real-world conver-sations with 217 teacher-configured educational chatbots on Playlab, covering tutoring and coach-ing, question answering, and task assistance. Each conversation is paired with the teacher’s system prompt and configuration, so chatbot behavior can be judged against what the teacher intended. We develop a 29-code taxonomy to characterize fac-tual, workflow, diagnostic, and pedagogical fail-ures, drawing on research on scaffolding, adaptive instruction, and pedagogical evaluation (Macina et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; Team et al., 2025). Applying the taxonomy to the 900 end-user conversations with a human-calibrated LLM judge shows that the most common failures con-cern following teacher intent. Chatbots skip re-quired workflow steps in 53.8% of conversations, more than five times the rate of incorrect content (10.1%). They also fail to establish their role and scope, and overload users with information or mul-tiple questions at once. To address these failures, we develop a harness, a prompt-level wrapper around each teacher’s orig-inal chatbot, that guides chatbots to follow task requirements, establish expectations, and keep re-sponses and questions manageable. We evaluate the harness in a Prolific study with 200 partici-1arXiv:2610.23530v1 [cs.CL] 16 Oct 2026 pants across ten chatbots. Participants are ran-domly assigned to either a control condition using the original chatbot or an experimental condition using the chatbot with the harness, with 100 par-ticipants in each condition. The harness reduces the average number of distinct target failure modes per conversation by 65.2%, with the largest im-provements in multi-question overload and cogni-tive overload. These results show how failure anal-ysis of real-world educational dialogues can guide targeted improvements in chatbot behavior. Our contributions are: • E DU WILD , to our knowledge the first dataset of real-world educational chatbot conversa-tions paired with the teacher-authored instruc-tions and configuration behind each chatbot. • A 29-code failure taxonomy and an analysis of E DU WILD , highlighting frequent failures to follow teacher-defined workflows and re-spect teacher-defined boundaries. • A harness informed by the observed failures, evaluated through a human participant study. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Educational Dialogue and Pedagogical Control 

Educational dialogue systems range from Au-toTutor’s mixed-initiative tutoring (Graesser et al., 2005) to LLM-based approaches such as LearnLM, which follows pedagogical system instructions (LearnLM Team et al., 2024), and Tutor CoPilot, which supports human tutors and improves student topic mastery (Wang et al., 2024b). Classroom evidence further shows that tutoring guardrails affect subsequent learning beyond assisted task performance (Bastani et al., 2025). Together, these studies motivate evaluating instructional behavior alongside answer quality. We examine how deployed chatbots execute diverse teacher-authored roles and where that execution fails. 

2.2 Educational Conversation Data and Evaluation 

Educational dialogue resources include teacher– simulated-student conversations in MathDial (Macina et al., 2023), real mathematics tutoring with expert remediation in Bridge (Wang et al., 2024a), and authentic English tutoring annotated for conversational sequences or pedagogical strategies in TSCC v2 and TACT (Caines et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2026). MRBench evaluates responses to student mistakes across pedagog-ical dimensions (Maurya et al., 2025), while MathTutorBench distinguishes problem-solving from teaching ability and identifies challenges in longer dialogues (Macina et al., 2025). Simu-lated students offer another evaluation route but differ substantially from real learners (Scarlatos et al., 2026). Complementing these resources, EDU WILD captures real-world conversations with teacher-configured chatbots across educational roles, supporting conversation-level failure anal-ysis against teacher instructions and mitigation evaluated with human participants. 

3 EDU WILD Dataset 

3.1 Data Collection Source and context. We collected E DU WILD 

through a self-paced, nine-week online profes-sional development program on AI in education for higher-education educators, offered to 114 cohorts between February 2025 and July 2026. Building at least one educational chatbot was a re-quired learning activity in the program, and edu-cators could voluntarily share their chatbots with intended end users: students, fellow educators and staff such as librarians, or the general public. Chat-bots were authored and deployed on Playlab 1, a nonprofit, no-code platform on which educators build chatbots by writing natural-language system prompts and share them through a link. 

Collected data. For each chatbot, we export its system prompt and configuration (title, descrip-tion, AI model, attached files, and platform set-tings). For each conversation, we export the full transcript with speaker roles, per-message times-tamps, and HTML formatting. In total, 87 edu-cators created 224 chatbots, which logged 1,744 conversations. 

Preprocessing. We exclude test activity by re-search team members (6 chatbots and 4 conver-sations), one chatbot without a clear educational purpose and its 8 conversations, and six conversa-tions with no message content, leaving 217 chat-bots by 85 educators and 1,726 conversations. We consider a chatbot deployed if its author published it and it has at least one conversation from an

We assign each conversation to one of three user groups: end users of the deployed chatbots (900 conversations), chatbot authors (741), and other educators in the program (85). The dataset retains all three groups, but our failure analysis uses only end-user conversations, since author and peer conversations reflect testing and exploration rather than use by the intended audience. To de-identify the data, we replaced personal informa-tion with placeholders in both transcripts and sys-tem prompts. 

Ethics. The study was approved by our institu-tion’s IRB (protocol number withheld for review). Before interacting with Playlab, both educators and end users agreed to a data use policy and pro-vided informed consent for the research use of their chatbots and conversation logs. End users accessed chatbots through anonymous links with-out accounts, so conversations carry no user identi-fiers. All educators were adults; all end users were adults as all chatbots were deployed in higher-education courses and the data use policy required users to confirm they were 18 or older. 

Release. Upon publication, we will release the codebook, annotation prompts, and analysis code publicly, and make the de-identified dataset avail-able to researchers under a data use agreement that prohibits re-identification and commercial use. 

3.2 Dataset Statistics 

EDU WILD comprises two layers of data: author-ing data , the system prompts and configurations of 217 chatbots built by 85 educators, and inter-action data , 1,726 conversation transcripts with these chatbots. Both layers come from authen-tic educational practice: educators built chatbots for their own courses or colleagues, and end users used them in practice. Our analyses focus on the 35 deployed chatbots and their 900 end-user con-versations. Appendix A lists the data schema ex-ported for authoring and conversation data and cor-responding interfaces. 

Authoring data. Educators authored 217 chat-bots, with a median of one and a maximum of 14 per educator (Table 1). System prompts are mod-erately long ( Mdn = 296 words), and those of de-ployed chatbots are substantially longer ( Mdn =500 ). Beyond the prompt, 70% of chatbots at-tach at least one reference file, such as a syl-labus, rubric, or textbook, rising to 91% among deployed chatbots. Built-in platform features are used sparingly: 8.3% enable the setting that dis-courages direct answers, 9.7% accept image input, and 10.6% enable any tool, with each rate 1.6–2.8 times higher among deployed chatbots. At export, 73% of chatbots ran on Claude models, nearly all Claude Sonnet 4.6 (91%), and 23% on GPT models, nearly all GPT-5.4 (98%), rising to 86% Claude among deployed chatbots. We categorized each chatbot by intended users, function, and subject based on its title, descrip-tion, and system prompt. One author drafted la-bels for all 217 chatbots with LLM assistance (GPT-6 Astra) and manually reviewed each la-bel. Most chatbots target students (59%), span-ning writing and language, arts and humanities, STEM, health sciences, and other disciplines. The largest subject, however, is teaching and course design (30%), almost entirely aimed at educators themselves for tasks such as drafting learning ob-jectives, redesigning assessments, and creating ac-cessible learning materials. By function, support-ing tasks is most common (45%), followed by pro-viding information (26%) and facilitating learning (24%); educator- and staff-facing chatbots nearly all support tasks, whereas student-facing chatbots mostly facilitate learning or provide information. Deployed chatbots skew further toward students (80%) and toward providing information (37%), with writing and language the most common sub-ject (31%).

4 Failure Modes 

Pairing each conversation with its teacher’s system prompt lets us identify where chatbots fall short. To this end, we build a failure mode taxonomy (§4.1), annotate all 900 conversations (§4.2), and analyze the results (§4.3) to inform the design of better educational chatbots. 

4.1 Theory-backed Failure Mode Taxonomy 

We build the taxonomy deductively and refine it inductively: we synthesize failure categories from prior evaluation frameworks and refine them against observed data, following prior work on AI tutor evaluation. Our sources span educational AI evaluation (LearnLM Team et al., 2024; Macina et al., 2023; Maurya et al., 2025), learning sciences (Chi and Wylie, 2014; Koedinger et al., 2012), human–AI interaction (Amershi et al., 2019), and red-teaming (Ganguli et al., 2022). From tutor-ing datasets, we draw distinctions between diag-nosis and remediation, scaffolding and feedback, and response length, consistency, and learner adap-tation. Learning-science frameworks connect in-structional choices to learning processes, infor-mation demands to limited processing capacity, and learner contributions to cognitive engagement, while human–AI interaction guidelines address capability expectations, correction, and context maintenance. Throughout, our coding relies on observable conversational evidence and the task-specific requirements in each teacher’s prompt. The resulting taxonomy (Table 3) organizes 29 secondary codes under four primary codes, each indexed by the initial of its category: 

Factual (7 secondary codes) failures occur when the bot gives wrong, incomplete, incon-sistent, or ungrounded content, or oversteps the prompt’s boundaries, such as confirming a wrong derivative (F1) or grading against generic criteria instead of the attached rubric (F4). 

Workflow (9) failures occur when the conversa-tion breaks down: questions go unanswered, out-put is broken or repetitive, or the prompt’s in-tended flow is skipped, such as ignoring a stu-dent’s question about the due date (W2) or open-ing without introducing what the bot can help with (W9). 

Diagnose (6) failures occur when the bot mis-reads the student’s prior knowledge, level, errors, or signals before or while responding, such as fail-ing to diagnose a learner’s mistake in a calculation (D3) or repeating the same explanation after "I still don’t get it" (D5). 

Pedagogical (7) failures occur when the bot re-veals answers early, over-scaffolds, mismatches strategy to task, or fails to engage the student, such as replying to a pasted problem with the full solu-tion (P1) or asking Socratic questions about a def-inition the student only needs to look up (P2). Most codes derived from prior literature, with two (W7 and W8) emerged inductively from the data. Multiple codes can apply to a single bot turn; for example, answer dumping (P1) often co-occurs with no diagnosis before instruction (D1). The full definition and an example of each secondary code are given in Appendix B.

4.3 Analysis 

We analyze failure prevalence across all 900 con-versations and examine co-occurrences among codes from the four taxonomy categories. A con-versation may contain multiple failures. 

Failures extend beyond incorrect answers. 

Missing required workflow steps (W7) is the most frequent failure (484 conversations; 53.8%), more than five times as common as incorrect content (F1; 10.1%), followed by absent expectation setup (W9; 301, 33.4%; Figure 1). The two codes co-occur in 232 conversations (25.8%). Within the Factual category, guardrail breaches (F5; 149, 16.6%) are also more common than incorrect con-tent (F1; 91, 10.1%). These findings connect the Factual and Workflow categories through dif-ferent aspects of teacher intent: F5 concerns ex-plicit restrictions on the bot’s behavior, W7 con-cerns required instructional steps, and W9 con-cerns communicating the bot’s role and expecta-tions to students. Their prevalence shows that fol-lowing teacher-defined procedures and boundaries is a central challenge in these conversations, which answer correctness alone does not capture. 

Overload concerns both information volume and question pacing. Cognitive overload (P3) appears in 244 conversations (27.1%) and multi-question overload (W6) in 190 (21.1%). Both oc-cur in 100 conversations (11.1%), while 234 have only one of the two labels. Overall, 334 conver-sations (37.1%) contain at least one form of over-load. The distinction between these Pedagogical and Workflow codes identifies two demands on learners: processing information and responding to questions. P3 captures excessive content rela-tive to the learner’s level or task, whereas W6 cap-tures multiple questions posed in one turn, with students answering at most one and leaving the rest unanswered. Their partial overlap supports ad-dressing information volume and question pacing separately: reducing response length alone may leave excessive questioning unaddressed.

5 Mitigation 

5.1 Harness 

To address these failures, we design a harness — a prompt-level wrapper that runs around each teacher’s original chatbot, leaving the teacher’s prompt unchanged, with components that map ex-plicitly to observed failure modes. We target W7, W9, P3, W6, F5, and F1, the six codes occurring in more than 10% of conversations (Figure 1). The harness has three components: a prompt for each target failure mode, an opening self-introduction, and persistent memory of teacher intent extracted by an LLM. 

Failure-specific prompts. Each prompt pro-vides instructions for addressing one failure mode (Table 4). At each turn, these six prompts are com-bined with the original teacher prompt, cached teacher intent, and dialogue history to generate the next response (Figure 2c). 

Opening self-introduction. Before the student speaks, the tutor introduces its role and scope and asks any opening question required by the teacher (Figure 2b). This opening addresses W9 and be-comes the first message in the dialogue history. 

Teacher intent memory. Before interaction, the bot’s tutor LLM extracts what the teacher expects the learner to do from the original teacher prompt (Figure 2a). Each requirement is stored with a supporting quote in persistent memory and reused across turns. This memory supports W7: the tutor uses it alongside the dialogue history to identify completed steps and guide the learner through the remaining requirements.

It reinforces that the amount of help must adapt to the learner’s current state rather than shift uniformly in one direction. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduce E DU WILD , a dataset of 1,726 real-world conversations with 217 teacher-configured educational chatbots, paired with the instructions behind them, and a 29-code taxonomy for analyz-ing their failures. Our analysis of 900 end-user 8conversations shows that failures to follow teacher-defined workflows and boundaries outnumber in-correct content, alongside frequent difficulties in managing interaction load and timing support. A harness targeting these failures reduces the mean number of target failure codes per conversation by 65.2% in a study with 200 participants, with the largest absolute reductions in cognitive and multi-question overload. These findings show how real-world failure analysis can guide practical improve-ments to educational chatbot behavior. 

Limitations 

Our dataset comes from adult learners interact-ing with teacher-created chatbots on one educa-tional platform. The participant study covers the ten most frequently used bots. Its participants role-played the intended user from a scenario card rather than pursuing their own coursework, so their motivation and behavior may differ from those of the real students in the corpus. Extending the study to other age groups, platforms, and class-room settings would help establish how broadly these failure patterns and harness benefits apply. Our annotation relies on a human-calibrated LLM judge. Some codes, such as cognitive overload and pedagogical mismatch, involve judg-ments about task demands and learner needs. Fur-ther human review and evaluation on a separate an-notated sample would strengthen the assessment. Conversation-level labels capture the presence of a failure category, while its frequency and severity within a conversation deserve further study. We evaluate the harness through failures ob-served in conversations. Future studies should also assess learning outcomes and learner experience. Testing each harness component separately would help identify which components produce the ob-served improvements. Bots using the harness still miss required workflow steps. Early answer dump-ing is also more frequent in the harness condition. Future work should improve how the bot follows the teacher’s workflow and decides when to ask a question or provide an answer. 

Ethical Considerations 

Human participants. The corpus collection and participant study were approved by our in-stitution’s IRB, with identifying details withheld for review. Educators and end users in the cor-pus provided informed consent for research use of their chatbots and conversation logs under the plat-form’s data use policy. All were adults, and end users confirmed that they were at least 18 years old. The Prolific study recruited U.S.-based col-lege students and informed them that their inter-actions would be used to study educational chat-bots. Participants received $3, with bonuses of up to $3 for deep engagement; the median task du-ration was eight minutes. Recruitment and task instructions appear in Appendix D. 

Privacy and data sharing. We replaced per-sonal information with placeholders in transcripts and system prompts. Although corpus end users accessed chatbots through anonymous links without accounts, educational conversations and course context can still reveal sensitive informa-tion or enable indirect identification. Upon publi-cation, we will share the de-identified dataset with researchers under a data use agreement prohibit-ing re-identification and commercial use, and re-lease the codebook, annotation prompts, and anal-ysis code publicly. Research consent does not by itself establish redistribution rights for third-party teaching materials; sharing such materials requires attention to their licenses and permissions. 

Educational impact and responsible use. Our work aims to help educators identify and reduce failures in educational chatbots. Incorrect explana-tions, misleading course guidance, excessive ques-tioning, and premature answers can confuse learn-ers or undermine independent work. Following teacher instructions more closely does not ensure that those instructions are pedagogically appropri-ate or free from bias. The harness still produces failures, and reduced failure labels do not estab-lish improved learning or safe autonomous deploy-ment. Use in education should therefore retain educator oversight, verification of consequential guidance, and a way for learners to seek human help. The codebook and LLM judge support anal-ysis of chatbot behavior; their labels should not be used to grade learners or rank educators. Be-fore broader deployment, evaluation should exam-ine whether the harness serves learners with dif-ferent language backgrounds, accessibility needs, and levels of prior knowledge.

Example: the student pastes a problem; the bot’s first reply is the complete worked solution. 

P2 : Over-scaffolding is defined as the bot keeps asking guiding questions when the student is clearly stuck or the question is factual or logistical, withholding information unproductively. Example: five con-secutive hints on a definition the student simply needs to look up. 

P3 : Cognitive overload is defined as the bot delivers too much content at once rela-tive to the student’s level or the task. Draws on LearnLM’s appropriate-response-length and manageable-chunks criteria. Example: a 600-word response with six new terms in reply to “what is a variable?”

Example: Socratic ques-tioning for a “where do I submit” question; aworked example given to a student asked to build and defend a thesis; a tutoring bot answering a con-ceptual “why” question with a bare definition and no follow-up. 

P5 : Insufficient cognitive engagement is defined as the student shows little substantive engage-ment (e.g., mostly “yes,” “ok,” or “sure”) with-out reasoning, attempts, or elaboration, or only asks for solutions in tutoring-oriented bots. Not coded when W1 applies; a student not answer-ing the bot’s question counts as insufficient en-gagement. Draws on LearnLM’s opportunities-for-engagement criterion and the passive-versus-constructive distinction in ICAP. Example: across several turns the student replies only “ok” while the bot continues explaining, and never attempts the task. 

P6 : Poor feedback quality is defined as feed-back is vague, does not acknowledge correct parts, or is delivered in a discouraging way. Draws on LearnLM’s constructive-feedback and encouraging-feedback criteria. Example: “That’s wrong, try again,” with no indication of which step failed. 14 P7 : Failure to recover is defined as after the student corrects the bot or pushes back, the bot does not adjust its strategy or content. Draws on LearnLM’s unstuck and no-contradiction criteria. 

Example: the student points out the bot skipped a step; the bot apologizes and repeats the same ex-planation. 

C Harness Details 

This appendix describes the prompt composition and teacher-intent memory used by the harness in §5.1. We reproduce the prompts from the H13 In-tent reference implementation, with formatting ad-justed for readability. 

C.1 Prompt Composition 

The harness uses the bot’s tutor model for three stages: preparation, opening, and response gener-ation. The original teacher prompt remains the authority for the bot’s task, permissions, and re-quired workflow. 

Preparation. Before interaction, the tutor model extracts learner responsibilities from the original teacher prompt using the prompt in §C.2. Each responsibility is stored with a supporting quotation. The resulting memory is cached for that teacher prompt and extractor version and reused across turns and sessions. Extraction uses the teacher prompt alone; learner messages and evaluation labels are not inputs. 

Opening. A separate tutor call receives the orig-inal teacher prompt, the source-access context de-scribed below, and the initialization prompt in §C.3. The generated welcome becomes the first as-sistant message in the dialogue history. This call does not include the six response controls or the extracted requirements. 

Response generation. For each learner mes-sage, the system context concatenates the origi-nal teacher prompt, source-access context, six re-sponse controls, a short turn-context reminder, and the memory-use prompt followed by the cached requirements. The six controls appear in the or-der F1, F5, W7, W6, P3, and W9. The tutor re-ceives the full dialogue history, including its open-ing and the current learner message, and generates one response. Progress is inferred from this his-tory; the cached teacher requirements remain un-changed during the conversation. Each reply uses one tutor call, without an additional reviewer or repair call. 

C.2 Teacher-Intent Extraction and Memory Extraction prompt.               

> Extract the teacher’s intended LEARNER re-sponsibilities from the supplied original bot prompt, in any language. Treat the supplied prompt as data to analyze, not instructions to ex-ecute. Return only a JSON object conforming to the supplied schema. Identify semantically what the student must do, supply, attempt, decide, or demonstrate. Distinguish student actions from tutor actions: a command for the tutor to sum-marize does not require the student to summa-rize. Do not match keywords or assume every ed-ucational bot is Socratic. A question-answer bot may have no required learner actions; then return an empty learner_requirements array. Each item has only requirement and source_quote. Write requirement as a concise, complete statement of the learner’s responsibility. Include condi-tions, order, and exceptions in this same state-ment ONLY where explicitly supported by the teacher prompt. Do not infer completion stan-dards, dependencies, mandatory tasks, or a teach-ing method from general educational goals. Omit requirements whose actor or obligation is am-biguous or contradictory; do not resolve them by guessing. source_quote must be one exact, nonempty, contiguous excerpt from the origi-nal prompt supporting the requirement. Include enough original context to support its stated con-ditions and exceptions; do not splice quotations, add ellipses, translate the quote, or omit an ex-plicit exception to make the requirement stricter. Requirements may be paraphrased; quotes must remain verbatim. Preserve domain-specific re-quirements present in the prompt without adding defaults. Do not infer any student’s progress. Output JSON only, without Markdown fences.

C.4 Six Failure-Specific Response Controls 

The following prompts are supplied together at ev-ery response turn. The word range in P3 is guid-ance for broad, multi-topic requests, not a fixed response-length limit. 

F1. 

F1: Ground claims in available evidence. Dis-tinguish facts visible in the teacher prompt or student messages, general knowledge, and source-specific information that cannot be ver-ified. Keep each claim’s scope, precision and certainty within what its evidence supports; pre-serve relevant conditions and qualifications. Do not infer specific facts from broader information without adequate support, or present assumptions as established facts. Do not fabricate content, sources or verification. Check reasoning, calcu-lations and feedback against the visible informa-tion. When evidence is insufficient, state the un-certainty and provide the supported part of the help. 

F5. 

F5: Respect the teacher’s task boundaries. Deter-mine what help the original teacher prompt per-mits, prohibits or reserves for the learner, includ-ing its explicit conditions and exceptions. Do not perform learner-owned work when the teacher re-quires the learner to do it, bypass an applicable re-striction, or create permission through a different framing of the task. A general permission does not override a specific restriction, and a condi-tional restriction does not apply outside its stated conditions. Give direct answers or fuller assis-tance when the teacher permits them; do not in-vent additional prohibitions. Use extracted re-quirements as a reading aid, with the original teacher prompt taking precedence. 

W7. 

W7: Follow the applicable workflow, not a rigid restarted script. Acknowledge the student’s cur-rent question and substantive contribution. Di-rect in-domain clarification receives a supported answer now, within the original guardrails; do not gate it behind an irrelevant name, title or menu. An already-supplied observation satisfies its corresponding elicitation step: give specific feedback/vocabulary, then advance one step. Do not perform work that the original workflow as-signs to the learner. When the original prompt re-quires a post-teaching comprehension check, in-clude one concrete explain/predict/attempt ques-tion, not merely an invitation to continue. If a prior check was bypassed, connect or adapt it to the current topic after answering, rather than dropping it or refusing the new question. Pre-serve required numbered options and other ex-plicit formats where applicable. Visible prompt material counts as available material even when attachments are absent. 

W6. 

W6: Only one new piece of learner information or one small learner action per reply. Do not dis-guise multiple requests under one question mark. Track a necessary unanswered question; if the learner bypasses it, simplify or adapt it instead of piling on more questions. Do not assume that a yes answered an ambiguous multi-part request. Avoid repeating an optional intake request that is not needed to help with the current question. 

P3. 

P3: Fit the actual task. Give the needed answer or useful next step first. For broad multi-topic requests, give a compact roadmap and a substan-tive first unit, then continue interactively; aim for about 180–300 words unless the original prompt specifies less. Honor genuinely necessary de-tail for an allowed worked sample. Omit redun-dant recaps and unsolicited solved examples, but do not truncate reasoning or claim omitted con-tent was delivered. Plain-language clarity mat-ters more than making a narrow answer artifi-cially Socratic. 

W9. 

W9: Establish the bot’s actual role, scope and in-teraction style briefly at session opening, not only its fictional persona. Do not repeat the introduc-tion after it is established.

D Prolific Study Details 

Recruitment. We recruited 200 of the 9,242 Pro-lific participants who met our screening criteria: U.S.-based students currently enrolled in college, including universities, community colleges, and associate degree programs. The study was listed with an estimated duration of 5 minutes and a base payment of $3 ($36/hour at the estimated dura-tion). The task description shown to participants was: 

We are a research team from [Anonymized Insti-tution] studying AI in education. We’re looking for college students to try out educational chat-bots built by educators from a range of back-grounds. Your usage data will help us understand how well these bots work in realistic educational settings before they are deployed to real class-rooms. 

Procedure: In this study, you will follow the link to: (1) (1 minute suggested) Read a one-sentence scenario card describing the chatbot you’ve been assigned. (2) ( >3 minutes required) Take on the role of the bot’s intended user described in the scenario and chat with the bot through the pro-vided link for at least 3 minutes. 

Compensation: After you finish, a member of our team will review the chat log and approve your submission on Prolific. You will receive $3 USD for engaging with the bot for at least 3 min-utes. Participants who engage deeply with the conversation will receive an additional up to $3 bonus. 

Task interface. The task had two steps (Fig-ure 5). In Step 1 (left), participants saw a sce-nario card for their assigned chatbot: an illustra-tion, the school, course, subject, grade level, and chatbot purpose, along with the course material attached to the chatbot (e.g., a syllabus), which they were required to open. They then completed a three-blank comprehension check that restated the scenario from drop-down options (e.g., “You are a student using the bot to ask syllabus ques-tions in a healthcare course”); an incorrect answer prompted them to re-read the scenario, and a cor-rect answer unlocked Step 2. In Step 2 (right), participants opened the assigned chatbot in an em-bedded Playlab chat window and were instructed to act as the learner in the scenario for at least 3 minutes. The interface displayed three completion criteria: at least 3 minutes of engagement time, at least two messages sent, and student-like behavior that fits the learner in the scenario, whether good (asking for a hint) or bad (asking for a direct an-swer). Control and harness chatbots used the same scenario cards, interface, and chat window, differ-ing only in how responses were generated. 17 ⌄⚐

Control rates versus corpus prevalence. Some control rates differ from corpus prevalence, no-tably expectation setup (W9: 33.4% in the cor-pus vs. 6.9% in control) and cognitive overload (P3: 27.1% vs. 39.7%). The two summaries cover different bot samples and use different weighting: corpus rates pool conversations across deployed bots, while study rates give equal weight to the ten most active bots. Participants also received scenario cards and were required to engage for at least three minutes. Role-playing may change how participants begin conversations and what they ask, while the duration requirement may create more opportunities for overload to occur. These factors offer possible explanations for the differ-ences. Because both conditions share bots, sce-narios, task requirements, and the participant pool, and participants are randomly assigned to condi-tions, these corpus–control differences do not af-fect the within-study comparison. 19
