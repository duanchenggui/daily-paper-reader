Abstract 

What happens after a learner makes an error, and how does the instructor’s response shape the next turn? Investigating these exchanges across classrooms requires context-sensitive annotation that is difficult to scale manually. We introduce SLA-LENS, an end-to-end LLM-assisted framework that connects classroom transcripts to structured pedagogical annota-tions and evidence-linked descriptive analyt-ics. Grounded in interactionist Second Lan-guage Acquisition (SLA) theory, its annotation schema captures second language learner er-rors, pedagogical functions, and uptake or re-pair while accounting for speaker roles, conver-sational context, and hierarchical labels. We apply the framework to 40 authentic online L2 Spanish classroom transcripts comprising over 21,000 sentence-level instances. We evaluate proprietary and open-weight LLMs, including task-specific QLoRA adaptation, using expert-reviewed gold annotations, held-out silver data, and metrics sensitive to different types of an-notation error. A deterministic reporting layer generates reproducible visualizations of partic-ipation, feedback, learner errors, uptake, and temporal interaction patterns, linked to support-ing transcript evidence. By making these pat-terns accessible and inspectable, SLA-LENS supports systematic classroom discourse re-search, pedagogical reflection, and curriculum design 1.

2 Related Work 

Interactionist SLA and the Computational Scal-ability Gap Interactionist SLA research has es-tablished the importance of negotiation of mean-ing, corrective feedback, modified output, and learner uptake in language development (Long, 1996; Swain, 2005; Gass and Mackey, 2015; Li, 2010). Studies have examined how feedback strate-gies influence learner responses and acquisition (Ellis et al., 2006; Sheen, 2010). Lyster and Ranta 2(1997) provides an influential framework linking learner production, instructor feedback, uptake, and repair, distinguishing strategies such as explicit cor-rection, recasts, clarification requests, metalinguis-tic feedback, and elicitation. Analyzing these phe-nomena requires context-sensitive discourse cod-ing. Conversation-analytic approaches provide de-tailed accounts of classroom interaction (Gardner, 2019), while quantitative observational studies rely on manually identifying and classifying relevant exchanges. Such events can be relatively sparse: across Lyster and Ranta (1997) and Panova and Lyster (2002), approximately 39 feedback moves, 20 uptake instances, and nine repairs were identi-fied per hour on average. Similarly, Loewen (2004) reported approximately 29 focus-on-form episodes per hour. These findings illustrate the substantial annotation effort required to examine interactional patterns across larger classroom corpora. 

Computational and LLM-Based Approaches to SLA Computational research has supported learner corpora, grammatical-error analysis, au-tomated assessment, and feedback generation (McEnery et al., 2019), including identification of discourse features in L2 writing (Eguchi and Kyle, 2024). Educational NLP has also addressed instructional discourse analysis (Demszky and Liu, 2023), computational teacher-learner feedback in-teractions (Castro-Garcia and Kordjamshidi, 2025), and adaptive language-learning support (Beaudin et al., 2007; Turapova et al., 2025). However, these approaches primarily examine written text, instructional support, or controlled interactions rather than jointly assessing multiple SLA phe-nomena in naturally occurring classroom conver-sations. Recent work has increasingly investigated LLMs as providers of corrective feedback, expla-nations, and conversations (Crusan and Mohebbi, 2026; Dong, 2026; Pitychoutis and Spathopoulou, 2026; M. Kamelabad et al., 2026). SLA-LENS instead uses LLMs to analyze existing human in-teraction, identifying context-dependent classroom events for subsequent quantitative analysis. This introduces challenges involving diarization, tran-scription noise, rare labels, and overlapping com-municative functions.

3 SLA-LENS: Transcript-to-Analytics Framework 

SLA-LENS is an end-to-end framework that trans-forms L2 classroom transcripts into structured SLA annotations and evidence-linked descriptive inter-actional analytics. As illustrated in Fig 1, the frame-work comprises three stages: transcript structuring, contextual LLM annotation, and deterministic ag-gregation and reporting. It produces two comple-mentary outputs: an inspectable annotated tran-script and a standalone analytical report supporting descriptive analysis of classroom interaction. 

3.1 Classroom Data and Transcript Representation

Classroom sessions are compar-atively instructor-led, with explicit learning tasks and instructional feedback, whereas tutoring ses-sions emphasize conversational practice and more open-ended learner production. 3.2 CSLA Annotation Schema 

The CSLA annotation schema operationalizes SLA-relevant interactional phenomena into structured labels suitable for human and LLM annotation. Developed with SLA expertise, it was iteratively refined through analysis of authentic classroom transcripts and annotation disagreements. Rather than exhaustively describing classroom discourse, the schema captures observable pedagogical and interactional events that support reproducible de-scriptive analysis. Table 2 summarizes the label families, with detailed definitions, decision rules, and examples provided in Appendix A. 

Two annotation dimensions. The schema sep-arates classroom events from learner errors . The 

labels dimension captures communicative func-tions such as prompts, feedback, learner responses, and classroom management, while the indepen-dent error_labels dimension identifies observ-able learner-language errors. Thus, a learner re-sponse may simultaneously receive PR (learner Prompt Response) and Er:G (Grammatical Error). Error annotations are restricted to learner produc-tion and exclude apparent errors attributable to tran-scription artifacts. 

Context and communicative function. Labels represent an utterance’s interactional function 4rather than its surface form. For example, repite 

(‘repeat’) may constitute an instructional prompt when initiating a task or corrective feedback when responding to erroneous learner production. Simi-larly, learner production following an instructional prompt receives PR , whereas responses within feed-back sequences belong to the R family. These dis-tinctions require local conversational context even though annotations are assigned at the sentence-row level. 

Priority and role constraints. Explicit priority rules resolve competing interpretations: Feedback takes precedence over other instructor functions when responding to learner production, while Up-take/Repair takes precedence over other learner functions within feedback sequences. Thus, F > 

other instructor functions and R > other learner functions. Speaker-role constraints further restrict label assignments, preventing instructor-specific la-bels on learner rows and vice versa. These rules guide annotation and subsequent evaluation. 

Hierarchy and multi-label events. Several la-bels belong to hierarchical families, such as F:R 

(Recast) and F:EC (Explicit Correction) within Feedback. This structure supports evaluation that distinguishes within-family confusion from errors across interactional functions. Multiple labels are assigned only when a row contains independently identifiable events, with label order reflecting their occurrence. For example, Muy bien, ahora intén-talo otra vez receives F:Pr, F:EL . Multi-label annotations account for 3.3% of corpus rows. 

Learner errors. The error taxonomy distin-guishes single grammatical ( Er:G ), single lexical (Er:L ), and multiple errors ( Er:M ), alongside sub-stantially English production when Spanish is con-textually expected ( Er:EU ). English use is not au-tomatically erroneous, particularly when permitted by the instructional context or used for classroom management (e.g., We can’t hear you, Professor ). Punctuation, formatting, and identifiable ASR arti-facts are excluded. 

3.3 End-to-End Analysis Pipeline 

SLA-LENS transforms input transcripts into the structured representation described in Section 3.1, preserving sentence-level timestamps, speaker identities, and turn information while normaliz-ing speaker roles as instructor or learner . The annotation engine processes transcript rows with surrounding conversational context and the CSLA annotation guidelines. For each target row, the LLM predicts classroom-event labels and learner 

error_labels , alongside confidence and a brief rationale. Structured-output validation checks label inventories, speaker-role constraints, and sentence alignment before annotations are merged with the original transcript. GPT-4.1 serves as the reference annotation backend, while open-weight alternatives are evaluated in Section 4.1. Implementation and prompting details are provided in Appendix B. The annotated transcript serves both as an in-dependent research artifact and as input to SLA-LENS’s deterministic reporting layer. This layer computes descriptive interactional measures, in-cluding participant contributions, prompt and feed-back distributions, learner-error patterns, uptake/re-pair outcomes, and their variation across lesson time. Explicit proximity-based rules additionally identify candidate associations between prompts and responses, learner errors and feedback, and feedback and subsequent uptake. All measures are linked to supporting sentence identifiers, times-tamps, and transcript excerpts, allowing users to inspect the underlying evidence. SLA-LENS pro-duces two complementary outputs: an exportable annotated CSV and a standalone analytical report containing visualizations, participant-level sum-maries, and an annotation glossary. Table 3 sum-marizes the supported measures, while Section 6 describes the reporting interface. 

4 Annotation Engine Evaluation 

The reliability of SLA-LENS’s descriptive analyt-ics depends on the quality of its underlying anno-tations. We therefore evaluate the contextual an-notation engine independently of the deterministic reporting layer, examining both annotation agree-ment with expert judgments and the feasibility of replacing the proprietary backend with locally de-ployable open-weight models.

4.3 Evaluation Metrics 

The CSLA schema is hierarchical, speaker-role-dependent, and occasionally multi-label. Strict exact-match accuracy therefore treats errors of different severity equally. We complement exact agreement with a hierarchical similarity score and targeted detection metrics for interactional phenom-ena central to SLA-LENS reporting. 

Hard Accuracy. Hard Accuracy measures the proportion of rows whose predicted classroom-event labels exactly match the reference annota-tions. For multi-label rows, both label identity and ordering must agree. 

Hierarchical Soft Score. We compute a row-level Soft Score that assigns partial credit accord-ing to the CSLA label hierarchy (Table 4) Exact matches receive full credit, within-family subtype errors receive partial credit, and predictions from another family compatible with the speaker role re-ceive lower credit. Role-incompatible predictions receive no credit. Multi-label cases additionally account for partially recovered labels, unsupported predictions, and incorrect label ordering. The final score is averaged across rows and scaled to 0-100. 

Diagnostic-Sensitive Evaluation. We separately report detection F1 for three interactional phenom-ena important to downstream analysis. Feedback F1 measures detection of any F:* subtype, while 

Uptake/Repair F1 evaluates the learner-response family ( R:S , R:IR , R:Ack , R:NR ). Error Detection F1 measures whether a learner row contains any error annotation, independently of its subtype. 5 Annotation Results 

We evaluate the SLA-LENS annotation engine against an expert-reviewed gold transcript and five held-out silver transcripts using the metrics in Sec-tion 4.3. Gold evaluation measures agreement with human annotations, while silver evaluation mea-sures agreement with GPT-generated labels. We compare GPT-4.1 with Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Gemma 4 12B, alongside Qwen3-8B adapted using quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA). 

5.1 Overall Performance 

Tables 5 and 6 summarize annotation performance against expert and silver references, respectively. On the gold set, GPT-4.1 achieves 76.72% Hard Accuracy and a hierarchical Soft Score of 84.12. Feedback detection reaches an F1 of 87.57%, while uptake/repair detection is lower at 76.64%. Er-ror detection achieves 81.40% F1, compared with 96.63% Error Hard Accuracy, reflecting the preva-lence of error-free rows. The open-weight compar-ison examines whether smaller, locally deployable models can recover the CSLA annotation structure and whether task-specific adaptation improves their performance. On the gold set, QLoRA adaptation improves Qwen3-8B Hard Accuracy from 62.40% to 69.80% (+7.40 percentage points) and Feedback F1 from 73.50% to 81.20% (+7.70 points). The adapted model also shows higher agreement with the GPT-generated silver references. 

5.2 Diagnostic-Critical Label Performance 

Overall accuracy can obscure performance on less frequent but analytically important events. We therefore separately evaluate Feedback, Uptake/Re-pair, and learner-error detection, which directly in-fluence SLA-LENS’s descriptive interactional mea-sures. Family-level F1 assesses event identification. GPT-4.1 identifies Feedback more reliably than Uptake/Repair, suggesting that learner-response an-notation remains a particularly challenging compo-nent. Such differences matter downstream: missed feedback can distort feedback distributions, while missed uptake can underrepresent learner responses following correction. 

5.3 Annotation Error Analysis 

Qualitative inspection of model–expert disagree-ments reveals challenges involving contextual in-terpretation, transcription artifacts, and annotation ambiguity. In one example, GPT classified muy bien (‘very good’) as praise, whereas all three adju-dicators classified it as a conversational flow marker (CoM): the teacher was introducing the first instruc-tional question rather than evaluating learner pro-duction. All three adjudicators also assigned Un-known to Sounds Avocado , identifying it as a tran-scription artifact, while GPT assigned a discourse category. Other cases reflect uncertainty in the ref-erence interpretation. Two adjudicators classified 

sí (‘yes’) as CoM, while the third interpreted it as the teacher’s response to an implicit learner prompt. These distinctions matter for downstream report-ing: misclassifying a flow marker as praise inflates praise counts, while classifying a transcription arti-fact introduces an unsupported event. Adjudicator 7disagreement also shows that some utterances per-mit multiple plausible interpretations. Together, these cases support reporting complementary eval-uation measures and retaining transcript evidence for human inspection.

6 Interactional Analytics and Reporting 

Beyond structured annotation, SLA-LENS gen-erates a standalone analytical report for re-searchers, educators, and other practitioners study-ing language-learning interaction. Constructed de-terministically from the annotated transcript with-out additional LLM-generated interpretation, the report provides reproducible descriptive summaries in HTML and PDF formats. 

6.1 Interactional Measures 

SLA-LENS organizes its analyses into three dimen-sions: (i) participation, including instructor-learner contributions and individual learner production; (ii) pedagogical events, including prompts, feed-back strategies, learner errors, and uptake/repair outcomes; and (iii) interactional sequences, iden-tifying candidate associations between learner er-rors, instructor feedback, and subsequent responses. The report presents frequency distributions, learner-level comparisons, normalized error incidence, and temporal visualizations. Sequence associations fol-low explicit conversational proximity rules and are not treated as verified pedagogical exchanges. 

6.2 Evidence-Grounded Analytical Report 

The standalone report presents session-level sum-mary cards showing transcript size, instructor and learner contributions, and counts of feedback, er-rors, and uptake, alongside participant information and data-quality indicators. Learner-level visual-izations compare participation, approximate word production, normalized error incidence, and error-category distributions across speakers. Additional views display pedagogical-event frequencies, les-son timelines, and candidate associations between errors, feedback, and learner responses. To sup-port interpretation, the report includes a compact CSLA label glossary and an evidence index linking annotations to sentence IDs, timestamps, speakers, and transcript excerpts. These descriptive outputs highlight patterns for investigation rather than es-tablishing learner proficiency or instructional ef-fectiveness. Appendix E presents screenshots of the report interface and learner-level analyses; the complete report is available in the supplementary repository. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduced SLA-LENS , an end-to-end frame-work that moves beyond transcription to make second-language classroom interaction computa-tionally observable and interpretable. Combin-ing a theory-informed annotation schema, con-textual LLM annotation, and deterministic report-ing, SLA-LENS transforms classroom transcripts into structured, evidence-linked descriptive ana-lytics. Our evaluation investigates the reliability of proprietary and open-weight annotation mod-els, including task-specific adaptation, while the reporting layer demonstrates how learner errors, feedback, uptake, participation, and temporal pat-terns can be explored at scale. Beyond support-ing pedagogical reflection, SLA-LENS provides a reproducible foundation for corpus-based SLA research and computational humanities. Future work will extend the framework toward longitu-dinal and cross-classroom comparisons, integra-tion of learner proficiency and sociolinguistic meta-data, and statistical modeling of interactional pat-terns and language development. By connecting automated discourse annotation with transparent analytical reporting, SLA-LENS offers a step to-ward more accessible, scalable, and empirically grounded analysis of language-learning interaction. The resulting evidence can help teachers reflect on 8their pedagogical practices and inform curriculum designers’ decisions about classroom activities and instructional support.

Limitations 

SLA-LENS is currently developed and evaluated on English–Spanish instructional interaction, limit-ing its demonstrated generalizability to other lan-guage pairs, proficiency levels, and educational settings. The corpus does not systematically in-corporate learner proficiency, demographic or so-ciolinguistic metadata, or longitudinal learner tra-jectories, preventing controlled comparisons across learner groups and direct investigation of language development. Furthermore, evaluation relies on a small subset of expert-reviewed gold transcript and LLM-generated silver annotations, which may introduce annotation bias and limit the reliability of broader performance estimates. The framework operates on sentence-level transcripts and does not explicitly identify complete interactional episodes or source-target relationships among learner errors, feedback, and repair. Sequence-based associations therefore rely on proximity heuristics rather than verified discourse links. Transcription and speaker-attribution errors may also propagate into annota-tion and reporting. Finally, the current analytics are descriptive and do not establish proficiency, learning gains, causal effects, or instructional effec-tiveness; the practical utility of the reports has not yet been validated through systematic user studies. 

Ethical Considerations 

SLA-LENS analyzes classroom interactions involv-ing human participants and therefore requires ap-propriate consent, privacy safeguards, and responsi-ble data handling. The study received institutional IRB approval, and all participating instructors and learners provided informed consent for recording and research use. Protecting participant anonymity is a priority throughout the workflow, including transcription, annotation, data analysis, aggrega-tion, and reporting. LLM-generated annotations may misclassify non-standard learner language, code-switching, or transcription artifacts as errors, potentially introducing biases into participant-level summaries. Accordingly, SLA-LENS provides de-scriptive, evidence-linked analyses rather than auto-mated judgments of learner proficiency or instruc-tional effectiveness, and findings should be inter-preted with human oversight. AI-assisted writing tools (e.g., ChatGPT and Grammarly) were used solely for grammatical refinement and clarity of presentation.

10 A CSLA Annotation Guidelines 

This appendix summarizes the operational guide-lines used to produce CSLA annotations in SLA-LENS. The schema contains two independent anno-tation dimensions: labels , representing classroom interaction events, and error_labels , represent-ing observable learner-language errors. The full annotation manual provides additional examples and edge cases. 

A.1 General Principles 

Annotations represent the communicative function 

of an utterance in conversational context rather than its grammatical form. Only phenomena supported by the available transcript are annotated. A single classroom-event label is preferred unless a row con-tains multiple independently observable events, in which case labels follow their temporal order. Two priority rules resolve common ambiguities: 

Instructor: F > other instructor functions. 

Learner: R > other learner functions. Thus, instructor utterances responding to learner production are classified as Feedback when appro-priate, even when their surface form resembles a prompt. Likewise, learner responses within feed-back sequences are classified as Uptake/Repair rather than ordinary prompt responses. 

Unknown Function. UNK is assigned when an utterance’s communicative function cannot be de-termined reliably from the available context. This includes severely fragmented or unintelligible tran-script segments and transcription artifacts that do not support a defensible interactional interpreta-tion. UNK is preferred over speculative assignment of another classroom-event label.

B SLA-LENS Annotation Pipeline Details 

B.1 Transcript Processing 

The annotation pipeline accepts .vtt , .txt , and 

.docx transcript files. Timestamped cues are parsed into speaker blocks and segmented into sentence-level rows using sentence-final punctu-ation and Spanish inverted punctuation. Each row retains its sentence ID, turn number, source turn number, timestamps, speaker identity, and utter-ance text. Sentences derived from the same source cue retain its timestamps. The annotation engine processes 12 target rows per batch, accompanied by up to five preceding and two following rows as conversational context. Only target rows receive annotations. The prompt combines the CSLA an-notation guidelines, role-specific constraints, prior-ity rules, and structured output requirements. The current LLM implementations use temperature 0, 

top_p =1, and a maximum of 4,000 completion to-kens. The complete prompts and JSON schema are provided in the supplementary repository.

The corpus contains 40 GPT-annotated classroom transcripts comprising 21,835 sentence-level rows. C.3 QLoRA Fine-Tuning 

We adapted Qwen3-8B using Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) on the GPT-annotated CSLA training corpus. The base model is loaded us-ing 4-bit NF4 quantization with bfloat16 compu-tation. Low-rank adapters are applied to the atten-tion and feed-forward projection modules ( q_proj ,

k_proj , v_proj , o_proj , gate_proj , up_proj ,and down_proj ), with rank r = 16 , scaling param-eter α = 32 , and dropout 0.05. Fine-tuning uses a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4, the paged 8-bit AdamW optimizer, and a cosine learning-rate schedule with a 3% warmup ratio. It is trained for three epochs with a per-device batch size of 2 and gradient ac-cumulation of 8, yielding an effective batch size of 16 on one GPU. The maximum sequence length is 8,192 tokens. Training is done on a single NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPU using Hugging Face Transform-ers, PEFT, TRL, and bitsandbytes.

Candidate Interaction Associations. SLA-LENS identifies candidate event associations using deterministic row-proximity rules. For each source event, the reporting algorithm searches forward for the first eligible target event within four intervening transcript rows, stopping if another source event occurs first. Three association types are considered: learner error to instructor corrective feedback, instructor feedback to learner uptake, and instructor prompt to learner prompt response. These associations are descriptive proximity heuristics and do not establish verified interactional episodes or causal relationships.

Each reported annotation and candidate interaction association can be traced to its supporting transcript rows through sentence identifiers, timestamps, speaker information, and original utterance text. The generated evidence index allows users to inspect these annotations in their conversational context. Examples and the complete report are provided in the supplementary repository.
