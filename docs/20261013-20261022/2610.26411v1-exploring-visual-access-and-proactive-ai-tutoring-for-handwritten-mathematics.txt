## Abstract 

Handwritten work captures mathematical reasoning that learners may not express in typed or voice-dictated messages. We built a multimodal AI tutoring platform in which a phone camera gives the tutor a live view of learners’ work on paper. In a randomized online study, 117 adults solved calculus problems with Text-Reactive, Visual-Reactive, or Visual-Proactive assistance. Analysis of 2,140 tutor messages showed that visual access shifted assistance toward checking ongoing work, while proactivity enhanced er-ror identification and directive hints. Learners valued feedback grounded in their writing; those who received proactive help rated it as supportive, and about a third wanted it more often. These find-ings inform when tutors should intervene and what they should ad-dress. We release the platform, prompts, and instruments, together with 255,984 timestamped images of handwritten work linked to tutoring conversations, intervention decisions, and learning out-comes, to support multimodal tutoring systems that preserve the af-fordances of handwritten work while providing adaptive feedback.

## 1 Introduction 

Effective tutoring adapts to learner problem-solving behavior. A human tutor follows a learner’s solution as it develops, notices an error at the step where it occurs, and decides moment by moment whether to intervene or let the learner continue [18, 63, 85]. Van-Lehn [88] found that computer-based tutoring with feedback and scaffolding at the level of individual steps was nearly as effective as human tutoring and more effective than answer-level feedback. Meta-analyses show that intelligent tutoring systems built on this principle improve learning outcomes [48, 56, 82]. Such step-level support requires that the tutor can see what the learner is doing. Step-based tutoring systems can follow intermediate steps be-cause learners enter them through the system’s interface. Much mathematical work, however, happens on paper, through diagrams, symbolic expressions, and revised intermediate steps. Handwriting input lets learners record equations faster and with fewer errors than typing [5], and entering work through a keyboard changes how learners work through problems [21]. Borchers et al. [12] call for combining the flexibility of paper practice with the support of tutoring systems. Chat-based AI tutors see only what learners de-scribe or separately share, and the learners who most need help are often the least able to recognize and articulate that need [2]. How can an AI tutor engage with this work without requiring learners to reproduce it in another interface? Research on collaborative physical tasks shows what a shared view of the work supplies. When a helper can see the worker’s task space, pairs need fewer words to establish reference, actions can stand in for spoken confirmations, and the helper can monitor progress and offer help at the right moment [25, 26, 46]. The view serves both grounding and awareness of the task state [27]. In stepwise mathematical problem solving, a useful hint depends on the step the learner is on now. When learners work on paper, a view of the page gives the tutor access to intermediate expressions and revisions that may never appear in the conversation. Earlier systems connected handwritten work to tutoring through instrumented pens, tablets, or interactive whiteboards [5, 21, 50, 51, 54], which require dedicated hardware and move writing away from the learner’s usual paper. Current multimodal AI is increasingly able to read handwritten expressions and diagrams from camera images of paper work and reason about them in conversation [9, 65, 94]. This makes it practical to support work on ordinary paper with a phone and laptop that learners already own. We built a platform in which a phone camera, supported above ordinary paper, gives a multimodal AI tutor a live view of the learner’s handwritten work while a laptop presents problems and chat (Figure 1A). Establishing a stable, readable view is a practical demand on the learner, and we report how participants managed it. A live view can change help in two ways. When a learner asks for help, the tutor can read the written step instead of requiring the learner to retype an expression or describe a diagram [19]. Be-tween requests, the view supports awareness of the developing work, providing a basis for proactivity : initiating help in response to work the learner has not described. Reactive help depends on the learner recognizing a difficulty and formulating a request [2]. Proactive help can reach a learner before a request, but may in-terrupt productive work or supply a step the learner could have developed independently [35, 44]. ## 2 Related Work 2.1 Seeing the Work: Shared Visual Context in Tutoring and Collaboration 

Studies of human tutors show how much of their effectiveness rests on watching the work. Merrill et al. [63] concluded that human tutors let students do as much of the work as possible while inter-vening on errors quickly enough to prevent floundering. Compared with the tutoring systems of the time, they left more of the error recovery to the student. VanLehn et al. [89] found that learning dur-ing tutoring concentrated at impasses, the moments when a student got stuck, made an error, or acted with uncertainty. Noticing an impasse depends on observing the work as it develops. Scaffolding research reaches the same conclusion from the tutor’s side: contin-gent support requires continuous diagnosis of what the learner has just done [86, 92]. Research on shared visual space in collaboration shows what a view of the work supplies and what it costs. Fussell et al. [25] found that pairs repairing a bicycle side by side worked about a quarter faster than remote pairs and referred to parts with far fewer words, but a head-mounted video link did not recover that advan-tage. In a follow-up, a view of the workspace let remote helpers see when the worker was stuck or ready for the next step and time their instructions accordingly, although keeping the camera on the relevant part took effort of its own [46]. Gergle et al. [27] distin-guished two functions of a shared view, grounding what is said and maintaining awareness of the task state, and showed that delay and a restricted field of view reduced these benefits. Visible actions could also replace spoken confirmations [26]. These findings come from human pairs. Whether an AI system can use a live view in the same way is a newer question. Bohus et al. [11] observed people working through physical tasks with a head-worn multimodal assistant and found that most of their ques-tions sought to ground its instructions in the objects, states, and actions in front of them; they identified tracking the user’s actions and deciding when to intervene as open problems. Shaikh et al. [78] showed that language models perform the grounding acts human interlocutors use, such as clarification and acknowledgment, far less often than people do. In mathematics, vision-language models underperformed on struggling students’ handwritten work and misdiagnosed their errors, even when the images were redrawn cleanly [9, 55, 65]. Learners, in turn, must judge whether the model has read their work correctly; Nguyen et al. [66] described how novices and code models misread each other and how many at-tempts novices spent repairing prompts when their intent had been misread. These studies establish that a shared view helps human helpers and that current models can use one, imperfectly. Our Text-Reactive and Visual-Reactive conditions isolate what a view of ordinary paper adds while learners solve problems, and Section 5.2 reports how it changed what learners asked and what the tutor did. 

## 2.2 Initiating Help: Proactive Assistance and Learner Control 

Whether a tutor should wait to be asked is an old question in learning environments, because help seeking is itself demanding. A review by Aleven et al. [2] concluded that students often fail to seek help when they need it, or use help in ways that avoid thinking, such as requesting hints until the answer appears [7]. Tutoring systems have therefore tried to detect need from behavior, predicting unproductive struggle from interaction logs [60] or dis-engagement from gaze [22], and to teach better help seeking [77]. Generative AI makes asking more demanding still: learners must know what to ask and how to judge what comes back [84], and in a semester-long deployment of a guard-railed programming assistant, requests ranged from conceptual questions to attempts to obtain the solution [41]. Intervening too readily has its own cost. The assistance dilemma [44] frames intervention as a balance between giving and withholding help, each with costs, and research on pro-ductive failure [39] suggested that support arriving before learners have struggled can remove the opportunity that produces learning. Mixed-initiative research frames the decision to intervene as one made under uncertainty about the user’s goals and the cost of a poorly timed interruption [35]. Interruptions are less disruptive at task boundaries [1, 37], and whether they arrive immediately, at a negotiated moment, or on a schedule changes their cost [62]. Recent proactive assistants built on large language models (LLMs) apply these ideas by observing ongoing work and deciding when to speak. In programming, Codellaborator’s inactivity-triggered suggestions were sometimes experienced as interruptions because they arrived while participants were still thinking [75]. Chen et al. [17] found that an assistant that chose when to interject from the editor state improved productivity over a reactive one. Par-ticipants preferred that assistant only when its interjections were spaced out and limited. Proactive assistants outside programming decide when to intervene from what they hear or see, such as the user’s speech [83] or a head-worn camera [8, 52, 76], and recent work adapts the initiation policy to individual preferences [91]. In learning, proactive mentoring around an instructional video in-creased interaction [68], and Li et al. [53] examined the tension Kotalwar et al. 

between timely intervention by process-aware tutors and learners’ boundaries on what may be observed. These systems observe code, speech, or camera streams, mostly in short tasks with small samples, and only Otmani et al. [68] mea-sured learning. Our Visual-Proactive condition adds initiative to a tutor that can see the paper. We report what it chose to ad-dress (Sections 5.2.2–5.2.3), whether learners found its messages interrupting (Section 5.3), and learning outcomes alongside both (Section 5.1.3). 

## 2.3 AI Tutoring for Mathematics with Handwritten Work 

This work builds on the tradition of step-level tutoring. VanLehn’s “inner loop” gives feedback and hints on each step within a prob-lem [87], and tutorial dialogue systems build explanations through conversation [31]. Both require the learner’s steps to be visible to the system, so intelligent tutors have long required work to be en-tered through structured interfaces [28]. LLM-based tutors relax the interface but keep the requirement. Dialogue corpora and tutoring benchmarks are organized around typed student turns [58, 59], and efforts to align model responses with pedagogy address what the tutor says, not what it can see [20, 61, 81]. Controlled evaluations of LLM tutoring report mixed outcomes. AI tutoring outperformed in-class active learning in a physics course [43]. In a UK classroom trial, a pedagogically tuned model supervised by human tutors supported learning with no detected difference from the tutors themselves [49]. Mathematics studies found gains from model-generated help com-parable to human-authored hints [71] and from a messaging-based tutor in Ghanaian schools [34]. Unrestricted access to a generative model, however, harmed high-school mathematics learning [10], and in programming, novices who were already succeeding used generative AI to accelerate while struggling novices were left with an illusion of competence [74]. Studies of AI-supported learning also distinguish assisted performance from later unaided perfor-mance [40], a distinction our design adopts. The typed-input requirement carries a particular cost in mathe-matics. Students expressed and solved problems differently by hand than by keyboard [4, 5]. Systems that tutor handwritten work have therefore built dedicated input surfaces [21, 50, 51, 54]. Photo-based math apps go the other way: they read a single picture of a problem, and submission-speed data suggest students increasingly use them to get answers quickly [80]. Vision-language models can now read handwritten mathematics [29, 65], including phone photographs of paper work [9], and reason over handwritten solution steps in tutoring settings [94], though their error rates remain substantial. This makes a view of ordinary paper a plausible, if imperfect, input for a tutor. We are not aware of work testing what a view of ordinary paper does for tutoring. Studies of handwriting input compared input modalities rather than what the tutor could see [4, 21]. Handwrit-ing tutors are described as systems or compared on the format of their feedback, without a condition that lacks the view and rarely with learning outcomes. LLM tutoring trials measure learning, but with typed input. We compare a tutor with and without a view of ordinary paper and, with the view, with and without initiative, among 117 adults, and report learning gains alongside a content analysis of what learners asked and what the tutor did (Section 5). 

## 3 Study Platform and Assistance Conditions 

We designed a common paper-and-phone platform to compare visual access and proactive assistance while keeping the interface, task materials, and tutoring policy consistent across conditions (Figure 1). 

## 3.1 Platform Setup 

We developed a guided browser-based setup so learners could estab-lish a readable paper view with their own devices, without installing an app. Learners scan a QR code on the laptop with their phone, which opens the phone’s camera page. Learners allow camera ac-cess and support the phone above their paper, leaving both hands available for writing. A laptop preview helps them check the view. ## 3.2 Solving Problems with the Tutor 

The laptop presents the problem, a final-answer field, and tutor chat. Learners work on paper and request help by typing or voice dictation. After a worked solution becomes available, they can review it with the tutor before proceeding to the next problem, which starts a new conversation. ## 3.3 Assistance Conditions 

We used GPT-5.6 Sol as a multimodal AI tutor. All conditions record paper views and share task materials, the chat interface, and tu-toring policy. The tutor receives the current problem, a private reference solution, the problem’s conversation, and whether the learner is still attempting the problem or reviewing its worked solution. Its access to paper views and permission to initiate help vary: 

• Text-Reactive responds to learner messages using the problem context and conversation, without access to the paper view. 

• Visual-Reactive additionally receives the current paper view with each learner message, allowing requests to refer to work not entered in chat. It responds only to learner messages. 

• Visual-Proactive uses the same requested-help policy as Visual-Reactive, but also observes paper views over time and can initiate help based on ongoing work. Throughout the paper, requested replies are the tutor’s answers to learner messages, and proactive messages are messages the tutor initiates. Only Visual-Proactive sends proactive messages. ## 3.4 Tutoring Policies 

Shared pedagogical policy. System prompts encode a shared ped-agogical policy, condition-specific requested-help instructions, and a proactive-decision policy. The application controls which con-ditions receive paper views and when the worked solution is re-leased. Appendix A.2 gives further detail and points to the complete prompts and source code in the project repository. The policy draws on step-level tutoring [87], contingent scaf-folding [85], and explanatory feedback [79]. It instructs the tutor to build on the learner’s own approach, notation, and step order whenever these are mathematically valid, explain the reasoning behind suggested steps, and leave meaningful work to the learner. The reference solution shows one correct path, and the tutor may depart from it. Learners can request help at any point. To balance assistance and learner effort [44], when a learner repeatedly asks for the next step without trying it, the tutor is instructed to help them attempt the current step instead. Learners expressing difficulty or requesting a demonstration can receive a worked intermediate step, leaving the next step to them. Evidence that a learner has carried out a step can come from the paper view, from chat, or from the learner’s own report, depending on condition; the tutor does not require learners to type out their work or confirm each step. During attempts, the tutor is instructed to withhold the final answer and, beyond such a worked intermediate step, avoid com-pleting steps for the learner. It can check proposed answers and help repair incorrect reasoning. Once the worked solution is re-vealed, the tutor can explain the solution in full. These boundaries apply in every condition. The reference solution supports the tu-tor’s correctness checks but does not guarantee that they are right, so we checked the mathematical correctness of a sample of tutor messages (Appendix D.4). Proactive intervention. A tutor that can see the work can inter-vene contingently, and must then decide when an observation calls for a message. The Visual-Proactive design addresses that de-cision directly. At regular intervals during an attempt, the tutor receives the latest paper view together with earlier views and the history of delivered help, and chooses one of three actions: repair a clear, consequential error; support a transition when a valid partial approach has stalled at the same unresolved step; or wait. Waiting is the default. The tutor waits when the learner’s writing is still progressing between views, when it has just given help that the learner has not yet had time to use, or when the page is blank or does not show the current problem. A pause alone is not treated as evidence that a learner is stuck [35]. The two intervening actions differ in the evidence they require and in what they deliver. A repair needs a single observation of a consequential error. It identifies the fault, explains why it matters, and gives one hint for repairing it without supplying the corrected expression. Transition support needs persistence: the same unre-solved operation across comparable views for a waiting interval, timed from the last meaningful progress or the last delivered help, whichever is more recent. Because a repair can follow a single ob-servation while transition support must wait for persistence, we call this the repair-first policy. Proactive messages stay within the same boundaries on mathematical assistance as requested help. A quiet period after every learner or tutor message keeps proac-tive help from crowding the conversation, and learner requests take priority. Proactive messages stop once the worked solution is shown. A brief chime draws attention to proactive messages; learn-ers can keep writing or respond in chat, and the tutor is instructed to acknowledge requests to work independently. Appendix A.2 gives the analysis interval, quiet period, waiting interval, and per-session limit; Appendix A.3 describes how we refined the paper view and the delivery of proactive messages after pilot sessions; and Section 5.2.3 reports how often the tutor chose each action. 

## 4 Study Method 4.1 Participants and Recruitment 

We recruited adults with prior calculus experience to examine sup-port for previously studied concepts and the practicality of phone-based tutoring in their own workspaces. Recruitment was on Pro-lific, among adults aged 18 or older in the United States who had studied differential calculus through coursework or structured self-study and passed a brief screening. Participants needed a laptop or desktop computer, a camera-equipped smartphone, and pen and paper. Compensation was US$20 per hour. Prospective participants first saw the paper-and-phone setup requirements; 74 chose not to proceed at that point, for reasons we did not record. Of the 169 people who entered the study, 117 com-pleted it. The 52 who exited did so during camera setup, most citing problems getting the phone camera connected and started (25) or difficulty keeping a stable, readable view while writing (19). The setup was identical in every condition and preceded any condition-specific experience. Participants were assigned to conditions by sim-ple randomization: 40 in Text-Reactive, 37 in Visual-Reactive, and 40 in Visual-Proactive. Participants also reported their pre-ferred working medium, how recently they had used calculus, their AI use for mathematics, and the courses they had taken (Table 1); Appendix B gives screening criteria. The study was approved by our institution’s Institutional Re-view Board. Participants provided electronic informed consent and could withdraw at any time. The consent materials described the collection of paper images, conversations, and survey recordings, including processing by third-party AI services. Participants were instructed to direct the camera toward their paper and keep per-sonal information out of the view and chat. Study records were stored on university-controlled systems with access restricted to the research team. 

## 4.2 Tasks and Tests 

The assisted activity used related-rates problems from introductory differential calculus. These problems ask how fast one quantity changes given the rate of another, and solving them means setting up a relationship between changing quantities, differentiating it, and substituting values. Learners often do this work on paper. All conditions used the same ordered bank of ten problems and worked solutions. Early problems covered the concepts the tests assess: rates under constraints, geometric relationships and the chain rule, and how a rate depends on the current state. Later problems provided further practice and combined concepts. This ordering gave learners a common starting point while letting faster learners reach additional problems within the activity period. Each required one numeric final answer. Appendix B.2 lists the sequence and conceptual focus. The pre-test and post-test used alternate nine-item forms cover-ing the same concepts through different questions, completed with-out AI assistance, with form order counterbalanced within each con-dition. Pilot sessions with the same recruitment population helped us calibrate wording, difficulty, scoring, timing, and consistency between forms (Appendix B). The repository’s study-materials/ 

folder includes both test forms with scoring keys and all assisted problems with worked solutions. We estimated internal consistency for the 117 participants using Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) [47], separately for each form at pre-test and post-test because consistency can vary across administrations [23]. Estimates ranged from .77 to .85; Table 7 in Appendix B reports all four estimates and their 95% confidence intervals. 

## 4.3 Study Design and Procedure

An on-screen introduction explained the activity and what the assigned tutor could do, including visual access and proactive help in the conditions that had them. During the 35-minute assisted activity, participants worked through as many problems as they could in sequence. ## 4.4 Data Analysis 

Tutor use, problems attempted, and learning gains addressed RQ1; content analysis addressed RQ2; survey ratings and thematic anal-ysis of participant accounts addressed RQ3. 

4.4.1 Tutor Use, Problems Attempted, and Learning Gains. We chose a statistical test for each measure according to its distribution. Test scores were analyzed with linear models adjusted for pre-test score. Message counts, problems attempted, and single sur-vey items, which are skewed or ordinal, were compared across conditions with Kruskal–Wallis tests, followed by pairwise Mann– Whitney 𝑈 tests [38]. Pairwise comparisons within a measure, and the label tests within each label set of the content analysis, are Holm-corrected. We report effect sizes with the main tests; each table names the measure it uses. 

Tutor use. To describe how much learners interacted with the tutor and who initiated help, we counted learner messages and tutor messages; in Visual-Proactive, we distinguished requested replies from proactive messages. We summarized counts as means, stan-dard deviations, and medians for the whole activity and separately for problem solving and worked-solution review. Messages before the worked solution was shown counted toward problem solving; messages afterward counted toward worked-solution review. 

Proactive decisions. In Visual-Proactive, we counted the tutor’s paper analyses, its decisions to wait or intervene, and the messages it delivered. Kotalwar et al. 

Problems attempted. We counted the distinct problems each par-ticipant opened, which we call problems attempted. We also checked whether condition differences depended on prior knowledge, using the exploratory prior-knowledge model described under learning gains below. 

Learning gains. Both tests were scored out of nine, with one point per correct item. Unsubmitted or “I don’t know” responses scored zero. We tested pre-to-post change on the whole sample with a two-sided paired 𝑡 -test, with Cohen’s 𝑑 computed as the mean gain divided by the standard deviation of participants’ score changes. To compare conditions, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) ad-justed each participant’s post-test score for their pre-test score [73, 90], with robust standard errors; slopes were not statistically distin-guishable across conditions (OLS interaction 𝑝 = .201; Section 5.1.3), and we report adjusted differences at the covariate mean, where they do not depend on the slope assumption. Residuals were ap-proximately normal with equal variances. We did not detect an effect of test-form order on gains ( 𝑡 (115 ) = −0.52, 𝑝 = .602). Scores are bounded at zero and nine: 14 participants scored nine and 6 scored zero on the post-test, and 20 scored zero on the pre-test, so fitted values near the bounds are approximations. A rank-based version of the condition model gave the same result ( 𝑝 = .324). We report adjusted means, pairwise differences with 95% confidence intervals, and partial 𝜂 2. With this sample the widest pairwise in-terval spans about 1.8 points, so adjusted differences smaller than about 0.9 points, half a residual standard deviation, could not be distinguished from zero; a difference of about 1.3 points, two thirds of a residual standard deviation, would be needed for 80% power. Because we decided to examine prior knowledge after inspecting the pre-to-post patterns, we report that analysis as exploratory. The exploratory model allowed the effect of pre-test score to differ by condition and kept pre-test score continuous rather than split into groups, because splitting loses power and makes results depend on where the cut falls [57]. We report the interaction test, the fitted lines, and condition differences at pre-test scores of one and six, the sample’s lower and upper quartiles. A median-split version of this analysis appears in Appendix E.1 as an exploratory check. 

4.4.2 Content Analysis of Tutoring Interactions. To characterize what learners asked for and what the tutor did, we labeled every tutor message together with the learner message that prompted it, in the context of its problem trajectory : one participant’s conversa-tion and recorded paper work on one problem, including worked-solution review. We used directed content analysis [36], drawing on help-seeking and tutorial dialogue research [16, 30, 79]. The codebook has two parts. Help-seeking purposes (six labels) describe what a learner’s request sought. Tutor moves (eight labels) describe what the tutor’s message did. A message can carry more than one label, because one message often does several things at once. “You dropped the negative sign” gives correctness feedback and identi-fies an error, and a single request can ask both for a check and for the next step. Purpose labels apply only to learner messages that request help, which the codebook identifies first; tutor moves apply to every tutor message, requested or proactive. Appendix C defines the labels. Three annotators from the research team developed and cali-brated the codebook. They independently annotated 39 messages from 16 trajectories, discussed disagreements, refined the defini-tions, and re-annotated all 39 with the final codebook. They then independently annotated a held-out evaluation set of 155 messages from 32 further trajectories, sampled across conditions. We mea-sured agreement with Krippendorff’s 𝛼 [6]. Because a message can carry several purpose or move labels, we compared label sets us-ing the MASI distance [72], which credits partial overlap between two annotators’ sets rather than counting any difference as a full disagreement, as in prior multi-label dialogue annotation [24, 33]. Agreement among the annotators was high ( 𝛼 = .799– .917; Table 2), indicating that the codebook could be applied consistently. Annotating all 2,140 tutor messages with their paper views by hand was impractical, so we used GPT-5.6 Terra as an annotation model, following recent work on human-evaluated LLM annota-tion [3, 70, 93]. The annotation model differed from the tutoring model, so the tutor did not score its own messages. The model received the same materials as the annotators: the codebook, the conversation, the task and reference solution, and the recorded paper views. We developed its prompts against the annotators’ an-notations of the 39-message calibration set, froze them, and then evaluated the model on the 155-message evaluation set against each annotator (Table 2). Model–human agreement was high for purposes ( 𝛼 = .867– .928) and lower for moves ( 𝛼 = .655– .693). The move coefficient reflects three labels with weaker agreement (task clarification, .47–.59; other, .59–.74; explanation, .72–.74), on which we make no condition claims. For the five moves we interpret in Section 5.2, per-label agreement with each annotator was at least .76 (Table 10). The model and the annotators agreed at 𝛼 = .941 on whether a learner message requested help. The frozen model then annotated the full corpus, which covers the 114 participants who received tutor messages. Appendix D gives the sampling, an-notation procedure, and per-label agreement. The annotators also rated mathematical correctness in the same evaluation set, and each rated 96.6–97.3% of the 146 messages with a mathematical claim as correct (Appendix D.4). We report each purpose as its share of a participant’s help re-quests and each move as its share of a participant’s tutor messages, averaged within condition with 95% participant-bootstrap confi-dence intervals; participants with no applicable message contribute no share. The reported percentages are therefore means of par-ticipants’ shares rather than percentages of the pooled messages. We compared participant shares across conditions with Kruskal– Wallis tests, Holm-corrected across the labels of each label set, with Holm-corrected pairwise Mann–Whitney tests within a label. Each participant contributes one share per label, so that talkative learn-ers do not dominate. For Visual-Proactive we also report move shares separately for requested replies and proactive messages (Ap-pendix E.2). These analyses characterize assistance requested and provided, not learner uptake.

## 5 Results 

We report results for each research question in turn. 

## 5.1 Tutor Use, Problems Attempted, and Learning (RQ1) 

We did not detect a difference between conditions in tutor use or in problems attempted. Learners gained from pre-test to post-test in every condition, and no significant difference emerged in adjusted post-test scores. 

5.1.1 Tutor Use by Condition, Mostly Learner-Initiated. Most dia-logue occurred during problem solving, with little exchange during worked-solution review (Table 3). Tests of message volume were not significant for learner messages or for tutor messages, and the medians were close. In Visual-Proactive, the tutor sent 4.2 proac-tive messages per participant in addition to 16.2 requested replies, versus 18.8 requested replies in Text-Reactive and 15.5 in Visual-Reactive. Tutoring was predominantly learner-initiated: even in Visual-Proactive, about 80% of tutor messages were requested replies.

5.1.2 Problems Attempted by Condition. Participants attempted about six of the ten problems on average (Table 3). The comparison between conditions was not significant. In the exploratory model described in Section 4.4.1 (interaction 𝐹 (2, 111 ) = 1.00, 𝑝 = .370, partial 𝜂 2 = .018), moderation by prior knowledge was likewise not detected. 

5.1.3 Every Condition Improved; Adjusted Scores by Condition. 

Across the whole sample, scores rose from 3.3 to 4.6 of nine, a significant increase of moderate size, and every condition improved (Table 4). Adjusting for pre-test score, we did not detect a difference between conditions. The largest adjusted difference, two thirds of a point favoring Visual-Proactive over Visual-Reactive, was about a third of a residual standard deviation and a quarter of the post-test standard deviation, with an interval that includes zero. 

Exploratory: no significant moderation by prior knowledge. The pre-test × condition interaction was not significant (Figure 3), though the fitted lines were not parallel. At a pre-test score of one, Visual-Proactive exceeded Visual-Reactive by 1.08 points [−0.18, 2.35] and Text-Reactive by 0.83 [ −0.42, 2.09]; at six the differences were 0.20 [ −0.81, 1.21] and −0.61 [ −1.56, 0.34]. The lines crossed between pre-test scores of about four and seven. A median-split version of this analysis (Appendix E.1) yielded one nominally significant contrast that did not hold up under robust standard errors, correction across contrasts, or a one-point change in the cut point. We therefore report a possible advantage of proactive tutoring for learners with low prior knowledge as a hypothesis for a larger study, not a finding.

## 5.3 Learner Experience (RQ3) 

Learners rated the visual conditions more highly on support for their ongoing work, and their own accounts explain what they valued and what they still wanted. We report the survey ratings first, then the four themes from the open responses. 

5.3.1 Ratings Favored Visual Access; Perceived Control Was High Throughout. Learners in both visual conditions agreed more strongly than Text-Reactive learners that they received useful help without restating their written steps (Table 5). Visual-Reactive learners also rated the fit of feedback to their current solving stage above Text-Reactive, with Visual-Proactive not distinguishable from either. Learners in both visual conditions agreed that feedback accurately reflected their written work, with Visual-Reactive rated significantly higher ( 𝑝 = .045; Table 5). Agreement that learners controlled which step to try next was high in every condition, and the test across conditions was not significant. The Visual-Proactive learners who received proactive help agreed that it supported their progress and disagreed that it interrupted their work. Camera setup was rated moderately easy in all conditions. Ratings tracked the content analysis: visual access improved perceived fit to ongoing work. 

5.3.2 Participant Accounts. Four themes summarize the 117 open-response accounts (Section 4.4.4). Counts are indicative of preva-lence in one researcher’s coding: they give the number of partici-pants whose accounts expressed an idea, out of 117 unless a smaller group is named. Accounts commonly expressed several ideas, so counts overlap. Spoken responses were machine-transcribed; we normalized capitalization in quotations and marked omissions with ellipses. Quotations otherwise preserve the original wording. 

Verification in place, with a risk of reliance. Learners in the visual conditions valued a tutor that read their work and checked it in place, echoing the rise in verification requests in Section 5.2.1. Of the 77 participants with a paper view, 12 said the tutor understood what their writing referred to and 6 described being spared from retyping. P89 (Visual-Reactive) valued confirmation that came “just by looking at my work so I didn’t have to like type it in,” and P30 (Visual-Proactive) found that “being able to show it where I’m at instead of having to try to write out that problem into a text based answer is so much easier.” Checking reassured 21 of the 77, compared with 3 of the 40 in Text-Reactive, and it mattered even to learners who felt able to solve alone: P14 (Visual-Reactive) could now solve similar problems “but it would be considerably more difficult without knowing if I have gone wrong on a particular step.” Several Text-Reactive learners wanted the same; P02 (Text-Reactive) “would have liked for the AI to be able to see what I’m doing on the paper and read the steps that I’m doing and follow along.” Easy checking had a cost for some: 6 participants, all in the visual conditions, worried about leaning on the tutor, and P57 (Visual-Reactive) admitted to “relying on it a bit too much at times I could have probably tried and remembered some of these things on my own.” 

Demand for more initiative. Asking for help was itself work for 23 participants, and 20 wished the tutor would step in without being asked. ## 6 Discussion 

In a randomized study with 117 adults, a paper view changed what the tutor said, and permission to act changed what help arrived. Kotalwar et al. 

## 6.1 Seeing the Work Changes What Has to Be Said 

A paper view removed a step from the interaction. The tutor con-firmed and corrected work the learner had never typed, and its requests that learners communicate their work or reasoning fell to under 3% of messages. Learners’ own requests leaned toward asking for a check, although the corrected test was not signifi-cant (Section 5.2.1). Participants’ accounts suggest why: the page carried context that would otherwise be typed, and confirmation of a correct step let them build on it. The page appears to have served as common ground [19] and performed both functions of a shared visual space, anchoring what was said and showing the state of the task [27]. A paper view brings to conversational tutoring the feedback on intermediate work that step-based systems pro-vided [87, 88], without their structured input and without the single snapshot that photo-based apps read [80]. One design implication is that a tutor with a view could offer to check the current step before being asked. 

## 6.2 From Seeing to Acting: What Proactivity Added 

The added error identification and directive hints came from the proactive messages and were not detected when Visual-Proactive was represented by its requested replies alone (Section 5.2.2). Error diagnosis was among the rarest request purposes, and P26 valued help that arrived before they had noticed their own mistake. Hu-man tutors watch for such errors [18, 63]; learning during tutoring concentrates at impasses [89], and learners often do not ask for help when they need it [2, 77]. Mixed-initiative design weighs the value of acting against the cost of interrupting [35], and the repair-first policy applied that rule conservatively. It waited after 96% of its analyses and sent nothing at all to eight of the 40 participants. Par-ticipants who received proactive messages rated them supportive rather than interrupting, and 11 of the 12 who wished for more had already received some. Prior proactive assistants were preferred only when interjections were spaced out and limited [17], were sometimes experienced as interruptions when triggered by inactiv-ity [75], or left learners feeling less in control [68]. The messages here were few, about four per learner, which alone may explain their acceptance. The trigger may also matter: the tutor acted on evidence of an error rather than on a pause, which may be part of why its messages were welcome. 

## 6.3 Three Decisions Behind Proactive Help 

Learners did not express one stable preference for more or less help. The same learner could welcome a proactive error alert while asking that next steps be left for them to request, and the accounts pulled in different directions (Section 5.3.2). Contingent scaffolding has long distinguished when to help, what to help with, and how much of the task to take over [85, 92]. Asking is work of its own, and generative AI makes it more demanding still (Section 2.2) [84]; 23 participants described asking for help in these terms. A single “more or less proactive” setting conflates when to help, what to help with, and how much to take over, and the accounts distinguish them. Learners wanted the tutor to intervene when they were wrong or stuck; they divided on how much of a step it should perform (16 wanted to keep the steps, 21 wanted more concrete steps) and on how deeply it should explain. The waiting rules and the model’s error threshold kept help from crowding the conversation, and some learners would have welcomed it more often. Tutor policies could therefore set the timing, focus, and depth of proactive help separately, with each choice evaluated against learning alongside learner preference [44]. Because the three dimensions were fixed together in a single policy, their separation here rests on learners’ accounts rather than on an experimental contrast. 

## 6.4 Learning After One Session 

Learners improved significantly from pre-test to post-test in every condition. Adjusted post-test scores did not differ detectably be-tween conditions, and an exploratory trend favored the proactive tutor for learners with low pre-test scores (Section 5.1.3). The study was not powered to detect an interaction of the observed size (Sec-tion 7), so the analysis does not rule out the pattern the assistance dilemma [44] predicts, in which more help benefits learners with low prior knowledge more than those with high prior knowledge. The added proactivity was also light: about four proactive messages per learner, and learning was assessed with a nine-item test. 

## 6.5 Everyday Devices as a Tutoring Interface 

Ordinary paper and a phone lower the hardware requirements of visual tutoring relative to tablets and digital canvases [50, 54]. They let a population that mostly preferred paper keep writing where they already write. The remote study also exposed the cost of the setup: 74 people declined after seeing the requirements, and about three in ten of those who entered exited during camera setup, most because the camera would not connect or the view would not stay readable (Section 4.1). Connection failures suggest software or device problems; stability problems are mechanical, and an inex-pensive stand might reduce that source of exits in a deployment. Kraut et al. [46] report a comparable cost in remote collaboration, where aiming the camera took effort of its own. Participants who completed the study rated setup moderately easy, and those ratings come only from people who established a view. Cloud inference carries a per-session cost, higher in the proactive condition, and sends written work to a third party. Smaller models specialized for handwritten mathematics, run locally, could reduce both cost and privacy exposure, as has been demonstrated for programming support [45]. 

## 6.6 A Dataset for Studying Assistance Around Ongoing Work 

Because the platform recorded the paper in all three conditions, the dataset supports comparisons that one tutor’s logs cannot. Trajec-tories can be replayed to a different tutor or a different intervention policy, and the same written work can be shown to tutors with and without visual access. The dataset links evolving handwriting, tutor-ing conversations, paper analyses, proactive messages, and learning outcomes for 117 participants, across 699 problem trajectories and 255,984 timestamped images. Existing resources provide structured mathematical dialogue [59], static images of student work [9], vi-sually grounded geometry tutoring [69], and multimodal tutoring evaluation [94]. Ours adds real learners’ evolving work on paper Visual Access and Proactive AI Tutoring 

with requested and proactive assistance, task state, and test perfor-mance. The open questions it supports include when a tutor should intervene, whether learners take up a correction, and how well a model reads real handwriting. The de-identified dataset is available in the project repository (Appendix A.2), together with a sample of 10 participants viewable in a dashboard (Appendix F). 

## 7 Limitations and Future Work 

Participants were US adults on Prolific who had studied calculus before, working on their own devices in their own spaces. The findings may not transfer directly to students in classrooms, to first-time learners, or to people with different access needs. The sample describes people who established a readable paper view, so the setup exits qualify every result and not only the setup-ease ratings. Every condition required the camera, so the study does not estimate its burden relative to plain chat tutoring. We tested one model with one set of tutoring and initiative policies; better ones are surely possible. The proactive policy as delivered was almost entirely error repair, and its trigger was not varied. Transition support was rarely triggered, and we did not analyze how often its persistence condition was met, so it is not evaluated here. The 35-minute activity and immediate tests capture a first en-counter with the tutor. Because every condition included tutoring, the gains include the effect of practice and cannot isolate the tu-tor’s contribution. Roughly 330 participants would be needed for 80% power to detect a prior-knowledge interaction of the size we observed. Help-seeking purposes and tutor moves were labeled by a model validated against three human annotators, so not every message was labeled by humans. The thematic analysis was carried out by one researcher. The experience measures are self-reports given immediately after the session by learners who had been told before the activity what their tutor could do, which may shape the ratings. Whether learners acted on each tutor message was not mea-sured; the recorded paper trajectories make that uptake analysis possible, and it is our next step. Repeated sessions and delayed tests would then show whether the effects of a first encounter persist. A larger sample spanning a wider range of prior knowledge could test whether proactive repair helps the learners least able to notice their own errors. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We contribute a platform in which a phone camera gives an AI tutor a live view of learners’ work on ordinary paper, and a dataset that aligns evolving handwriting with tutoring conversations and learn-ing outcomes. In a randomized study with 117 adults, we compared three tutors: chat alone, chat plus the paper view, and the paper view plus permission to initiate help. Visual access shifted the tutor’s messages toward correctness feedback on existing work, and learn-ers with the view rated the help as more useful because they did not need to restate their steps. Initiative added error identification and directive hints, and recipients rated the proactive help supportive. Learners in every condition improved significantly from pre-test to post-test, and we did not detect a significant difference in adjusted post-test scores between conditions; an exploratory trend favored proactive tutoring for learners with low prior knowledge. These findings inform when a tutor should act on what it sees and what it should address, and the released platform and dataset support further research on the next generation of such tutors.

## A.2 Tutoring Prompts and System Code 

The project repository, https://github.com/nachiketdk/proactive-ai-tutoring, contains the materials described here and in Appen-dices B and F, together with the dataset described in Section 6.6; its README.md maps the folders to the paper and explains their use. The study-materials/ folder holds the setup and tutorial instruc-tions, both test forms with scoring keys, the assisted problems with worked solutions, and the background and final questionnaires (Appendix B), and the trajectory-sample/ folder holds the dash-board sample (Appendix F). The tutoring-prompts/ folder pro-vides the shared pedagogical policy, the requested-help prompts for Text-Reactive and Visual-Reactive, and the proactive prompt for Visual-Proactive. The system-code/ folder provides the tutoring modules and participant-interface source, including problem and paper context, proactive decisions, and worked-solution handling. A local demo exercises these components through a provider-neutral model adapter; recruitment, payment, and deployment services are omitted. The README distinguishes the demo interface from the original participant client. 

Proactive decision parameters. In Visual-Proactive, the server scheduled a paper analysis on every seventh received paper view, about once every seven seconds at the roughly one-view-per-second capture rate, with at most 300 analyses per session. Each analysis supplied the latest view, earlier views for comparison, the conversa-tion and proactive-message history, and a computed flag indicating whether the same work had been observed for the 120-second wait-ing interval. No analysis started and no proactive message was delivered within 45 seconds of any learner or tutor message, and a proactive message prepared before the learner sent a new message was withheld. Model reasoning effort was set to medium. These values were set during pilot rehearsals rather than derived from prior literature. 

Illustrative applications of the tutoring policy. A learner might model a rectangle’s fixed area as 𝑙𝑤 = 12, where 𝑙 and 𝑤 are its changing length and width, then write 𝑙 ′𝑤 ′ = 0 (primes denote time derivatives) and ask whether the differentiation is correct. In Text-Reactive, the tutor can check the expression if it is shared in chat. Visual access allows it to locate the product-rule error on paper even when the expression has not been typed, and explain why both changing dimensions matter. In Visual-Proactive, the visible error can prompt help before the learner asks. Proactive help can also address an unfinished solution without a visible error. A learner might draw a correct diagram but not yet use its geometric relationship to express the volume; a hint can help connect these steps. These examples illustrate the instructed policy; the complete prompts specify its assistance and waiting rules. 

## A.3 Pilot-Informed System Refinement 

Pilot sessions prompted two refinements. Participants arranged their phones and paper in different ways, so handwriting was sometimes rotated in the paper view, which led to misreadings by the tutor; we added automatic orientation correction before views reached the tutor. We also reduced the delay between ob-serving the learner’s work and delivering help. We checked both changes by replaying pilot sessions and running end-to-end test sessions, examining mathematical correctness, use of the avail-able context, adherence to the tutoring policy, and the delivery of proactive messages.

## C Interaction Coding Framework C.1 Coding Unit and Applicability 

Each tutor message formed one coding unit within a problem trajec-tory (Section 4.4.2). Help-seeking purposes described the initiating learner message; tutor moves described the tutor’s message. Proac-tive messages had no initiating learner request. Coding used the task, reference solution, preceding conversation, and relevant paper views. 

Table 8: Help-seeking purposes. A request can carry more than one purpose. 

Purpose Definition Verification Requests a check of an answer, expression, approach, or interpretation. Procedural guidance Requests a formula, method, next step, or help carrying out a mathematical operation. Explanation Requests mathematical meaning or a reason for a result or operation. Error diagnosis Asks where or why existing work may be wrong. Task clarification Asks about task wording, givens, or the quantity, comparison, time, or units requested. Other Requests assistance outside these purposes, such as submission help or coordinating the camera view. 

Table 9: Tutor moves. Labels describe the function attempted. 

Move Definition Correctness feedback Affirms or rejects existing learner mathematics or a proposed interpretation. Error identification Locates or describes a specific mistake the tutor asserts is present in the learner’s work. Directive hint Directs attention or suggests an operation while leaving that operation for the learner to perform. Elicitation Invites the learner to communicate work, an answer, or reasoning to the tutor. Direct mathematical provision Supplies a formula, procedure, relationship, corrected expression, or worked step. Explanation Supplies a mathematical reason or interpretation beyond the action or answer itself. Task clarification Interprets task wording, givens, or the requested quantity, comparison, time, or units. Other Provides assistance outside these moves, such as interface help or conversational coordination. 

We first identified whether the learner’s message requested help. Purpose labels applied only to help requests; tutor moves applied to every tutor message. Each applicable purpose and move was coded as present, absent, or cannot determine, so a message could carry several purposes or moves. Other indicated an identifiable function outside the listed categories; cannot determine indicated insufficient evidence. 

## C.2 Codebook 

The categories adapt distinctions from tutoring questions and feed-back research [30, 67, 79], retaining overlapping functions as in dialogue-act coding [16]. Tables 8 and 9 define the six help-seeking purposes and eight tutor moves. Labels capture distinct functions that may co-occur (Sec-tion 4.4.2). A directive hint leaves an operation to the learner; supplying its result is direct mathematical provision. ## D Annotation Evaluation D.1 Coding and Evaluation Samples 

Three annotators refined the codebook on 39 tutor messages from 16 problem trajectories and then independently coded an evaluation set of 155 messages from 32 further trajectories, one per participant, stratified by condition and problem position. The evaluation set contained 11 Text-Reactive, 11 Visual-Reactive, and 10 Visual-Proactive trajectories, comprising 143 requested replies and 12 proactive messages. Annotators used the codebook, the conversa-tion, the task and reference solution, and the recorded paper views. Each annotator’s judgments were retained and compared separately with the annotation model. 

## D.2 Annotation Model 

The annotation model was GPT-5.6 Terra with medium reasoning. A first pass transcribed the recorded handwriting, marking uncer-tain readings; a second pass received the codebook, the task and reference solution, the preceding conversation, the focal messages, the relevant paper views, and the transcription, and produced the labels. Prompts and the context procedure were developed on the 39-message set and frozen before the 155-message evaluation [70]. Inputs excluded paper views recorded after the tutor’s message, later conversation and outcomes, condition labels, and human an-notations. 

## D.3 Agreement 

Agreement used Krippendorff’s 𝛼 [6, 32], with the MASI distance for purpose and move label sets [24, 33, 72] and nominal distance for individual labels. Purpose agreement used the 110 messages with an applicable help request (109 in each model–annotator pair); move agreement used all 155 messages. Table 10 reports agreement for each label. Exact agreement between the model and Annotators 1–3 was 92.7%, 94.5%, and 90.8% for purpose sets and 61.9%, 56.1%, and 56.8% for move sets. Agreement also varied by condition. Among the 60 Visual-Proactive messages in the evaluation set, model– annotator agreement on directive hint (.57–.64) and elicitation (.65– .85) was lower than in the reactive conditions (.88–1.00), while correctness feedback (.86–.93) and error identification (.79–.86) remained close to the pooled values. 

## D.4 Mathematical Correctness of Tutor Messages 

The three annotators also rated the mathematical correctness of each tutor message in the 155-message evaluation set, judged against the task, the reference solution, and the learner’s written work. Each message was rated correct, mixed (containing any incorrect mathematics), or cannot determine. Nine messages made no mathematical claim, such as acknowledgments, and were excluded. Of the remaining 146 messages, each annotator rated 141 or 142 as correct (96.6–97.3%), 0 to 2 as mixed, and 2 to 5 as cannot determine; 139 were rated correct by all three.
