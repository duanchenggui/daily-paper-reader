Abstract 

Existing student models in educational appli-cations largely focus on student responses to questions. However, as tutoring becomes more accessible at large scale, it is important to an-alyze dialogues between tutors and students, which may reveal fine-grained evidence on stu-dent knowledge. In this work, we propose a unified framework that models both student re-sponses to questions and tutor-posed subtasks during dialogues on a shared item response theory (IRT) scale. We first identify mathe-matical subtasks in tutoring dialogues, rewrite them into self-contained questions, and identify the correctness of student responses to these subtasks. We then develop an IRT framework that estimates question/subtask difficulty from text, tracks students’ evolving abilities across question-answering and dialogue interactions, and a novel method to leverages tutor moves be-tween consecutive subtasks to regularize their difficulty values. We evaluate our framework on two real-world math education datasets con-taining both human- and AI-tutored dialogues. Results show that our framework outperforms strong baselines on both question difficulty pre-diction and response correctness prediction.

Estimating student knowledge and item characteris-tics from students’ responses to mathematical ques-tions/items is central to online learning platforms, which supports personalized practice, targeted tu-toring, and reliable assessment. For example, for multiple-choice questions, only knowing whether a student selected the correct option or which in-correct option they selected does not allow us to pinpoint their misunderstanding. Powered by generative artificial intelligence (AI), online learning platforms increasingly com-bine standalone question-answering practice with on-demand tutoring (Eedi, 2024; Khan Academy, 2023; Carnegie Learning, 2024). Related Work However, existing approaches do not provide a rigorous way to analyze original question responses and tutor-posed tasks together. Prior work has explored question difficulty predic-tion from question content (Huang et al., 2017; Benedetto et al., 2021) and simulated student re-sponses (Scarlatos et al., 2025b), as well as KT in tutoring dialogues (Scarlatos et al., 2025a). More recently, Huang et al. (2026) incorporate tutor-turn difficulty and IRT into conversational KT. However, two challenges remain for jointly modeling original questions and dialogue subtasks. First, tutoring dia-logues follow personalized trajectories, resulting in subtasks that are often not shared across students and thus difficult to calibrate using traditional IRT. Second, many tutor-posed tasks implicitly refer to quantities, expressions, or reasoning steps intro-duced earlier in the problem or dialogue, making them difficult to represent as self-contained, stan-dalone questions. Together, these challenges leave open the question of how to place original ques-tions and tutor-posed tasks on the same scale and use them to track student knowledge. We provide a detailed discussion of related work in Appendix A. 

Contributions In this work, we propose URF-QD (Unified Response Framework for Questions and tutoring Dialogues), a framework for jointly modeling responses to original questions and math-ematical subtasks arising in corresponding tutoring dialogues. Our contributions are threefold. First, we identify tutor turns that pose mathematical tasks and rewrite these context-dependent tasks as con-cise, self-contained questions, enabling their diffi-culties and student responses to be modeled jointly with those of original questions. Second, we intro-duce a dynamic IRT model that tracks the student’s evolving knowledge state from responses to both original questions and dialogue subtasks; impor-tantly, we introduce a novel tutor move-implied regularization term to supervise a relative ordering between consecutive subtasks. Third, we evalu-ate our framework on two real-world math educa-tion datasets containing both question-answering records and tutoring dialogues. Extensive evalu-ation shows that our framework consistently im-proves question difficulty prediction and response correctness prediction. We use human evaluation and qualitative analyses to validate and illustrate components of our framework, and present a pre-liminary example of how our framework can be used for large language model (LLM) tutor bench-marking in the Appendix. Our implementation will be made publicly available to enable future research on student assessment from dialogues. 

2 Problem Formulation 

We consider the setting of students working in digi-tal learning platforms, where they practice on math questions and interact with tutors when they want additional support. Our goal is to estimate a stu-dent’s ability from how they respond to both ques-tions and tutor-posed subtasks that arise naturally during tutoring dialogues. For each student j, we denote the binary-valued correctness of their response to an original ques-tion qi as yi,j ∈ { 0, 1}, where 1 corresponds to a correct response. When receiving tutoring support on question qi, the student and the tutor engage in a dialogue di,j = ( s0, t 1, s 1, ..., t M , s M ), where sm

and tm denote student and tutor utterances respec-tively and M is the number of total tutor-student turn pairs. The initial student turn s0 is present when the student initiates the conversation. We focus on tutor turns that pose a mathematical subtask, i.e., when the tutor requires the student to produce a mathematically meaningful response, such as an intermediate calculation, factual recall, method selection, interpretation, or explanation. 3 Methodology 

We now detail our framework for jointly modeling student performance in question-answering activi-ties and tutoring dialogues. Our key idea is to place original questions and tutor-posed dialogue sub-tasks on the same IRT parameter scale. As shown in Figure 2, our framework has three main com-ponents. First, we identify mathematical subtasks posed by tutors during dialogues and transform these context-dependent tasks into the format of standalone questions. Second, we use a dynamic IRT model to jointly model responses to original questions and transformed dialogue subtasks on a shared scale. Third, we leverage tutor peda-gogical move labels to impose difficulty ordering constraints between consecutive tutor-posed tasks, to provide additional supervision when estimating their difficulties. We note that, for simplicity, we restrict ourselves to 1PL IRT models (Hambleton and Swaminathan, 2013), which is often referred to as the Rasch model (Rasch, 1960), where each question is only characterized by a single scalar-valued difficulty parameter. Our framework can be extended to other IRT models, although diffi-culty estimation is known to be more accurate than scale/discrimination estimation for 2PL IRT mod-els (Chen and Chen, 2026). 

3.1 Subtask Transformation 

Tutoring dialogues contain many intermediate mathematical tasks that provide additional evi-dence of student ability. However, tutor utterances are often context-dependent and differ substantially in form from standalone assessment questions. We therefore first identify tutor turns that constitute mathematical subtasks and then rewrite each iden-tified subtask into a standalone question, allowing original questions and subtasks to be represented in a common form for subsequent modeling. 3Subtask Identification For each dialogue di,j ,we consider every tutor turn that is immediately followed by a student turn as a candidate subtask. We prompt GPT-5.4 with the complete dialogue to identify the valid mathematical subtasks: for each candidate, it determines whether the tutor turn poses an explicit mathematical task and whether the subsequent student turn attempts to answer it. We retain only candidates that satisfy both condi-tions. Following (Scarlatos et al., 2026), for each retained tutor subtask, we annotate whether the stu-dent response sm correctly answers the tutor-posed task. For each scored response, we denote this cor-rectness label as yi,j,m ∈ { 0, 1}. Turns without an applicable mathematical response are left unscored and are not used as response observations. 

Standalone Question Transformation For ev-ery identified tutor subtask tm, we prompt GPT-5.4 to rewrite the tutor’s request as a concise, stan-dalone assessment question ui,j,m . Given the orig-inal problem and dialogue with the target turn marked, the model resolves contextual references and supplies only the information needed to make the question self-contained while preserving its mathematical demand. We provide further prompt details in Appendix G. For each scored subtask, we map the transformed question ui,j,m and its response label yi,j,m to the corresponding interaction k in the student’s chrono-logical history Hj : xj,k = ui,j,m , y j,k = yi,j,m .

This mapping represents dialogue subtasks in the same form as original questions, enabling both in-teraction types to be modeled jointly. 

3.2 URF-QD: Joint Modeling of Questions and Dialogue Subtasks 

Given the chronological interaction history Hj ,which contains both original question responses and transformed dialogue subtask interactions, we model student performance using a dynamic IRT framework. We process interactions sequentially and maintain a latent student state hj,k that sum-marizes the student’s interactions up to step k. At interaction k, we first encode the current task xj,k 

and estimate its difficulty. We then project the pre-ceding state hj,k −1 to a scalar ability and use a 1PL IRT model to predict the student’s response. After observing yj,k , we update the student state using the task representation and response correctness.

Rather than assigning an independent difficulty parameter to every observed task, the model estimates difficulty from its textual content, enabling the learned difficulty function to general-ize across questions, students, and dialogues. 

Dynamic Student State and Ability We rep-resent student j’s interaction history with a la-tent state hj,k that summarizes all observed task-response pairs through step k. After observing the student’s response yj,k to task xj,k , we incorporate this new evidence into the state. We first repre-sent response correctness using a learned embed-ding: rj,k = Ey(yj,k ), where Ey contains separate learnable embeddings for correct and incorrect re-sponses to both original questions and dialogue sub-tasks. We then concatenate resulting correctness embedding with task’s textual embedding. One can use any deep learning-based KT method for this dynamic update; unless otherwise noted, we defer to the deep KT (DKT) method (Piech et al., 2015). 

IRT Response Model Before the student at-tempts task xj,k , we estimate their current abil-ity by projecting the preceding state to a scalar: 

aj,k = ga(hj,k −1), where ga is a MLP. This pre-interaction ability is used in the IRT response model below and does not depend on the current response yj,k . After that response is observed, the updated state hj,k is used to estimate ability at inter-action k + 1 . Unlike a static IRT ability parameter, 

aj,k is dynamic throughout the student’s interac-tion sequence and updated when new responses are observed. Therefore, dialogue subtask responses serve not only as correctness-prediction targets, but also as additional observations that refine future ability estimates. Given the student’s current abil-ity aj,k and the difficulty of the current task, bj,k ,we model response correctness using a 1PL logistic 4IRT model: 

p(yj,k = 1 | xj,k , H j,<k ) = σ(aj,k − bj,k ),

where σ(·) denotes the sigmoid function. Because original questions and dialogue subtasks share the same task encoder, difficulty head, ability projec-tion, and IRT response function, their difficulties and the student’s dynamic ability are represented on a common latent scale. 

3.3 Difficulty Order Regularization 

Correctness observations supervise the relationship between student ability and task difficulty, but tu-toring dialogues provide an additional source of information: the tutor’s pedagogical move can im-ply a relative difficulty ordering between consec-utive subtasks. We exploit two cases, scaffolding after struggle and pushing for rigor (Zhang et al., 2026), and use them to regularize task difficulties. 

Pedagogical Move Annotation For each pair of consecutive tutor subtasks separated by a single student response, we prompt GPT-5.4 to determine whether the transition implies a tutor move that implies any relationship between the subtask dif-ficulties. The model is provided with the origi-nal problem, the raw tutoring dialogue with sub-tasks marked, eligible tutor-turn pairs, and the an-notated correctness label of the intervening student response. We withhold the student’s response to the second subtask, so that the relation is determined from the tutor’s pedagogical action rather than from whether the student subsequently succeeds or fails. We denote the set of turns that correspond to scaffolding after struggle moves as Ω1. In practice, when students respond incorrectly to tutor-posed subtasks or indicate that they do not know the an-swer, tutors often scaffold by breaking down the original subtask into smaller, simpler, more con-crete subtasks; such moves imply that bj,k < b j,k −1.Similarly, we denote the set of pushing for rigor turns as Ω2. A pair is assigned to Ω2 when the previous student response is correct and the sub-sequent tutor subtask increases the required depth of reasoning, for example by eliciting a justifica-tion, explanation, derivation, interpretation, proof, or other conceptual reasoning; such moves imply 

bj,k > b j,k −1. We assign no constraint when the intervening response cannot be scored for correct-ness, when the two subtasks are unrelated, or when the second tutor turn merely repeats the task, re-quests a generic retry, continues a routine multi-step solution, or otherwise does not substantively change the cognitive demand.

3.4 Prediction Targets, Training Objectives 

We study two complementary prediction targets us-ing separate training objectives. Question difficulty prediction evaluates whether the shared difficulty encoder can accurately estimate the difficulty of previously unseen questions. Response correctness prediction evaluates whether original-question and dialogue-subtask responses can jointly track a stu-dent’s evolving ability and accurately predict their future performance.

4 Experimental Evaluation 

We now detail our experimental setup to validate the effectiveness of our framework. 

4.1 Datasets 

We conduct experiments on two math tutoring datasets collected by two major online learning platforms, Eedi and Khan Academy . For sim-plicity, we refer to these datasets by their respec-tive platform names. Both datasets contain stu-dent interactions with math questions, where stu-dents can initiate chat-based tutoring conversa-tions on demand at any point during the question-answering process. The Eedi dataset contains con-versations between real students and human tutors, whereas the Khan Academy dataset contains con-versations between real students and an AI tutor model. After processing, the Eedi dataset con-tains 588 students, 3, 689 questions, 1, 775 dia-logues, and 152 , 280 question-answering records, while the Khan Academy dataset contains 136 

students, 3, 423 questions, 1, 800 dialogues, and 

56 , 529 question-answering records. We provide additional dataset details in Appendix B. 

4.2 Metrics 

For question difficulty prediction, following prior work (Huang et al., 2017; Scarlatos et al., 2025b), we use the widely adopted Pearson Correlation Co-efficient (PCC) to measure the linear correlation be-tween predicted and ground-truth question difficul-ties, Spearman Rank Correlation Coefficient (SCC) to measure the correlation between predicted and ground-truth difficulty rankings among questions, Mean Squared Error (MSE) to measure squared prediction error, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) to measure absolute prediction error. For response correctness prediction, we use Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), accuracy, and F1 score metrics. 

4.3 Baselines 

We compare URF-QD against kNN Mean, Random Forest, ICL kNN, and Diff SFT (ModernBERT and Qwen3.5-4B) for question difficulty prediction. For response correctness prediction, we compare against DKT, DKT (Joint), AKT, simpleKT, and URF-QD (Orig. Question) to assess the contribu-tion of dialogue subtask responses within and be-yond our framework. We use Joint to denote train-ing on both original-question and dialogue-subtask responses and Reg. to denote relative difficulty or-der regularization. We provide detailed baseline descriptions in Appendix C.1. 

4.4 Experimental Setup 

We report the average performance on 5-fold cross validation. We split datasets across problems (stu-dents) for question difficulty prediction (response correctness prediction) evaluation, to evaluate gen-eralization to unseen problems (students). We use Qwen3.5-4B for URF-QD and evaluate ICL kNN using GPT-5.4. We provide full experimental de-tails in Appendix C.2.

7 Conclusions and Future Work 

In this paper, we presented URF-QD, a framework for jointly modeling original questions and tutor-posed subtasks on a shared IRT scale. The key is to transform dialogue subtasks into standalone ques-tions and leverage pedagogical relations to regular-ize their difficulty estimates. Through experiments on two real-world math learning datasets, we show that URF-QD improves both question difficulty pre-diction and response correctness prediction. In Ap-pendix F, we showcase how our framework opens up the possibility of many downstream analyses, benchmarking an LLM tutor’s ability to pose tasks at appropriate difficulty levels that maximize stu-dent learning outcomes. There are several avenues for future work. First, the shared dynamic scale could support more anal-ysis into the strategy and behavior of LLM tutors. Future work should also use the extracted insights to guide adaptive subtask posing for both human and LLM tutors. Second, future work should ex-tend URF-QD to incorporate visual elements, such as images and diagrams, that commonly appear in mathematics questions. Finally, future work should examine whether the framework general-izes to other educational domains and settings. 8Limitations 

There are several limitations to our work. First, al-though we conduct experiments on two real-world math learning datasets, the Khan Academy dataset is not publicly available, and only a subset of the Eedi dataset is publicly accessible. To the best of our knowledge, there are no other readily available datasets containing both student question-answering records and corresponding tutoring di-alogues at the granularity required by our frame-work, making broader evaluation challenging. Sec-ond, since neither dataset provides ground-truth question difficulty labels, we use difficulty esti-mates obtained by fitting an IRT model to observed student responses as reference labels. These esti-mates depend on the assumptions and quality of the fitted IRT model and may not perfectly reflect the true difficulty of each question. Finally, we restrict our framework to the 1PL IRT model and binary response correctness labels, which may not fully capture the complexity of student knowledge and mathematical responses. Extending our framework to more expressive IRT models and finer-grained response evaluations is an important direction for future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

There are several potential societal benefits to our work. By jointly modeling student responses to original questions and tutoring dialogues, our framework can provide more fine-grained estimates of student knowledge and question difficulty, po-tentially supporting more accurate educational as-sessment and personalized learning. In particular, leveraging intermediate mathematical subtasks in tutoring dialogues may help educators better under-stand students’ learning needs and provide more targeted tutoring support. There are also several po-tential risks. Inaccurate estimates of student ability or question difficulty could lead to inappropriate learning recommendations or unfair assessments. Additionally, our framework relies on LLM-based annotations and transformations of tutoring dia-logues, which may introduce errors or biases into downstream predictions. Such biases could dis-proportionately affect students from underrepre-sented populations or with different linguistic back-grounds. Moreover, demographic information is unavailable in our datasets, limiting our ability to evaluate potential disparities across student popu-lations. We emphasize that our framework is in-tended to support, rather than replace, educators’ judgments, and further evaluation of fairness and downstream learning outcomes is necessary before deployment in real-world educational settings.

A Related Work 

A.1 Question Difficulty Prediction and Student Response Modeling 

IRT estimates student ability and item difficulty from observed responses (Rasch, 1960), but re-quires response data to calibrate unseen questions. Text-based difficulty prediction addresses this cold-start setting using linguistic features and pretrained language models (Huang et al., 2017; Benedetto et al., 2021; Gombert et al., 2024; AlKhuzaey et al., 2024). Recent work also predicts IRT parameters from item embeddings (Chen and Chen, 2026). An-other direction simulates student responses with language models (Zelikman et al., 2023), including SMART, which aligns simulated students with IRT abilities and estimates difficulty from scored syn-thetic responses (Scarlatos et al., 2025b). Unlike these item-centric methods, our framework lever-ages real student responses to tutor-posed mathe-matical subtasks as additional supervision for dif-ficulty prediction, allowing dialogue evidence to inform difficulty estimates for unseen original ques-tions. KT models students’ evolving knowledge from interaction histories, ranging from Bayesian KT (Corbett and Anderson, 1994) and DKT (Piech et al., 2015) to attention-based models such as AKT and simpleKT (Ghosh et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Deep-IRT further combines neu-ral KT with interpretable IRT parameters (Yeung, 2019). These methods primarily model predefined exercise-response sequences, whereas our frame-work incorporates dialogue-derived subtasks as ad-ditional response observations and tracks students’ evolving abilities across both original questions and tutoring dialogues on a shared IRT scale. 

A.2 Student Modeling in Tutoring Dialogues 

Tutoring dialogues reveal student understanding through intermediate mathematical tasks and adap-tive feedback. Datasets such as MathDial (Macina et al., 2023) and QATD-2k (Zent et al., 2025) support research on tutoring strategies and ped-agogical feedback (Petukhova and Kochmar, 2025; Kochmar et al., 2025). More directly, Scarlatos et al. (2025a) explore KT in tutoring dialogues, and Huang et al. (2026) incorporate tutor-turn difficulty and IRT into conversational KT. However, these approaches focus on predicting dialogue responses rather than jointly modeling original-question and subtask difficulties to predict the difficulty of un-seen questions. Tutors also adjust task demands by scaffolding after struggle or pushing for rigor after success (Zhang et al., 2026). We use these pedagogical transitions to constrain the relative difficulty of consecutive subtasks. Together with standalone subtask rewriting, this enables tutoring dialogues to provide supervision for both question difficulty and student ability estimation. 

B Additional Dataset Details 

We provide additional details on the two datasets. Both datasets are fully anonymized, so demo-graphic information about individual students and tutors is not available. The Eedi dataset contains multiple-choice math question records and tutoring dialogues between real students and human tutors. After processing, the dataset contains 4, 175 sub-task instances across 1, 775 dialogues. Each ques-tion is associated with a set of “subjects”, which we use to define the knowledge components (KCs). We assign each dialogue the same KCs as its as-sociated question. Among the subtask instances, 

467 correspond to scaffold after student struggle and 48 correspond to push for rigor. The Khan Academy dataset contains 4, 996 subtask instances across 1, 800 dialogues. Each question is associ-ated with a list of “skills”, which we similarly use to define the KCs for the corresponding dialogue. The dataset contains 549 cases of scaffold after stu-dent struggle and 21 cases of push for rigor. The 11 questions from both datasets span a broad range of mathematical topics, including Algebra, Geometry, and Number. 

C Additional Experimental Details 

We provide additional details on the baseline meth-ods and experimental implementation for repro-ducibility. 

C.1 Baselines Difficulty Prediction Baselines Following prior work (AlKhuzaey et al., 2024; Benedetto, 2023; Kapoor et al., 2026), we adopt a strong fine-tuning baseline, Diff SFT , which fine-tunes a recent and capable encoder-based language model, specifi-cally ModernBERT-base (Warner et al., 2024). We use a regression setup, minimizing MSE, to directly predict question difficulties given the question’s text and metadata. We additionally train Diff SFT (Qwen3.5-4B) using the same question encoder as our framework. We then report a Random Forest 

baseline, which is common for difficulty predic-tion (AlKhuzaey et al., 2024; Gombert et al., 2024), where we encode the item’s question text and meta data using SBERT model (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019), and use the resulting embeddings as input features to fit a random forest regressor to item dif-ficulty. We also use a simple yet strong baseline, 

kNN Mean , that retrieves the k questions in the train set with the highest SBERT embedding co-sine similarity, and takes their mean difficulty as the predicted test item difficulty. We search over 

k ∈ [1 , 5] and select the best performing value on the validation set, selecting k = 4 . Finally, we adapt an LLM prompting baseline leveraging in-context learning (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020), ICL kNN , where we prompt GPT-5.4 with k in-context examples of questions retrieved from the train set using k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN). We use the same SBERT model as above, and perform a similar hy-perparameter search, selecting k = 4 . We provide the prompt details in Appendix G. All these base-lines use only original questions and their reference difficulties, without dialogue-subtask response su-pervision or difficulty ordering regularization. 

Response Prediction Baselines For student re-sponse correctness prediction, we compare against three KT baselines: DKT (Piech et al., 2015), a simple recurrent neural network-based KT method, and DKT (Joint) , which is trained on the com-bined chronological sequence of original question and dialogue subtask responses; AKT (Ghosh et al., 2020), a full attention-based KT method; and sim-pleKT (Liu et al., 2023) a simple but robust KT method. Unless marked as Joint, these baselines are trained only on original question responses. We evaluate all methods on the same held-out original-question response events. Dialogue-subtask re-sponses are incorporated as additional training ob-servations and as history for the joint models. We additionally report URF-QD (Orig. Ques-tion) , an original question-only variant of our re-sponse prediction model. It retains the same text-based difficulty estimator, sequential student state model, and IRT response function as our full frame-work, but excludes all dialogue subtask interactions and the difficulty ordering regularizer. This base-line isolates the contribution of dialogue evidence within the proposed architecture. 

C.2 Implementation Details 

We use Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026) as the frozen LLM encoder for our framework and multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1 SBERT model for baselines and ablation studies. We use GPT-5.4 (OpenAI, 2026) for the prompting-based baseline (ICL kNN), mathematical subtask identification, subtask cor-rectness labeling, standalone-question transforma-tion, and pedagogical move annotation. We adopt the LLM-based correctness annotation protocol of Scarlatos et al. (2026), which demonstrated sub-stantial agreement with expert human annotations (Cohen’s κ= 0.722), comparable to human inter-annotator agreement ( κ = 0.719), providing empiri-cal support for its reliability. For question difficulty prediction, we obtain 

2560 -dimensional question embeddings by mean pooling over the final-layer hidden states of the frozen LLM encoder. We use a three-layer MLP and GELU activations to predict scalar question difficulty. We train the difficulty predictor using AdamW with a fixed learning rate of 5 · 10 −3, full-batch optimization, and gradient norm clipping at 

10 .0 for 120 epochs. For the ICL (kNN) baseline, we ask the model to first reason about the question’s difficulty in a chain-of-thought manner before gen-erating a numeric difficulty prediction. To obtain reference student abilities and ques-tion difficulties, we fit a 1PL IRT model using ob-served student responses to original questions. We fit the reference IRT model on all eligible original-question responses, without a train/validation/test split, including responses associated with questions 12 subsequently assigned to validation and test sets. We implement IRT fitting using SciPy with the L-BFGS-B optimization algorithm, setting the max-imum number of iterations to 500 . We apply L2 regularization to both student ability and question difficulty parameters, with a regularization strength of 1.0 for each. For response correctness prediction, we train an LSTM with a hidden-state dimension of 200 to model the evolving student state. We represent re-sponse correctness using a learned 32 -dimensional embedding, which is concatenated with the 2560 -dimensional question embedding to form a 2592 -dimensional LSTM input. Before predicting the current response, we project the preceding LSTM hidden state to a scalar student ability using a two layer MLP and a GELU activation. We train the model using AdamW with a fixed learning rate of 

1·10 −3, gradient norm clipping at 10 .0, and a batch size of 64 for 10 epochs. For both prediction tasks, we set the difficulty-order regularization weight to 

λ = 1 .We compute AUC using scikit-learn and Pearson and Spearman correlations using NumPy. To the best of our knowledge, the software and models used in our implementation either have open-source licenses or do not list a license. In addition, our use of these resources, including the OpenAI API, is consistent with their intended terms of use. We will ensure that the license and terms reflect the sources on which our implementation builds when we release code. We use ChatGPT to assist with writing and editing the manuscript.

D.2 Impact of Relative-Order Regularization 

We further analyze the effect of the relative-order regularization strength λ. For question difficulty prediction, λ = 0 is equivalent to removing the relative-order regularization. As shown in Fig-ure 3a, λ = 0 .1 performs similarly to λ = 0 , sug-gesting that a weak regularization signal has limited effect. Performance improves as λ increases to 0.5

and 1, while λ = 1 and λ = 3 achieve comparable performance across the four metrics. In contrast, increasing λ to 10 leads to a clear performance drop. A possible reason is that overly strong reg-ularization causes the training objective to focus excessively on relative difficulty ordering rather than accurately fitting the absolute difficulty labels. For response correctness prediction, Figure 3b shows that performance remains relatively stable across λ ∈ { 0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 3}, suggesting that re-sponse prediction is not sensitive to regularization weight within a moderate range. In contrast, setting 

λ = 10 substantially hurts all three metrics, likely because overly strong regularization constrains the model at the expense of optimizing the response-prediction objective. To further examine how the regularization affects learned question difficulties, we evaluate the satis-faction rate of the pedagogically implied difficulty orderings. Specifically, we calculate the proportion of subtask pairs satisfying bj,k < b j,k −1 for scaf-folding after struggle ( Ω1) and bj,k > b j,k −1 for pushing for rigor ( Ω2). As shown in Table 6, the relative-order regu-larization substantially improves the consistency between predicted difficulties and pedagogical or-dering constraints. Without regularization ( λ = 0 ), the overall satisfaction rate is only 0.699. Increas-ing λ to 1 raises this rate to 0.899, with improve-ments for both scaffolding after struggle (0.697 to 0.898) and pushing for rigor (0.756 to 0.911). This result indicates that the regularization effectively encourages the difficulty predictor to capture the relative mathematical demands reflected in tutors’ pedagogical moves. While increasing λ further improves order satis-faction, reaching 0.914 at λ = 10 , it leads to worse difficulty prediction performance, suggesting that excessive regularization may compromise absolute difficulty estimation. Overall, these results demon-strate that our proposed relative-order regulariza-tion effectively aligns learned task difficulties with the pedagogically implied difficulty orderings in tutoring dialogues, providing better-calibrated diffi-culty estimates that benefit downstream predictions when applied at a moderate strength.

F LLM Tutor Benchmarking 

We now provide an example of how URF-QD opens door for many learning analytics tasks. In this preliminary example, we investigate how the zone of proximal development (Chaiklin et al., 2003) takes effect in tutoring dialogues: by study-ing the relationship between tutor-posed task diffi-culty and student learning outcomes, we can study what types of tasks during scaffolding are the most beneficial. Using these findings, we can create a benchmark to evaluate the pedagogical capabilities of LLMs, in terms of posing scaffolding tasks at 15 the right difficulty level to facilitate student learn-ing. Such a benchmark would complement exist-ing benchmarks such as MathTutorBench (Macina et al., 2025) and TutorMoments (Zhang et al., 2026), which do not use principled psychometric models and only rely on LLM-as-a-judge. 

Task difficulty and next question correctness 

We revisit the Eedi dialogues using the previ-ously fitted, five-fold student-disjoint dynamic 1PL model with a LSTM and frozen text encoder. For each dialogue, for all tutor-posed subtasks, we com-pute the average gap in the estimated student abil-ity θ and the turn difficulty b. The learning out-come measure we study is the correctness of the student’s response on the first original question re-sponse after the dialogue’s recorded start, which shares at least one KC label with the dialogue and does not have an associated dialogue. This filtering yields 553 dialogues from 327 students and 514 next question responses. We perform a simple lin-ear fit, shown in Figure 4, which reveals a positive relationship between next question response out-comes and θ − b, with slope 0.613 and a p-value of 0.001 . Therefore, empirical data suggests that larger gaps, corresponding to easier subtasks rel-ative to the student’s ability, are associated with higher subsequent response correctness. 

Human versus LLM tutor tasks With this find-ing, we now perform a comparison between hu-man tutors and LLM tutors to examine how they tend to pose subtasks during the dialogue. For each subtask, we prompt GPT-5.4 with the orig-inal problem and preceding dialogue to generate an appropriate next tutoring task and a standalone rewrite. We then estimate the task’s difficulty with the trained difficulty encoder. This analysis results in 2,994 paired human tutor-LLM tutor subtask comparisons across 916 dialogues. Figure 5 visual-izes average turn-averaged θ − b for the 21 human tutors with at least 20 such comparisons done. We have an interesting finding: all but one human tutor poses easier subtasks than the LLM tutor, on aver-age. Substituting this result into the analysis above, this comparison yields an advantage for human tu-tor vs. GPT-5.4 of 1.11 percentage points, with a p-value of 0.006 . We leave the investigation of all LLMs and different pedagogical prompts to future work. 

Potential usage This simple analysis shows that our framework URF-QD enables many detailed analysis into tutoring strategies; by associating each tutor-posed task with an IRT difficulty, we can leverage adaptive testing (Wainer et al., 2000) tech-niques to study the tradeoff between tutors posing tasks to pinpoint the student’s error vs. delivering targeted feedback. More importantly, we will be able to build the world’s first outcome -based tu-toring benchmark, which explicitly relates LLMs’ tutoring behavior with student learning outcomes.
