Abstract. Indonesia’s fragmented geography and uneven infrastructure pose persistent challenges for adopting new educational technologies. While national initiatives aim to mainstream AI in K–12 schools by 2025, policy readiness does not automatically mean teachers are ready for the classroom. This study surveys Indonesian K–12 teachers to ex-amine: (1) their understanding of AI, (2) how they and their students use AI, and (3) their views on the acceptability of AI in learning activ-ities. Through semi-automated thematic analysis, we identify common misconceptions and use cases. Our findings reveal a notable mismatch: while teachers find AI use in education acceptable and support it for the sake of efficiency, they remain fully aware of the risks to learning depth and academic honesty. We also find that this high level of acceptance is currently coupled with a poor understanding of how AI actually works. 

Keywords: AI Misconception · LLMs · Pedagogy · Indonesia. 

# 1 Introduction 

Global educational landscape is undergoing a paradigm shift driven by the rapid adoption of AI technologies. International guidelines and policy recommenda-tions increasingly stress that realizing AI’s educational benefits depends on sys-tem readiness, particularly, the teacher’s capacity, equitable infrastructure, and governance [8, 19]. However, in many middle-income and structurally diverse ed-ucation systems, AI adoption is advancing on ambitious policy timelines while teacher preparation and other enabling conditions remain uneven across regions and school sectors [14]. This mismatch creates a key implementation risk: self-reported "readiness" may not correspond to classroom-relevant understanding and judgment, limiting the ability to target professional development effectively or anticipate downstream pedagogical harms. Moreover, without sustained sup-port for teacher AI literacy, gaps in preparedness can widen existing digital arXiv:2610.26758v1 [cs.HC] 19 Oct 2026 2 A.F. Aji et al. 

divides rather than reduce them [4]. In this study, we refer to AI literacy as the teachers’ demonstrated conceptual understanding of what AI systems, includ-ing Large Language Models (LLM)-based tools, can and cannot do, and their ability to make informed pedagogical decisions about how AI should be used in classroom practice. Indonesia provides a salient case of these broader dynamics. The transition is being accelerated by aggressive government mandates, including the National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2020–2045 [12] and the 2025 Academic Draft on Coding and AI Education [11]. These policy frameworks seek to integrate AI and coding into the national curriculum beginning in the 2025–2026 academic year. As this deadline approaches, a critical question remains: Are Indonesian teachers prepared for this pedagogical shift? 

Indonesia’s geographical and socioeconomic diversity pose structural barriers to uniform technology adoption. Infrastructure gaps constrain national readiness: approximately 65% of schools reportedly lack stable internet and 35% operate without reliable electricity [1]. Systemic disparities further influence teacher ca-pacity, as urban educators typically access better compensation and professional development than those in rural regions [6]. Consequently, opportunities to un-derstand and ethically evaluate AI are likely uneven across regions. As AI adoption intensifies, it remains unclear how teachers will navigate com-plex pedagogical decisions given uncertainties regarding their own AI conception and literacy. Research highlights risks of over-reliance, including reduced criti-cal thinking [13, 16, 25]. Because teachers’ conceptions determine how tools are operationalized, a lack of understanding regarding AI capabilities may reinforce passive learning rather than critical engagement. To address this, we survey In-donesian educators’ readiness for AI adoption. While utilizing Indonesian data, the measurement approach and readiness tensions identified remain relevant to other systems facing similar constraints. The preliminary study is guided by the following research questions: 

RQ.1 : What (mis)conceptions do K-12 Indonesian teachers have towards AI? 

RQ.2 : What are the practical use cases of AI among K-12 Indonesian teachers? 

RQ.3 : How do teacher perceive associated benefits and risks of AI in education? 

RQ.4 : What are the teachers’ pedagogical stances regarding the acceptability of student AI adoption in learning activities? 

# 2 Background and Related Works 

Indonesia’s diverse K-12 system is governed by a national framework shaping institutional provision and teacher support. The National Education System Law (Law No. 20/2003) [20] permits multiple provider types (public and pri-vate), creating variation in curricula and resourcing. Teacher roles also vary; Law No. 14/2005 [21] defines teachers as professional educators, yet employment ranges from state positions (ASN) to non-ASN hires, impacting incentives and professional development access [22]. Despite national funding like the School AI Misconception and Adoption Among Indonesian K-12 Teachers 3

Operational Assistance grant (BOS), infrastructure differs by locality, affecting technological readiness. These structural differences create a measurement chal-lenge: since opportunities to practice AI pedagogy are uneven, assessment must distinguish perceived readiness from classroom-relevant competence. Against this backdrop, the government has begun scaling teacher capacity in AI. In 2025, the Ministry reported deploying 2,915 facilitators to train 62,898 teachers across 53,568 schools. While an important step, this initiative under-scores the gap between policy ambition and scale: Indonesia has 3,857,718 K–12 teachers across 276,415 schools. Given this size, accurately measuring AI liter-acy is essential for targeting professional development and monitoring whether training translates into competence. Most evidence on Indonesian teachers’ AI literacy relies on self-report in-struments, which capture perceptions but are limited proxies for competence. Self-reports are vulnerable to bias and inconsistent interpretations, particularly given uneven AI exposure. Recent studies exemplify this. Drajati et al. (2025) [7] surveyed 176 EFL teachers using translated Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS) items [28] and interviews; they found teachers least confident in using AI but confident in recognizing misuse, alongside resource constraints. Similarly, Batubara et al. (2025) [2] examined elementary teachers’ readiness using adapted self-efficacy scales [28] [9] and open-ended responses, reporting high perceptions but lower practical readiness, with concerns regarding privacy and bias. Prior studies provide useful signals about teachers’ perceived readiness for AI, but offer less insight into how teachers articulate their understanding of AI and its classroom use. Under pressure to adopt AI, fixed-response survey answers may reflect aspiration rather than how teachers actually describe AI, reported uses, and acceptable classroom boundaries. Our study addresses this gap by using open-ended items and task-specific pedagogical judgments to elicit how teachers understand AI concepts and justify classroom uses. This approach allows us to examine the substance of teachers’ expressed understanding, not only their confidence ratings. These findings provide a foundation for designing differentiated professional development, evaluating training efficacy, and helping policymakers target support. 

# 3 Methods 

3.1 Participants Recruitment 

We recruited participants through our professional word-of-mouth networks, reaching out directly to K–12 schools (elementary, junior high, and high school) across Indonesia to build an initial pool of potential teacher participants. At each school, a contact person circulated a study advertisement and an online registra-tion via Google Form through which teachers could submit their interest in the study. The form briefly describe that we are recruiting teachers to study how AI being adopted within their teaching-learning activities and they are being asked to fill in several demographic informations: sex, age, subject they taught, education level, employment status, school’s sector, and region. 4 A.F. Aji et al. 

To ensure more balanced representation across different socioeconomic back-grounds of teachers, we employed quota sampling when screening participants from the pool. Specifically, we set quotas for sex, school level, school region, and employment status. However, because the pool of registrants from outside West Java was limited, we did not apply quota constraints for that subgroup, instead, we invited participants from outside West Java via random sampling from the available pool. Screened participants were then invited to the study and received IDR 50,000 compensation for an approximately one-hour session, which is twice the average hourly rate in Indonesia. 

3.2 Survey Design 

We designed a mixed-format survey of 13 items spanning open-ended responses, multiple-response selections, and Likert-style ratings, divided into 3 sections. These sections aimed to probe teachers’ AI (mis)conceptions and literacy (Q1– Q4), reported AI use in teaching-learning activities (Q5–Q12), and pedagogical judgments about the acceptability of students’ AI usage in learning activities (Q13). Our survey items are in 5.

Survey Items Rationale For Q1-Q4, we adapted and localized 3 open-ended questions from [15] that aimed to elicit the teacher’s initial conceptions of AI in a minimally leading way. We further add a new question (Q2) to explicitly probe the teacher’s mental models how popular LLM works. In Q5–Q6, we elicited concrete, self-reported examples of how teachers and students use AI in every-day learning contexts. Q7–Q8 then captured teachers’ stance toward AI use by teachers versus students, along with the reasons underlying each stance as well as the conditions under which teachers would change their position. In Q9–Q12, we asked teachers to articulate perceived positive and negative impacts of AI use for teachers and students. Finally, we probed teachers’ pedagogical decision about when student AI use is acceptable by eliciting task and stage-specific judg-ments rather than general attitudes. In Q13, teachers indicated which learning tasks they believe students use AI for. For each selected task, we asked teachers to rate the appropriateness of student AI use on a structured scale and to fur-ther evaluate AI use across fine-grained stages within that task. This branching, stage-level design was intended to probe teachers’ boundaries of AI usage in a format that supports comparison across various learning activities.

After the teachers completed the survey, we invited them for an unstructured interview, providing feedback that informed several revisions. 3.3 Data Collection and Analysis 

We conducted the data collection from September to November 2025. Each screened participant received a study invitation link, directing them to our online survey. Survey responses were stored in a tabular format, where each row corresponds to a participant and columns include: (1) item-level responses (open-ended text responses and structured selections and Likert ratings) and (2) demographic information, yielding a mixed-type tabular data. 

Analysis Overview We analyzed the dataset using a mixed-methods work-flow. For structured items, we summarized the responses descriptively (e.g., fre-quencies and distributions) and used these variables to contextualize patterns in the qualitative findings. For open-ended items, we applied a human-in-the-loop NLP workflow to identify and label the major themes (topics) participants expressed, combining topic modeling with researcher review and LLM-assisted rubric development. 

Human-in-the-Loop NLP Workflow Following prior work on LLM-assisted topic modeling [29], we analyzed each open-ended question using a three-step workflow: (1) topic generation with Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) [3], (2) 

topic refinement (collapse/split) with LLM support and human review [29], and (3) topic representation via an LLM-generated coding rubric [10]. We first ran LDA with 2–10 topics per question and selected the topic count using the Cv

coherence score [23]. We then reviewed the 20 most representative responses per topic to draft provisional labels. Next, ChatGPT (GPT-5.2) was used in a human-in-the-loop process to improve interpretability by proposing and validat-ing merges/splits. After finalizing the topic set, ChatGPT generated operational definitions and a coding rubric for each label. Finally, GPT-5 mini applied the rubric at scale to assign a label to each response; researchers iteratively audited representative outputs and refined rubrics as needed for alignment. The average agreement rate between the LLM-assigned codes and human judgment, com-puted on 10% sample of the data across questions, is 95.6%; showing strong alignment between LLM and human judgments based on the rubric 5.

Filtering AI-generated Responses We hypothesize that AI-generated re-sponses are the main concern threatening our data quality. Since automatic detectors are unreliable for short, non-English text [30], we used a two-stage human-in-the-loop filter informed by evidence that experienced LLM users bet-ter recognize AI-written text [24]. We first used a RegEx-based exclusion-first 6 A.F. Aji et al. 

heuristic to surface candidate cases. The script returned no alert when a response showed cues that we treated as more characteristic of human-written Indonesian survey text, including reduplication such as “orang2”, abbreviations such as “dll”, “dst”, or “dkk”, non-standard spacing around punctuation, lowercase list items, lowercase or variably capitalized forms of “ai”/“ChatGPT”, lowercase sentence starts, or missing sentence-final punctuation. Only responses without those cues were then checked for red flags, namely length greater than 400 characters, dou-ble newlines, numbered or bulleted list structure, non-ASCII characters or emo-jis, the formal pronoun “anda”, and parenthetical explanations such as “Artificial Intelligence (AI)”. Two authors then independently reviewed flagged cases, and we excluded a response only when both judged it to be AI-generated.

Opposition and Perceived Risks This section explores the factors driving opposition to AI integration in education and the perceived risks respondents associated with its use. While support for AI was largely motivated by effi-ciency, opposition was most commonly framed around the detrimental effects of over-reliance. This over-dependency is perceived to foster complacency and skill degradation, negative outcomes that are intrinsically linked to the efficiency gains AI provides. In addition, while teachers value AI assistance in their daily activities, they expressed caution about the risk of inaccurate outputs. To understand the specific grounds for resistance, we asked Q11: “I will not support AI use by teachers if...” . Responses clustered into six themes. Given growing concerns about AI’s ed-ucational effects, pedagogical integrity should take priority over mere efficiency: using AI to produce direct answers can undermine learning, and even ideation requires careful use to avoid harms teachers identified. 

# 5 Discussion 

Our findings reveal a disconnect between AI adoption and AI literacy among Indonesian educators. While AI has rapidly penetrated the teaching profession, underlying conceptual models remain superficial or misinformed. This gap cre-ates a critical tension because teachers are increasingly reliant on a “black box” they lack the literacy to evaluate. This literacy lag is particularly evident in the prevalence of high level or vague descriptions of AI mechanics, which suggests that while the tools are being utilized, their internal logic remains opaque. Paradoxically, while educators are acutely aware of systemic risks such as academic dishonesty, cognitive over-reliance, and student laziness, their support for AI integration remains remarkably high. This approval persists even when teachers perceive student usage as primarily a shortcut for direct answer seeking. Such a stance suggests that the perceived utility of AI in managing workloads currently outweighs traditional pedagogical caution. This results in a permis-sive environment where AI is supported across most use cases, despite a clear recognition of the potential long term harms. 

Interpreting AI Literacy in Context These findings do not suggest teachers need specialist AI expertise; rather, they require the functional AI literacy to judge when AI supports learning versus when it requires verification or risks undermining the learning process. When teachers have inaccurate mental models, such as treating ChatGPT as a search engine, it negatively shapes classroom policies, monitoring practices, and the normalization of AI-supported activities. 

AI-Use in Surveys We found several respondents who used AI in the questions. In one of the questions, their answer included the phrase “As an AI language model developed by Google,” which shows that they did not double-check the response and simply copied and pasted it. Another respondent had most of their responses flagged as suspicious, with the remaining two questions confirmed as AI-generated. One of these included markdown formatting which serves no pur-pose on our survey platform. We find this result interesting and ironic, as it anecdotally illustrates the negative side effects of AI that the respondents them-selves confirmed, such as over-reliance, laziness, and the need for instantaneous results, including a failure to check for correctness for the sake of work efficiency. 

Recommendation Based on the misconceptions identified, we recommend a more practice-oriented approach to AI literacy and governance in Indonesian AI Misconception and Adoption Among Indonesian K-12 Teachers 13 

K–12 schools. Although the Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education pro-vides initial training, many teachers still hold inaccurate mental models (e.g., treating ChatGPT as a search engine). At the same time, teachers are not un-aware of risks: many explicitly mentioned harms such as dependency and dis-honesty, yet some still support AI for direct answer seeking and many continue to support AI for report writing, reflecting how strongly perceived efficiency and convenience shape adoption decisions. To close this gap, AI literacy programs should be longer and directly address misconceptions about accuracy, hallucinations, bias, and privacy [27]. Train-ing should also require demonstrated responsible use through authentic tasks (e.g., producing an AI-supported lesson plans, documenting verification steps, and explaining how over-reliance will be mitigated), rather than relying on self-reported readiness so that learning quality remains the priority [5, 18, 26]. In parallel, guidance should be consolidated in a simple portal and written as short, operational documents with concrete “do/don’t" examples [5, 17], supported by follow-up checks such as scenario-based assessments or micro-certifications to verify classroom-relevant competence [26, 27]. At the school level, clear rules are needed: teachers should state AI policies in the syllabus (permissible purposes, disclosure requirements, and prohibited uses [5]), and students should document their AI process to protect integrity. Schools should also form AI committees to adapt national guidance to local contexts and monitor risks [26], treating AI governance as an ongoing process rather than a one-time document [27]. Finally, given the persistent appeal of AI’s convenience and the broad support for its adoption in Indonesia, even in high-risk use cases like answer seeking and report writing, teacher training in AI should shift from general awareness to intensive risk mitigation. Without explicit preparation for managing these trade-offs, strong support for AI may translate into classroom practices that inadvertently compromises pedagogical integrity. 

Limitations These findings should be interpreted with several limitations. The sample is not nationally representative, with limited data from private-school teachers outside West Java. Additionally, our recruitment method may introduce selection bias toward technology-engaged educators. Furthermore, the study re-lies entirely on self-reported survey responses rather than observed classroom behavior. Finally, our analysis is limited to descriptive patterns and does not include inferential subgroup comparisons. 

# 6 Conclusion 

In this study, we examined the AI conception, use cases, and pedagogical stances of 132 Indonesian K–12 educators through a mixed-methods survey. We found that while adoption and support for AI are high, actual technical understanding implied from the teacher’s conceptions of AI remains superficial, with widespread misconceptions regarding AI and LLM functionality. Furthermore, teachers ex-pressed a clear awareness of the impact on student critical thinking and academic honesty, though most teacher supports AI use including by students. We suggest 14 A.F. that future professional development and policy frameworks must move beyond general awareness toward performance-based training that directly addresses these conceptual gaps and provides concrete strategies for risk mitigation.
