# MAVE: A Multi-Agent Framework for Grounded Pedagogical Feedback from Online Class Videos 

Qingyue Chen 1 Huaiyuan Yao 1 Wanpeng Xu 1 Varun Shourie 1

Qianwen Wang 2 Hua Wei 11 Arizona State University 2 University of Minnesota Timely pedagogical feedback is difficult to scale in online classes, where long and noisy videos make student responses hard to locate and automated systems often produce generic advice weakly grounded in actual classroom behavior. The key challenge is to connect the instructional goal of a specific lesson segment with temporally aligned multimodal evidence of student responses, and then translate the observed gap into an actionable teaching move. We introduce MAVE (Multi-Agents for Video-based Educational Feedback), a multi-agent framework for grounded pedagogical feedback from online-class videos. MAVE decomposes feedback generation into four roles: the Curator structures the lesson and infers expected student activity; the Observer extracts aligned speech, visual, and interaction evidence; the Assessor estimates the activity supported by student behavior; and the Advisor generates gap-conditioned teaching recommendations. To evaluate this capability, we introduce MAVEBench, a benchmark designed to measure whether feedback systems can ground diagnoses in multimodal student evidence, and generate actionable pedagogical feedback. MAVEBench contains 173 knowledge-point segments from approximately 20 hours of authentic online-class videos across STEM and Humanities, together with comprehensive simulation. Experiments show that MAVE outperforms direct prompting, Reflexion, and TextGrad in diagnostic correctness, instructional alignment, and actionability. Our code is available at https://github.com/DaRL-GenAI/MAVE 

1. Introduction 

Online instruction increasingly takes place in screen-sharing-heavy classrooms, where instructors present materials while student responses are distributed across compressed video tiles, chat, and other fragmented channels. This distribution makes it difficult to monitor how students respond to instructional activities in real time [1]. In such settings, student responses are often delayed, muted, or only partially observable, making it difficult to distinguish passive listening from productive but hidden activity. Although expert supervisors can convert classroom observations into actionable pedagogical feedback [2], such observation does not scale across large courses. As a result, instructors receive little interpretable evidence about when student engagement breaks down, why specific instructional activities fail, or how interaction patterns could be improved [3, 4]; instructional refinement remains anecdotal and cannot be replicated across cohorts. We therefore study the task of generating instructor-facing pedagogical feedback from online-class videos. As illustrated in Figure 1, the input is a local instructional segment with aligned student-side evidence, and the output is a teaching-oriented diagnosis and concrete instructional move grounded in that evidence. For one instructional segment, MAVE places the intended ICAP target and the state supported by aligned student evidence on the ordinal ICAP scale, measures the intended and observed gap, and returns a time-local teaching move with an observable success criterion. Arrows indicate data flow rather than causal effects. Meeting this requirement is hard for three reasons, as shown in Fig. 2. First, classroom videos are long and noisy: extended exposition, off-task movement, filler speech, and fragmented participation make en-gagement cues sparse and segment-local [5]. Second, student engagement is multimodal and temporally dis-tributed: note-taking, delayed verbal responses, gaze shifts, and discussion occur asynchronously across modalities. Third, the feedback gap goes unmeasured: existing engagement-analysis systems collapse each segment to a single global label or affect category, and LLM-based feedback approaches rely on uncon-strained self-reflection [6, 7] or rubric scoring [8, 9], so neither compares what the lesson aimed to elicit with what students actually showed. Current systems therefore cannot tell whether low engagement reflects expected passive learning, missing instructional scaf-folding, or a mismatch between intent and response. Our central premise is that pedagogical feedback must be grounded in real student responses. LLM judges and rubric scores can help evaluate feedback quality, but they should not be the sole basis for gener-ating feedback. Otherwise, a system may learn to pro-duce recommendations that satisfy rubric language while remaining weakly connected to what students actually did, creating a reward-hacking failure mode. For instructor-facing feedback, a recommendation is useful only when it can be traced to observable student behavior and translated into a concrete teaching move. We therefore frame automated pedagogical feed-back as a grounded reasoning chain linking instructional intent , observed student evidence , and pedagogical action .To ground student-state modeling in educational theory, we adopt the ICAP framework [10] provides the shared scale for this chain: each segment can be assigned an intended cognitive demand and an observed student-response state as Passive, Active, Constructive, or Interactive. The intended–observed gap makes the problem diagnosable, but diagnosis alone is not enough. Useful feedback must also select an intervention that matches the direction of the gap, cite the evidence behind it, and provide an observable success criterion. To operationalize this chain, we introduce MAVE , a multi-agent framework for actionable pedagogical feedback from authentic online-class videos. The Curator structures long videos into instructional segments and infers the intended cognitive-engagement target; the Observer extracts time-aligned speech, visual, and interaction evidence; the Assessor estimates the student-response state and the intended–observed gap; and the Advisor converts this gap into an evidence-grounded teaching recommendation. This decomposition addresses the main challenges of online-class feedback: long and noisy videos, scattered multimodal evidence, and the lack of an explicit feedback gap.

2. Related Work 

2.1 AI in Educational Analytics and Feedback 

Automated classroom analysis has used computer vision, audio processing, and multimodal sensing to infer attention and behavior from faces, gaze, posture, physiological signals, and classroom context [11, 12, 13]. Engagement is typically formulated as multiclass classification or regression [14, 15]; although ordinal prediction is well studied elsewhere [16], classroom pipelines rarely use that structure to represent uncertainty. Systems such as EmotionCues [17], Zheng et al. [18], and Gao et al. [19] provide behavioral or domain-specific signals, while adaptive learning methods use explicit preferences or scalar rewards [20, 21, 22]. These approaches provide useful but isolated engagement signals, without linking them to the instructional purpose. They therefore do not explain whether student responses match what the instructor intended to elicit, or translate that mismatch into an executable teaching move. MAVE addresses this gap by aligning the instructional segment, the student-response window, and the evidence supporting the ICAP label. 

2.2 Multi-Agent LLMs for Feedback Generation 

LLM agents can be composed through role decomposition, debate, and cross-verification [23, 24]. Reflexion [6] and TextGrad [7] refine outputs through self-critique or textual gradients, and educational applications use LLMs to assess teaching quality and propose instructional suggestions [25, 26, 8, 9]. Their optimization signal, however, usually comes from critiques or rubric scores, with limited grounding in student responses.

3. MAVE Benchmark 

3.1 Dataset 

To rigorously evaluate MAVE in realistic environments, we introduce MAVEBench —a representative multi-modal benchmark bridging authentic classroom dynamics with structured pedagogical assessment. Collected from diverse open-source online lectures, MAVEBench enforces stringent selection criteria: structured peda-gogical sequences, recoverable student visual tiles, and clear speech interactions. The curated benchmark offers broad ecological representativeness, spanning both STEM and Humanities across four distinct edu-cational tiers (K–12, higher education, adult/vocational training, and teacher professional development). This heterogeneous corpus encapsulates diverse teaching formats, noise distributions, and student response modalities. As illustrated in Figure 3, MAVEBench exhibits balanced domain coverage, rich multimodal volume, and diverse instructional durations. 

Statistics. MAVEBench provides 173 KPs totaling 1,025 minutes of retained instructional segments, and contains about 5.8K cleaned frames and 110K face crops. The STEM samples contribute 106 KPs, while the Humanities samples contribute 67 KPs. KPs are LLM-segmented and converted into contiguous KP bundles for the controlled transition study. Recoverable class sizes average ≈18 students per video (range 6–30), i.e., 

≈360 student–video instances (students are not re-identified across videos). The long, noisy context and sparse A/C/I behaviors make MAVEBench a challenging testbed for evidence-grounded feedback. 

Multimodal preprocessing. Each video is transcribed with ASR and segmented by an LLM into semantically coherent KPs with topics and temporal boundaries; these KPs are the atomic units for intended/observed ICAP comparison in MAVE. In parallel, we sample frames, detect Zoom layouts (gallery / sidebar / spotlight), and crop per-student face windows. All facial and vocal identifiers are anonymized. Segmentation is constrained to cover the retained instructional timeline with contiguous, non-overlapping intervals. A boundary is placed at a change in knowledge objective or instructional activity rather than at a fixed number of frames. The aligned bundle for KP k contains its transcript, temporal interval, sampled classroom views, and recoverable

student crops. Consequently, all methods are evaluated on the same evidence bundle and cannot obtain an advantage from choosing easier boundaries or a different temporal context. 

Visual data cleaning. Online classroom screenshots contain substantial non-behavioral noise. We therefore apply a dedicated cleaning stage before engagement analysis: (i) black frames —empty or near-black student tiles with no visible person; (ii) icon-only frames —tiles showing only avatars, profile icons, or static placehold-ers without a real face; and (iii) unreadable PPT / slide content —frames dominated by presentation slides or screen-share regions whose text/graphics cannot be reliably recognized as student behavior evidence. Excluded items are recorded in per-video black/icon/PPT exclusion lists and removed from the Observer’s visual evidence stream, so ICAP assessment is grounded on observable student activity. 

Benchmark unit and quality control. The KP is the benchmark unit because it is the shortest interval for which an instructional objective and its student response can be compared meaningfully. Segment boundaries are checked for temporal continuity and coverage, and the associated transcript is retained alongside sampled student views. Preprocessing records why evidence is removed, so an excluded avatar or slide is not silently interpreted as disengagement. This design separates three sources of uncertainty: whether the relevant moment was localized, whether student behavior was recoverable, and whether that behavior supports an ICAP label. Downstream agents can therefore abstain on a missing modality while still using the remaining evidence. MAVEBench is intended for segment-level feedback evaluation. The benchmark deliberately preserves missingness. Camera-off tiles, occlusion, unreadable slides, and ambiguous speakers are recorded as unavailable evidence, not converted into Passive labels. Class-level proportions use only recoverable student windows and are accompanied by the recoverable count. This prevents a method from appearing confident simply because a modality failed. Because the videos are public but contain identifiable classroom behavior, released artifacts use anonymized identifiers and derived evidence rather than presenting identity as an analytic variable. 

3.2 Evaluation Protocol 

Unlike conventional engagement analysis, pedagogical feedback is not valued only for predicting a student state. A useful recommendation must correctly diagnose the intended–observed gap, remain aligned with the instructional objective, and translate the diagnosis into an executable teaching move. We therefore evaluate MAVE with two complementary protocols: expert rubric evaluation and controlled student-agent simulation. 

Expert Rubric Evaluation. The primary evaluation asks expert raters to score one evidence-grounded recommendation for each KP. All method outputs are anonymized and presented in a shared format. Experts rate each output on three 1–5 dimensions: (i) ICAP Accuracy —whether the feedback identifies the observed cognitive-engagement state and the intended–observed gap; (ii) Alignment —whether the proposed intervention preserves the KP objective and targets the requested transition; and (iii) Actionability —whether the feedback provides concrete teacher language, timing, or steps that can be executed within the segment. Generic advice such as ”increase engagement” cannot receive a high Actionability score unless it specifies both the activity and the observable evidence criterion. 4. Method 

Overview. As shown in figure 4, we formulate automated teaching feedback as a discrepancy reasoning problem between the instructor’s intended ICAP objective and the students’ observed ICAP state. Given a raw online classroom video V, MAVE decomposes the problem into four coordinated agents: the Curator 

(pedagogical structuring and target inference), the Observer (multimodal evidence extraction), the Assessor 

(ICAP state estimation), and the Advisor (feedback generation), detailed in the subsections below. 

Task Formulation. Formally, let the classroom video be partitioned into K instructional segments {V k}Kk=1

and let m ∈ { 1, . . . , Mk} index the recoverable student windows in segment k. For each segment, MAVE estimates (1) an intended ICAP target ck ∈ Y , where Y = {P, A, C, I}, (2) per-student observed states 

qk,m ∈ Y , (3) optional Passive-student engagement levels ek,m ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3 } in detail mode, and (4) a feedback text Fk that explains the discrepancy between ck and the observed classroom distribution. The Assessor is responsible only for measuring student cognitive engagement. It first routes each student– segment pair through complementary linguistic and visual classifiers, and it invokes the finer engagement analysis only for students ultimately assigned to the Passive state in detail mode. The Advisor is responsible only for acting on the pedagogical gap: given the intended level, the distribution of observed ICAP states, and the passive-student engagement summary, it synthesizes feedback. This separation keeps diagnostic measurement independent from recommendation generation.

4.1 Curator: Pedagogical Structuring 

The Curator reconstructs the pedagogical structure of the lecture from the raw classroom stream, which aims to infer the cognitively appropriate ICAP objective. In other words, the agent estimates what level of student cognitive engagement a particular knowledge unit should ideally elicit according to pedagogical principles. 

Instructional parsing. As an auxiliary tool, the Curator first invokes a Transformer-based automatic speech recognition (ASR) module to obtain a timestamp-aligned transcript T = {(wn, tn)}Nn=1, where wn denotes the recognized token and tn denotes its temporal boundary. The transcript is aligned with the classroom timeline and grouped into contiguous instructional windows. 

Instructional unit construction. Based on the transcript and temporal structure, the Curator partitions the lecture into pedagogically coherent instructional units Uk = ( Wk, τk), where Wk denotes the instructional content within the segment and τk = [ tsk, tek] denotes its temporal interval. Each instructional unit corresponds to a relatively self-contained knowledge delivery episode. This segmentation establishes a stable temporal abstraction for downstream multimodal reasoning and enables direct alignment between instructional context and student responses. 

ICAP target inference. For each instructional unit Uk, the Curator analyzes the instructional content, dis-course structure, and pedagogical interaction patterns to infer the cognitively appropriate ICAP target 

ck ∈ { P, A, C, I}. Instead of predicting what the teacher subjectively intended, the Curator estimates what form of cognitive engagement the knowledge component should ideally promote. For example, factual exposition may correspond to Passive or Active engagement, while conceptual synthesis, reasoning prompts, or collaborative problem solving are expected to induce Constructive or Interactive engagement. The inferred ICAP target serves as the pedagogical reference state for the subsequent discrepancy analysis. In practice, feedback should specify a realizable next step rather than demand maximal interaction everywhere. We therefore use Constructive (C) and Interactive (I) states as actionable targets: Constructive engagement asks each learner to generate an explanation, prediction, or solution beyond the presented material, whereas Interactive engagement additionally requires responsive knowledge construction across learners. The target combines conceptual demand, lesson position, and evidence of readiness. Concretely, the rule-based gate forces an Interactive target when Interactive behavior is already present or the recoverable Constructive coverage is at least 50%; otherwise it forces a Constructive target. A soft early-stage safeguard overrides Interactive for the first 30% of a lecture, when exposition and orientation are still dominant. The Curator then supplies the target-specific rationale and question, so an Interactive target cannot degenerate into a generic recommendation to “add discussion.” 

4.2 Observer: Learner Feedback Modeling 

Rather than predicting engagement labels directly, the Observer converts multimodal classroom signals into temporally aligned natural-language evidence for downstream pedagogical reasoning. 

Audio interaction parsing. For each instructional unit Uk, the Observer invokes an automatic speech recognition (ASR) module to obtain timestamp-aligned classroom transcripts. Based on speaker turns and interaction context, the Observer further separates student utterances from the instructor’s speech, yielding a student-side interaction transcript Ak = {(si, ti)}, where si denotes a student utterance and ti denotes

The Observer describes what is visible or audible but does not infer ICAP; this provenance boundary makes later decisions auditable and limits behavioral over-interpretation. 

4.3 Assessor: ICAP State Estimation 

The Assessor estimates an ICAP state for each student and instructional unit rather than assigning one label to the whole classroom. Its outputs are consumed by the Advisor and are not used to generate recommendations directly. We use a conservative routing rule with three complementary prompts. A discourse prompt returns “Passive/Active” when no student speech is detected, “Constructive” when one student produces constructive output, and “Interactive” only when at least two students contribute constructive turns with evidence of turn taking. A separate visual-action detector resolves the “Passive/Active” case to Active when overt learning-related motor activity (e.g., writing, drawing, or manipulating a learning object) is observed; otherwise the student remains Passive. Thus, a silent student is not promoted to Constructive or Interactive merely because a face is visible or a posture is stable. 

Detail-mode Passive monitoring. When the routed state is P and detail mode is enabled, the Observer’s cleaned student crop is analyzed at a finer temporal resolution. Frames are sampled at fixed intervals, aggregated into one-minute factual narratives, and grouped into non-overlapping three-minute windows. For student m in window t, an engagement judge selects an ordinal anchor am,t ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3 } based on observable evidence and makes binary plausibility judgments for neighboring levels. Specifically, am,t = 0 indicates frequent looking away/down or obvious inattentiveness; am,t = 1 indicates weak or unstable attention, such as inconsistent orientation or posture; am,t = 2 indicates generally forward and stable attention with only minor lapses; and am,t = 3 indicates stronger observable engagement cues, such as nodding, leaning in, speaking, or active hand movements without sustained distraction. These levels refine engagement within 

the Passive state and do not alter the student’s ICAP classification.

The detail branch follows a fixed evidence path. The cleaner first removes black, icon-only, and presentation-dominated frames. The visual narrator then summarizes up to six sampled frames from each one-minute interval in factual prose, and consecutive summaries are grouped into non-overlapping three-minute windows. The judge reads the three summaries, selects one anchor level, and returns binary plausibility judgments for neighboring ordinal levels. A two-level deviation cannot be accepted when its corresponding one-level deviation is rejected. The prompt instructs the judge to use only visible behavior, select one 0–3 anchor from the overall three-minute tendency, avoid defaulting to level 2, and return the anchor, four binary deviation judgments, and a brief evidence-grounded reason as strict JSON. Stable forward orientation or strong focus cues support level 3, whereas recurring distraction supports levels 0 or 1. Aggregating per-student decisions also exposes coverage : a segment may contain one strong constructive answer while most of the class remains Passive. MAVE reports both the highest supported ICAP behavior and the fraction of recoverable students at each state, avoiding the common error of treating a vocal minority as evidence that the objective was achieved class-wide.

The gap is directional and coverage-aware. If the target is Constructive but the class is predominantly Passive, the first priority is to make thinking externally observable with an individual prediction or explanation; peer interaction is not yet required. If several learners reach Constructive but coverage remains low, the Advisor expands participation before raising the cognitive level. When Constructive engagement is broadly established and the target is Interactive, the recommendation instead introduces turn-taking in which students build on, challenge, or reconcile peers’ contributions. Conversely, if the target has already been reached, the Advisor preserves the successful activity and suggests only a small refinement. This ordering distinguishes increasing coverage at the current level from moving a smaller set of learners to a higher one.

For a Constructive target, the proposed question must elicit new learner-generated content without relying on peer exchange. For an Interactive target, it must require at least two learners to respond to one another. The reference answer constrains domain correctness, while citations to the transcript and student evidence constrain behavioral grounding.

5.1 Experiment Setup 

We evaluate all methods on MAVEBench, which contains 173 KP-level instructional segments from ap-proximately 20 hours of authentic online-class videos (Section 3.1). Following the evaluation protocol in Section 3.2, we report two complementary evaluations: offline expert rubric evaluation for feedback quality and controlled student-agent simulation for state transitions. For offline evaluation, expert scores are com-puted on the 173 rated KP-level outputs by five expert evaluators with university-level teaching experience. All methods receive the same anonymized evidence, KP boundaries, transcripts, output schemas, decoding settings, retry policy, and GPT-4o-mini backbone. This matched setup ensures that differences reflect agent specialization, routing, and gap-conditioned reasoning rather than differences in preprocessing or perceptual inputs. The controlled simulation uses the same 173 KPs with paired baseline/guided conditions and five random seeds; only this simulation uses GPT-5 for student-agent runs. 

5.2 Comparisons and Baselines 

Because evidence-grounded pedagogical feedback from online-class videos is a new task, there is no es-tablished education-specific baseline that directly matches our input-output setting. We therefore adapt several general feedback generation and refinement methods to this domain, while keeping the evidence, instructional context, and output schema identical across methods. We compare with a directly prompted VLM ( Prompting ), Reflexion [6], and TextGrad [7]. Each receives the same transcript, instructional context, student evidence, and output schema. Prompting tests direct evidence-to-advice generation; Reflexion adds iterative self-critique and memory; TextGrad optimizes a draft with textual feedback. The comparison thus spans one-pass, reflective, and optimization-based generation. Only MAVE explicitly separates target inference, evidence description, state estimation, and gap-conditioned advising.

Removing the Assessor causes intermediate losses of 1.31 and 1.09 points. Intended-level inference is therefore most consequential in STEM, whereas multimodal coverage is especially consequential in Humanities. Passing raw observations directly to the Advisor cannot replace an explicit, calibrated state summary. This asymmetric pattern also cautions against treating Average alone as a complete explanation: the same pipeline stage can fail differently as instructional evidence changes across domains.

5.8 Qualitative Case Study 

Figure 7 illustrates a representative Humanities segment in which the instructor explains a multi-step solution and the Curator assigns a Constructive target. The Observer finds limited student speech and infrequent note taking across the recoverable student windows; the Assessor consequently routes the segment to a predominantly Passive state. Rather than responding with the generic instruction to “increase interaction,” the Advisor localizes the gap to the intermediate reasoning step, rewrites it as an individual prediction-and-explanation prompt, supplies a scaffold, and states a reference answer against which the response can be checked. This example shows how the agent interfaces preserve a trace from visible or audible evidence, through an ICAP diagnosis, to a teacher action. Prompting offers a broad participation suggestion without tying it to the observed behavior. Reflexion improves the wording but does not identify which cognitive output is missing. TextGrad produces a more structured activity, yet parts of it are weakly connected to the local evidence. MAVE’s advantage is therefore not simply longer feedback: its recommendation identifies the target, the coverage bottleneck, and the observable response that would close the gap.

6. Discussion and Conclusion 

We presented MAVE , a multi-agent framework that grounds pedagogical feedback in ICAP theory and multimodal classroom evidence. The central idea is to represent feedback as a gap between the cognitive activity appropriate for an instructional segment and the activity supported by student evidence in that same segment. This shifts classroom analytics from aggregate descriptions of attention to a specific, actionable diagnosis of where instructional intent and student response diverge. MAVE operationalizes this idea through inspectable intermediate artifacts. The Curator constructs knowledge-point units and ICAP targets; the Observer extracts time-aligned speech and visible behavior while preserving missingness; the Assessor conservatively estimates ICAP states and, when needed, refines Passive cases with ordinal calibration; and the Advisor generates feedback from both cognitive level and classroom coverage. This staged design keeps measurement separate from recommendation, reducing the risk that fluent advice silently changes the diagnosis. It also distinguishes two pedagogically different moves: broadening participation at the current level versus moving already-constructive learners toward interaction. Across real-world STEM and Humanities lectures, MAVE achieves the highest expert ratings under matched evidence and model inputs. Ablations further show that target reference, multimodal grounding, and calibrated state estimates each provide distinct value. Under controlled student-agent simulations, MAVE’s rewrites and scaffolds induce more frequent and deeper ICAP upgrades.
