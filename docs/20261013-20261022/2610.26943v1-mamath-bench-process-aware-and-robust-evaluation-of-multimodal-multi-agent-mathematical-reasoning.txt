# Abstract 

Mathematical reasoning is a fundamental capability for ed-ucational applications of Large Language Models (LLMs), where reliable problem solving requires not only correct an-swers but also coherent and verifiable reasoning processes. Multi-agent systems (MASs) have shown growing potential in this domain by leveraging collaboration and verification among multiple LLM agents. However, existing evaluations primarily focus on the correctness of final answers in text-only settings, offering limited insight into multimodal reason-ing capabilities, the reliability of intermediate reasoning steps, and robustness against reasoning errors. To address these limitations, we introduce MAMath-Bench, a process-aware benchmark designed to evaluate the multimodal mathematical reasoning capabilities of MASs. MAMath-Bench comprises 1,003 problems spanning 11 mathematical categories, multi-ple difficulty levels, and several modalities—including text, images, and audio. It features fine-grained milestone anno-tations to evaluate systems across three dimensions: problem-solving correctness, reasoning-process reliability, and robust-ness to adversarial reasoning-chain perturbations. Our eval-uation yields three key findings. First, multi-agent collab-oration does not consistently improve final-answer accuracy over strong single LLMs. Second, collaboration can sub-stantially enhance the coverage of intermediate reasoning, but such gains may come at the cost of increased reasoning re-dundancy. Third, current systems remain vulnerable to ad-versarial reasoning errors, particularly those involving subtle structural perturbations. The dataset is available at https: //github.com/mira-ai-lab/AgentMath .

Keywords: multimodal mathematical reasoning, process-level evaluation, multi-agent systems 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) [3, 1, 9] have spurred the development of Multi-Agent Systems (MASs) [25, 13, 5] for complex problem-solving. MASs have been increasingly applied in a variety of real-world do-mains, including enterprise management [36, 41, 19], soft-ware programming [28, 47, 44], medical diagnosis [37, 49, 23] and smart education [17, 24, 40]. By coordinating multiple agents with specialized roles, such as a planner, a solver, and a verifier, MASs can address complex tasks through sequen-tial workflows [30] or debate-based interactions [4], depend-ing on task requirements. This collaborative design offers a structured framework for decomposing tasks, coordinating intermediate reasoning, and supporting iterative verification. This characteristic is particularly significant in the educational question-answering scenario [32, 46, 2], because students re-quire not only the correct final answers but also a coherent, logical, and verifiable reasoning process [20]. To evaluate the performance of MASs in educational question-answering scenarios, a substantial body of re-search [38, 15] has adopted Mathematical Question Answer-ing (MQA) as a standard benchmark, given its stringent de-mands for structured logical reasoning. However, the prevail-ing evaluation protocols typically restrict problem inputs to unimodal formats [6, 12, 8] and assess performance primarily through exact response matching [48, 45, 42]. However, ex-isting benchmarks do not fully address the evaluation require-ments of educational mathematical reasoning, as illustrated in Figure 1. We identify three key limitations. 

Limited Multimodal Coverage. In educational settings, mathematical problems are often presented through heteroge-neous forms of information, such as textual descriptions, ge-ometric diagrams, symbolic expressions, and spoken instruc-tions. Insufficient Process Evaluation. For educational applica-tions, the reasoning process is itself an important evaluation target, since a correct answer may still be supported by in-complete, redundant, or logically unreliable derivations. Eval-uating intermediate reasoning is therefore necessary to distin-guish systems that achieve similar answer accuracy but differ substantially in reasoning completeness and reliability. 

Limited Robustness Assessment. In educational scenar-ios, erroneous intermediate steps, misleading solution pro-cedures, or incorrect information from collaborating agents should not be propagated and presented as reliable explana-tions. Existing benchmarks rarely assess whether MASs can identify and handle such reasoning errors, making it difficult to characterize their reliability under imperfect intermediate conditions. To address existing limitations, we introduce MAMath-Bench, which establishes a unified evaluation protocol for sys-tematically assessing and comparing multimodal mathemati-cal reasoning capabilities across different MASs. MAMath-Bench comprises 1,003 carefully curated problems spanning text, visual, and audio modalities. Beyond evaluating final an-swers, each sample includes milestone-based reasoning anno-tations, enabling an examination of the reasoning process it-self. The benchmark includes positive subsets across three dif-ficulty levels and negative subsets constructed via five types of reasoning perturbations, supporting a three-dimensional evalu-ation covering problem-solving correctness, collaborative pro-cess reliability, and robustness to adversarial reasoning-chain perturbations. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • A multimodal benchmark for multi-agent mathemat-ical reasoning. We propose MAMath-Bench, a bench-mark that extends the evaluation of LLM-based MASs from predominantly text-based mathematical reasoning to scenarios involving heterogeneous inputs—including text, vision, and audio. It provides fine-grained annota-tions, enabling analysis at the levels of both final answers and reasoning processes. • An evaluation framework balancing reasoning pro-cesses and robustness. Moving beyond the traditional focus on final-answer accuracy, we comprehensively evaluate problem-solving correctness, the reliability of intermediate reasoning, and robustness against adversar-ial reasoning errors. • A systematic empirical analysis of multi-agent collab-oration mechanisms. Through extensive comparisons between representative single-agent LLMs and multi-agent frameworks, we find that collaboration does not necessarily yield a universal advantage in final-answer accuracy; instead, it introduces significant trade-offs among reasoning coverage, redundancy, and robustness. These findings reveal a substantial gap between answer correctness and process reliability, offering deep insights into the strengths and limitations of current collaboration mechanisms. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 MQA Datasets 

MQA benchmarks have been extensively studied to evalu-ate the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs across a range of difficulty levels and reasoning complexities. Among them, GSM8K [6] is one of the most widely used datasets, con-sisting of grade-school-level arithmetic word problems that require multi-step reasoning over natural language. To as-sess robustness, SVAMP [26] introduces adversarial variations of math word problems designed to reduce reliance on su-perficial patterns. For more advanced mathematical reason-ing, MATH [12] provides competition-level problems span-ning Algebra, Geometry, Number Theory, and Combinatorics, often requiring long and precise chains of symbolic reason-ing. Additional challenging benchmarks, such as Olympiad-Bench [11], further emphasize rigorous deduction and deeper reasoning abilities. Despite their diversity, these datasets pri-marily focus on evaluating individual models’ reasoning per-formance, with limited consideration of collaborative or inter-active problem-solving processes, which are central to MASs. 

2.2 General-Domain Multi-Agent Benchmarks 

Early work on agents primarily focused on evaluating their decision-making [22], planning [31], and tool use [29], with relatively limited attention to coordination among multiple agents. Recent surveys have systematically examined collab-oration mechanisms, failure attribution, and self-evolution in LLM-based multi-agent systems [27], highlighting the impor-tance of understanding not only collaborative outcomes but also the underlying interaction and failure processes. More recent benchmarks have increasingly emphasized the quality of collaboration as a dynamic process. For example, Bat-tleAgentBench [34] decomposes evaluation into progressively challenging stages covering cooperative, competitive, and hy-brid interaction settings, explicitly targeting fine-grained col-laboration behaviors beyond final task success. Similarly, MultiAgentBench [50] introduces diverse scenarios with both aligned and conflicting goals, and evaluates coordination through milestone-based metrics, communication quality, and planning effectiveness. 2POSITIVE SAMPLES

In addition, recent benchmarks have moved toward more realistic environments. REALM-Bench [7] focuses on real-world planning and scheduling tasks, incorporating metrics such as coordination efficiency, adaptability, resource manage-ment, and constraint satisfaction. MAS-Orchestra [14] further studies how factors including reasoning depth, parallelism, and robustness affect multi-agent performance. M3MAD-Bench [16] standardizes the evaluation of multi-agent debate across domains and modalities, covering both text-only and vision-language settings. However, these benchmarks are typically designed for gen-eral domains such as social interaction, research assistance, and open-domain reasoning, where mathematical reasoning constitutes only a small subset. To the best of our knowledge, there remains a lack of benchmarks specifically designed for multimodal mathematical reasoning in educational settings. 

2.3 Multi-Agent Frameworks for Mathematics 

Motivated by the success of MASs, recent work has ex-plored their application to mathematical reasoning. De-bate4Math [45] allows agents to iteratively critique each other’s reasoning before reaching consensus, improving per-formance on challenging benchmarks such as GSM8K [6] and MATH [12]. Beyond multi-agent debate, several ap-proaches explore alternative interaction paradigms. For ex-ample, MARS COLL [35] introduces role-based collabora-tion, where multiple agents (e.g., author, reviewer, and meta-reviewer) jointly decompose and solve problems, achieving improved accuracy on GSM8K [6] and other benchmarks con-taining math questions. AdCo [21] investigates hybrid co-operation–competition mechanisms. ECON [43] formulates multi-agent coordination as an incomplete-information game, explicitly modeling the trade-off between accuracy and com-putational cost. Other works focus on improving reasoning structure and efficiency. MA-ToT [10] extends Tree-of-Thought reasoning into multi-agent search, while approaches like MACM [15] and ReMA [33] incorporate cognitive decomposition and memory mechanisms to enable iterative refinement. Addition-ally, ILR [18] introduces interactive learning, allowing agents to improve reasoning strategies over time through feedback. Specialized frameworks such as MathAgent [42] further adopt mixture-of-experts designs to handle multimodal mathemati-cal reasoning tasks, while MathChat [38] explores dialogue-based collaborative problem solving. Despite these advances, a unified evaluation framework that jointly considers multimodal mathematical understanding, in-termediate reasoning processes, and robustness remains under-explored. MAMath-Bench is designed to bridge this gap. 

# 3 MAMath-Bench 

An evaluation dataset for mathematical reasoning in educa-tional scenarios should reflect two characteristics of real learn-ing and assessment. First, students encounter problems with different levels of complexity, requiring an evaluation to dis-tinguish whether reasoning remains effective as the difficulty increases. Second, educational assessment concerns not only 3the correctness of the final answer, but also the validity of the reasoning process, since intermediate errors can reveal misun-derstandings even when the final answer is correct. These con-siderations motivate the two-dimensional design of MAMath-Bench, which is organized along the dimensions of problem difficulty and reasoning quality, as illustrated in Figure 2. The difficulty dimension comprises easy, medium, and hard prob-lems, while the reasoning-quality dimension introduces posi-tive and negative samples. Positive samples cover text, sym-bolic expressions, geometric diagrams, and audiovisual con-tent with complete and correct reasoning. Negative samples inject five representative reasoning flaws—wrong, redundant, repeated, missing, and swapped steps—so that the benchmark can assess whether agents can recognize and maintain reliable reasoning processes in educational mathematics tasks. 

3.1 Annotation 

We employ a four-stage expert annotation pipeline covering data preparation, milestone annotation, adversarial perturba-tion construction, and quality verification. All annotations are conducted by annotators with mathematical training and sub-sequently subjected to cross-review and expert arbitration. 

3.1.1 Raw Data Collection and Difficulty Classification 

To construct a representative and well-structured evaluation set for educational mathematical reasoning, we develop the benchmark data through three stages: source collection, qual-ity filtering, and difficulty organization. 

Dataset Sources. To accurately capture the reasoning per-formance of large models at different levels of cognitive com-plexity, we collect mathematical problems from a diverse set of established benchmarks and open-source repositories. Specif-ically, GSM8K [6] and SVAMP [26] are used to provide relatively short-chain arithmetic and word-problem reason-ing; MAVIS-Geometry and MAVIS-Function contribute vi-sually grounded mathematical problems involving geometric structures and functional relationships; and more challenging competition-level problems are drawn from the Level-5 sub-set of Competition Math and OlympiadBench [11]. Together, these sources cover 11 major mathematical problem types and provide a broad range of reasoning depths and modality con-figurations. 

Dataset Filtering. We adopt a dual filtering mechanism of manual and expert rules to eliminate redundant problems 

that are computationally cumbersome but logically simple, and prioritize problems that require substantial spatial reasoning, number-theoretic deduction, and combinatorial reasoning. In this way, the selected samples are better suited for evaluating the upper limits of agents’ reasoning capability. 

Dataset Organization. We further organize the samples into a progressive difficulty hierarchy according to reasoning-chain length, modality composition, and knowledge-domain complexity. Based on these criteria, the benchmark is divided into easy, medium, and hard levels to better reflect the empha-sis on conceptual understanding and multi-step reasoning in mathematics education. 3.1.2 Milestone Annotation 

We represent each reference reasoning process as an ordered sequence of milestones M = {m1, m 2, . . . , m n}. Each mile-stone corresponds to a self-contained reasoning unit consist-ing of the required conditions, the applied rule or theorem, and the resulting intermediate conclusion. This design ensures that each milestone is sufficiently self-contained for indepen-dent verification while preserving the logical dependencies be-tween successive reasoning stages. To clarify the logical boundaries and core content of each milestone, the annotation team performed fine-grained mile-stone decomposition on all 800 positive samples. An example of milestone decomposition is shown in Figure 3. Furthermore, some mathematical problems admit multiple valid reasoning strategies, making evaluation against a sin-gle reference trajectory potentially restrictive. For problems identified during annotation as admitting distinct valid solution paths, we therefore provide multiple milestone sequences. In total, 63 samples are annotated with multiple solution paths. 

3.1.3 Adversarial Negative Sample Construction 

To systematically evaluate whether MASs can identify errors arising within otherwise plausible reasoning processes, we construct negative samples by applying controlled logical per-turbations to valid reasoning chains. Rather than introducing arbitrary noise, these perturbations preserve the original prob-lem context and overall reasoning structure while modifying specific properties of the reasoning process, allowing system failures to be attributed to identifiable reasoning defects. Fol-lowing the evaluation criteria of ReasonEval [39], we consider five representative types of reasoning defects. All negative samples follow the single-defect-injection principle to enable category-wise analysis of error detection capability. 4Specifically, the five perturbation types are constructed as follows. • Wrong Steps are generated by introducing calculation er-rors into valid reasoning milestones, such as modifying arithmetic operations or numerical results while keeping the surrounding reasoning context unchanged. • Redundant Steps are created by inserting unnecessary reasoning milestones that do not contribute additional in-formation or logical progress to the solution, while re-maining mathematically plausible. • Repeated Steps are generated by duplicating existing reasoning content, including both completely repeated milestones and partially repeated consecutive steps, lead-ing to unnecessary repetition in the reasoning chain. • Missing Steps are constructed by removing essential milestones from the original reasoning trajectory, result-ing in incomplete logical transitions between intermedi-ate conclusions. • Swapped Steps are generated by exchanging the order of two dependent milestones in the original gold reason-ing chain. Such perturbations preserve the involved rea-soning components but introduce inconsistencies in the dependency order, causing subsequent numerical compu-tations or logical deductions to become misaligned. 

3.1.4 Quality Control and Consistency Assessment 

To ensure annotation quality and academic rigor, we design a two-round cross-validation mechanism to strictly control the annotation process and results, ensuring consistency and accu-racy. The specific process is as follows: • First-Round Blind Review: Eight graduate students ma-joring in mathematics and computer science are paired up to conduct blind reviews of milestone decompositions and negative sample defect annotations for all samples. They are unaware of the sample source and the research hypotheses. Samples with inconsistent annotations are marked as disputed samples. • Second-Round Arbitration: An arbitration panel com-posed of two computer science professors reviews each disputed sample, clarifies annotation standards, and reaches a consensus to ensure the rigor of the annotation results.

# 4 Experiments 

To systematically evaluate multimodal mathematical rea-soning capabilities, we structure our experiments around three core research questions: • RQ1: How accurately can current single-LLM and multi-agent systems solve multimodal mathematical problems? • RQ2: How reliable are their intermediate reasoning steps? • RQ3: How robust are these systems to adversarial per-turbations in the reasoning chain? 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

4.1.1 Datasets 

All models are evaluated on MAMath-Bench under a uni-fied evaluation protocol. The positive split is used to assess final-answer correctness and intermediate reasoning quality. For negative samples, the problem and its perturbed reason-ing chain are provided as input to evaluate the ability to detect reasoning anomalies. Performance is reported both overall and by error type. 

4.1.2 Non-Collaborative Baselines and Collaborative MASs 

To comprehensively assess mathematical reasoning capabili-ties in educational scenarios and examine the benefits of col-laborative reasoning, we evaluate both representative single LLMs, which serve as non-collaborative reference baselines, and multi-agent reasoning frameworks. Based on their rele-vance to mathematical reasoning and representation of distinct 5collaboration paradigms, we selected four MASs, including MACM [15], MARS COLL [35], Debate4MATH [45], and AdCo [21]. For all multi-agent baselines, we follow their official open-source implementations and preserve the original agent roles, interaction protocols, and system prompts whenever appli-cable. To ensure a consistent evaluation setting, only the task-specific descriptions required to present MAMath-Bench problems are standardized across systems, while the remain-ing prompts and collaboration mechanisms are kept consistent with their original implementations. In addition, all evaluated MASs use GPT-4.1 as their backbone model.

4.4 Adversarial Reasoning Robustness 

Figure 8 presents a more granular comparison across five types of adversarial reasoning flaws: wrong steps, redundant steps, missing steps, repeated steps, and swapped steps. Detec-tion performance varies significantly across these error types. The results for repeated steps and wrong steps are particu-larly striking. Several systems achieved extremely high de-tection accuracy for these types of perturbations. In con-trast, detecting redundant steps proved consistently difficult. All tested systems showed detection accuracy below approxi-mately 33%, with many models hovering near 10%. Regard-ing missing steps, even powerful systems achieved only mod-erate accuracy. In the detection of swapped steps, Qwen3-VL-32B demonstrated the strongest performance, while most other models showed accuracy close to zero. This is likely because detecting structural ordering errors requires explicitly model-ing the dependencies between intermediate reasoning steps, rather than simply verifying the correctness of individual state-ments. Overall, these results highlight two broad categories of rea-soning flaws: • Locally observable errors, such as explicit repetitions or obviously incorrect steps, which are relatively easy to de-tect; • Global structural errors, such as redundancies, omissions, or sequencing errors, which require an understanding of dependencies across the entire reasoning process. 

# 5 Limitations and Future Directions 

Despite the comprehensive evaluation conducted by MAMath-Bench, several limitations remain. 

The evaluated multi-agent frameworks adopt differ-ent interaction protocols. Therefore, performance differ-ences across debate, condition-oriented collaboration, and cooperative-competitive mechanisms cannot be attributed solely to their collaboration strategies. Future work will adopt standardized agent configurations and controlled ablations to isolate the effects of individual collaboration mechanisms bet-ter. 

The current benchmark does not yet fully capture the diversity and complexity of collaborative mathematical reasoning. Although MAMath-Bench encompasses various mathematical categories, difficulty levels, modalities, and ad-versarial errors, further evaluation is required regarding longer reasoning chains, richer cross-modal dependencies, and agent-level interactions. Future extensions will not only expand the benchmark’s content but also introduce metrics for interaction-level aspects such as agent contributions, communication effi-ciency, consensus formation, and error propagation. 

Data contamination affects fairness to a certain extent. 

Some problems in MAMath-Bench are adapted from publicly available mathematical reasoning datasets. We cannot com-pletely rule out the possibility that some source problems were encountered during pretraining. This issue may affect abso-lute performance estimates. Nevertheless, MAMath-Bench fo-cuses not only on final-answer correctness but also on process-level milestone alignment and adversarial reasoning-error de-tection, which require additional evaluation beyond reproduc-ing known answers. Future versions will incorporate newly constructed and temporally controlled problems to mitigate potential contamination further. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduce MAMath-Bench, a benchmark designed to evaluate the multimodal mathematical reasoning capabilities of MASs across three complementary dimensions: solution correctness, process reliability, and robustness to adversar-ial reasoning-chain perturbations. By integrating multimodal mathematical problems, annotations of reasoning processes based on key steps, and adversarial reasoning perturbations, the benchmark enables an evaluation that goes beyond the tra-ditional metric of final-answer accuracy. Experimental results demonstrate that multi-agent collaboration does not offer a universal advantage in terms of solution accuracy; rather, dis-tinct collaboration mechanisms reshape the reasoning process in vastly different ways. Specifically, increasing the coverage of intermediate reasoning steps often introduces significant re-dundancy, whereas concise reasoning may compromise pro-cess completeness. Furthermore, even powerful systems are prone to structurally subtle reasoning errors, revealing a sig-nificant gap between answer correctness and reasoning relia-bility.

A.2 Prompt Templates 

A.2.1 Positive Sample Prompts 

To ensure a fair comparison, all evaluated models receive stan-dardized mathematical reasoning instructions. The same task instruction is applied across different models, while the input modality is adjusted according to the corresponding problem setting. The system prompt is: You are a helpful assistant capable of math reason-ing. Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed {} .For multimodal problems, the prompt construction module preserves the original modality information and converts each instance into a unified message representation. Text-only in-puts are directly inserted into the user message, while image and audio inputs are attached as corresponding multimodal content blocks. 12 A.2.2 Negative Sample Prompts 

For robustness evaluation, we design task-specific prompts for identifying reasoning defects in corrupted solution chains. Unlike standard mathematical solving prompts, these instruc-tions explicitly require models to analyze existing reasoning processes rather than solve the problem from scratch. The prompts constrain the output format to ensure consistent eval-uation of error detection performance. • Wrong Step: The model is required to locate the first incorrect reasoning step caused by an invalid calculation or logical mistake. The expected output is the index of the incorrect step. • Redundant Step: The model is instructed to identify un-necessary reasoning steps that do not contribute to solv-ing the problem. The expected output is a list of redun-dant step indices. • Repeated Step: The model is required to detect dupli-cated reasoning steps appearing in the solution chain. The expected output is the index of the repeated step. • Missing Step: The model is asked to identify omitted reasoning units that are necessary for completing the so-lution process. The expected output includes the missing step and its corresponding position. • Swapped Step: The model is required to detect two rea-soning steps whose order has been incorrectly exchanged. The expected output is the pair of swapped step indices.
