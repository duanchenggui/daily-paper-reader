# Abstract 

The rapid advancement of image-to-text extraction systems and large language models (LLMs) poses new challenges for educational assessment by making assignment materials easy to transcribe and solve with minimal human effort. To address this problem, we present AIRRI (Artificial Intelligence Resilient Reading Interface), a framework for generating machine-resistant educational content, a dataset of higher-education assignments for evaluation, and an initial evaluation of the human readability of AIRRI machine-resistant text designs. AIRRI employs two categories of machine-resistance techniques. First, AIRRI applies image transformations including character spacing, word spacing, line spacing, character opacity variation, stripe overlays, and image noise. Second, AIRRI applies decision-based adversarial attacks: RayS, SurFree, and ADBA. We evaluate AIRRI against three image-to-text systems: Tesseract, EasyOCR, and the transformer-based GOT-OCR2 model. Our results show that AIRRI substantially degrades automated text extraction performance. AIRRI increased character error rates for EasyOCR (6.9% to 55.9%), Tesseract (2.3% to 100.0%), and GOT-OCR2 (30.7% to 48.1%). Word error rates increased for EasyOCR (24.9% to 96.7%) and Tesseract (10.7% to 100.0%). Among the evaluated decision-based attacks, ADBA consistently produced the largest degradation in image-to-text performance, while RayS and SurFree produced more moderate reductions. EasyOCR and Tesseract were generally more susceptible to both image-based and adversarial perturbations than the transformer-based GOT-OCR2 model. Our findings suggest that AIRRI can significantly reduce the reliability of image-to-text-to-LLM workflows, increasing the effort required for AI-assisted assignment completion. # 1. Introduction 

Digital text is designed to maximize accessibility [30] and machine readability [36], enabling copying, searching, indexing, and automated processing [3]. While these properties provide significant usability benefits [37], they also make content easily subject to automated extraction. Advances in image-to-text and language-processing systems have made it increasingly possible to rapidly convert on-screen text into machine-readable representations and process it automatically [1, 8, 26, 42]. As artificial intelligence systems continue to improve, this raises an important question: can educational material remain readable and usable for human learners while becoming more resistant to automated extraction and machine-assisted completion? Modern LLMs can generate human-like responses, sum-marize content, and answer questions [6, 34]. Combined with image-to-text extraction systems, they enable educational material to be rapidly converted into machine-readable text and processed through automated workflows. Thus, assignment content can be extracted from screenshots and given to AI systems for question answering, summarization, or assignment completion with minimal effort [4]. This creates a growing challenge for educators seeking to promote direct student engagement while limiting low-effort AI-assisted workflows. Existing responses to AI-assisted academic misuse fre-quently focus on behavioral policies[41], plagiarism detection, browser restrictions, or later identification of AI-generated work. While these approaches may discourage misuse or identify suspicious outputs after submission, they often do not address how easily students can extract and process educational content through image-to-text and LLM-based workflows. Furthermore, detection-based approaches may become less reliable[28] as generative models continue to improve and student usage patterns evolve. This motivates exploring preventive approaches that increase the difficulty of automated extraction while preserving the usability of instructional materials. To address these challenges, we propose AIRRI (Artificial Intelligence Resilient Reading Interface). A system designed 1arXiv:2610.18724v1 [cs.CV] 13 Oct 2026 to increase resistance to automated extraction while preserving the readability of educational material for human users. AIRRI transforms assignment and instructional content into a machine-resistant format that impedes image-to-text recognition and downstream automated processing. This increases the difficulty of submitting extracted material directly to LLMs. AIRRI could enable educators to distribute content that remains available to human readers while making low-effort image-to-text and LLM-assisted workflows harder. Rather than eliminating misuse en-tirely, AIRRI increases the human effort required for such behav-ior. A student supplying assignment content to an LLM would need to manually transcribe it rather than rely on automated ex-traction methods. We investigate whether controlled text pertur-bations can reduce the effectiveness of image-to-text and down-stream automated processing while using levels of perturbation to preserve some readability. We evaluate AIRRI across multiple image-to-text systems using real-world educational assignments and quantitative performance metrics to examine the trade-off between machine resistance and the severity of text perturbation. 

# 2. AIRRI Pipeline 

AIRRI renders educational content as images and applies a combination of typographic, image-level, and adversarial perturbations. We cover AIRRI’s threat model, pipeline architecture, rendering, defense mechanisms, and evaluation. 

## 2.1. Threat Model 

AIRRI assumes an adversary who attempts to process educa-tional material through an image-to-text extraction pipeline followed by an LLM with minimal manual effort. The adversary is assumed to have access to modern automated text-extraction systems and may capture assignment content via screenshots or other extraction methods. Unlike traditional adversarial machine learning [12], AIRRI does not seek to induce specific misclassifications. Instead, it seeks to degrade automated text extraction while maintaining some usability for human readers. An attack succeeds when an image-to-text system extracts enough information to reconstruct the original text with little or no manual transcription. A defense succeeds when it sub-stantially degrades extraction quality while preserving human readability. AIRRI does not seek to make extraction impossible; instead, it increases the effort required for automated processing and reduces the effectiveness of AI-assisted extraction. 

## 2.2. Pipeline Overview 

Figure 1 shows an overview of the AIRRI pipeline. Given an input assignment or instructional document, AIRRI produces a protected image intended to disrupt automated image-to-text extraction. AIRRI implements three categories of perturbations: typographic, image-level, and adversarial. The effectiveness of these perturbations is evaluated using EasyOCR[17], Tesseract[29], and GOT-OCR2[44]. The AIRRI pipeline has five stages: (1) Input Acquisition ,

(2) Text Rendering , (3) Perturbation Application , (4) Automated Extraction , and (5) Evaluation . During Stage 1, instructor-provided educational material is the source text. Stage 2 renders the source material to a canvas-based image using fixed rendering parameters. Stage 3 applies the selected perturbation techniques to generate a protected image. Stage 4 evaluates extraction resistance by processing both protected and unprotected images through three image-to-text systems: EasyOCR, Tesseract, and GOT-OCR2, representing traditional and transformer-based extraction architectures. Stage 5 evaluates extraction quality and downstream assignment completion using Character Error Rate (CER), Word Error Rate (WER) [18, 31], and LLM Error Rate (LER). 

## 2.3. Text Generation Pipeline 

AIRRI transforms instructor-provided educational material into a machine-resistant image representation using an HTML canvas rendering pipeline. Input text is rendered using the font Roboto at 16 px with configurable character, word, and line spacing. We selected Roboto because it is the most viewed font on the web per Google Font Analytics [13, 37] and is often the sole font studied in related readability research [20, 21, 23, 25]. Text is rendered with automatic line wrapping to preserve paragraph structure and reading order before exporting as a PNG image. For experimental consistency, all renderings were generated in a web browser at a fixed resolution of 1920 ×1080. Clean and protected images were produced from identical source text and rendering settings, differing only through the application of AIRRI perturbations. 

## 2.4. Defense Mechanisms 

AIRRI employs three categories of defense mechanisms: typo-graphic perturbations, image-level perturbations, and adversarial perturbations. Typographic perturbations modify the appearance and layout of rendered text, image-level perturbations alter the rendered image, and adversarial perturbations introduce model-targeted modifications intended to reduce extraction accuracy. 

2.4.1. Typographic Perturbations 

AIRRI applies four configurable typographic perturbation techniques to alter text appearance and reduce image-to-text extraction quality. Our text designs are based on prior readability literature to try and preserve human readability [24, 38]. Prior research shows that typographic attributes such as font selection and character/word spacing can influence reading performance and readability [7, 32, 33]. Unless otherwise specified, each perturbation is applied uniformly throughout the rendered document. ## 2.5. Evaluation Pipeline 

The AIRRI evaluation pipeline measures automated text extraction performance by comparing extracted text against ground-truth source material. The evaluation process has two stages: automated extraction and extraction scoring. 

2.5.1. Automated Extraction 

AIRRI evaluates machine resistance using three image-to-text systems: Tesseract, EasyOCR, and GOT-OCR2. We selected these systems to represent both traditional image-to-text architectures and modern transformer-based vision-language models. For each rendered image, all three extraction systems generate an independent text transcription. We evaluate Tesseract using the English language model, page segmentation mode 6, and OCR engine mode 3. We evaluate EasyOCR using its English language model with paragraph-level text aggregation enabled. GOT-OCR2 is evaluated using the publicly available stepfun-ai/GOT-OCR-2.0-hf checkpoint. 

2.5.2. Extraction Scoring 

AIRRI compares the extracted text from each image-to-text system against its ground-truth assignment text provided in the dataset manifest. AIRRI evaluates extraction performance using Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER), two standard text-recognition metrics. For character-level evaluation, we normalize both the extracted and ground-truth text by convert-ing all characters to lowercase and removing whitespace. ## 2.6. Dataset for Evaluation 

Our new evaluation dataset has nine higher-education (HE) assignments collected from instructors at two universities. Five assignments were from a University of Connecticut English instructor and two from a University of Rhode Island History instructor. One English assignment has three essay topics with distinct assignment texts; thus, we created three separate evaluation instances. The final dataset contains seven English assignments and two History assignments. For evaluation, each assignment was rendered as clean and AIRRI-protected images, and CER and WER were computed. Table 1 summarizes the introduced dataset. Evaluation was also performed on open-source datasets of 8th and 12th grade-level reading passages (8-and-12) provided by The Readability Consortium [37]. 

# 3. Results 

We evaluate AIRRI’s effectiveness in degrading automated text extraction. We first compare extraction performance on clean and AIRRI-protected documents, then analyze the effects of individual adversarial attacks and extraction systems using CER and WER. Unless otherwise specified, the AIRRI pipeline gen-erated AIRRI-protected documents, including all typographic and image-level perturbations applied simultaneously. 

## 3.1. Typographic + Image-Level Perturbations 

Table 2 summarizes extraction performance for clean and AIRRI-protected documents across all image-to-text systems on both the HE Assignment and 8-and-12 datasets. Results are reported using Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER), higher values indicate greater extraction difficulty. AIRRI increased extraction error rates for EasyOCR and Tesseract. EasyOCR CER increased from 6.9% to 55.9%, while WER increased from 24.9% to 96.7%. Tesseract showed the largest degradation: CER increased from 2.3% to 100% and WER from 10.7% to 100%. This indicates complete extraction failure on all protected documents in the evaluation set. GOT-OCR2 was less affected by the applied perturbations than some of the other models. CER increased from 30.7% to 48.1%, while WER decreased slightly from 63.3% to 61.9%. Examination of individual assignments showed considerable variation in performance: some protected documents produced substantially higher error rates than their clean counterparts, whereas others produced lower error rates. On the 8-and-12 dataset, AIRRI also degraded extraction across all systems. EasyOCR CER increases from 7.3% to 81.1%. GOT-OCR2 showed the largest increase: CER rising from 0.8% to 90.4% and WER from 6.9% to 91.5%. Tesseract CER increased from 1.0% to 38.8%, less degradation than on the HE Assignment, though WER still reached 99.9%. 

3.1.1. Individual Image Transformation Performance 

To evaluate the effectiveness of individual image transforma-tions, each transformation was applied independently at five intensity levels, including a clean baseline (0%) and perturbation strengths of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%. The 100% level corresponded either to the maximum supported perturbation value or an empirically selected upper bound intended to avoid excessive visual degradation, whichever was lower. Although stronger perturbations generally led to higher OCR error rates, excessively strong transformations may also reduce human readability. We measured OCR performance at each intensity level and compared it with the clean baseline. Table 3 reports the mean increase in image-to-text error rate across all assignments, perturbation intensity levels, and extraction systems. Among the evaluated image transformations, stripe overlays produced the largest degradation in automated text extraction performance, increasing CER by an average of 35.9% and WER by 38.3%. In comparison, opacity jitter and image noise resulted in substantially smaller increases in error rates. Although performance varied across assignments and perturbation intensities, stripe overlays consistently produced the largest increases in both CER and WER.

## 3.4. Interviews with Human Readability Experts 

We conducted semi-structured interviews with four domain ex-perts in typography, design, and cognitive psychology. We showed each human readability expert our machine-resistant designs and their perturbation levels. The interviews focused on the designs’ potential effects on reading speed and comprehen-sion across different reading modes, such as interlude reading or skimming. The final part of the interviews covered how the different designs affect neurodivergent readers and future design ideas we should explore. We performed thematic coding to ana-lyze qualitative results from the semi-structured interviews. This effort revealed three codes for creating human-readable perturba-tions of machine-resistant text: preserving letterform structure, line tracking, and adequate foreground-background contrast. Despite this weaker performance on isolated crops, GOT-OCR’s full-composite CER remains near zero on both datasets, as shown in Table 5, which indicates the model leverages document-level context to compensate for localized noise. However, this contextual advantage disappears when the evaluation scope is narrowed to the attacked region alone. 

## 4.2. Image-Level Perturbation Analysis 

Across the evaluated dataset, AIRRI consistently increased extraction error rates for EasyOCR and Tesseract, with Tesseract exhibiting complete failure to extract on all protected documents. These findings suggest that the typographic and image-level per-turbations employed by AIRRI effectively disrupt the assump-tions underlying many traditional image-to-text pipelines [8, 42]. A notable observation is the difference between traditional image-to-text systems and the transformer-based GOT-OCR2 model [1, 44]. While AIRRI substantially degraded the performance of EasyOCR and Tesseract, GOT-OCR2 exhibited greater resistance and more variable behavior. Some assign-ments experienced increased extraction errors, while others showed improved performance relative to the clean baseline. Notably, its aggregate WER decreased slightly from 63.26% to 61.90%, despite CER increasing substantially from 30.70% to 48.09%. This suggests that AIRRI affects character- and word-level errors differently for GOT-OCR2. One possible explanation is that the perturbations alter segmentation or contextual decoding in ways that reduce some word-level 8errors while increasing character-level corruption; however, the underlying mechanism was not isolated in our experiments. 

## 4.3. Individual Image-Level Perturbation Analysis 

The individual perturbation experiments provide insight into which image-level transformations contribute most strongly to machine resistance. Among the evaluated techniques, stripe overlays consistently produced the largest increases in both CER and WER, substantially outperforming image noise and character opacity variation. These results suggest that structured visual modifications disrupt automated text extraction more effectively than localized pixel-level distortions [15, 26]. Character opacity variation exhibited a different pattern [14]. Across low and moderate intensity levels, opacity jitter produced relatively little degradation in extraction performance. We observed substantial increases in extraction error only at the highest tested intensity level, suggesting a threshold effect. However, the highest intensity setting also noticeably reduced human readability, reflecting the tradeoff between visual accessibility and text modification documented in readability research[3, 16, 27, 30, 43]. As a result, while extreme opacity variation can disrupt automated extraction, it may be less practi-cal than stripe overlays, which achieved substantial degradation without requiring equally aggressive visual distortion. Although stripe overlays were the most effective individual transformation, the complete AIRRI pipeline had the greatest degradation in extraction performance. It combines all typo-graphic and image-level perturbations. Thus, AIRRI produced higher error rates than any single transformation alone. This result suggests that different perturbations likely interfere with different stages of the extraction process, and that their combined effects provide stronger protection than isolated modifications. 

## 4.4. Impact on Downstream LLM Performance 

While CER and WER quantify extraction quality, the LLM evaluation shows that extraction errors do not always translate directly into downstream task failure. Tesseract exhibited complete extraction failure on protected documents, resulting in an LER of 100%. EasyOCR and GOT-OCR2 frequently produced noisy or incomplete extractions that retained sufficient information to reconstruct partial assignments, resulting in lower downstream failure rates. Our findings suggest that extraction-quality metrics alone may not fully capture the practical impact of extraction errors on modern AI-assisted workflows. Future work could explore human-inspectable AI-assisted workflows [45]. Although LLMs can often infer missing information and compensate for extraction mistakes, AIRRI increased the aggregate LER from 7.4% to 55.6%, indicating a substantial reduction in successful assignment completion. This demonstrates that sufficiently degraded extractions can still prevent reconstruction of assignment content, suggesting that AIRRI remains effective even against downstream AI-assisted recovery. 

## 4.5. Image-Level vs. Adversarial Perturbations 

This section compares the effectiveness and computational characteristics of AIRRI’s image-level and adversarial pertur-bations. While both approaches aim to degrade automated text extraction, image-level perturbations apply across the entire rendered document, whereas adversarial perturbations introduce bounded pixel-level modifications within selected text regions. 

4.5.1. Effectiveness Against Image-to-Text Systems 

Overall, our results suggest that image-level perturbations provide the strongest protection in our setting. Among the evaluated adversarial methods, ADBA achieved the strongest extraction degradation, particularly at ε = 64 /255 . However, the image-level perturbations generally produced larger and more consistent increases in extraction error across the evaluated image-to-text systems. A notable exception was GOT-OCR2, where ADBA produced higher target-region error rates than those observed under the image-level perturbations. ADBA’s strong performance against GOT-OCR2 suggests that adversarial approaches may offer advantages against more complex image-to-text systems, although additional evaluation of modern vision-language models is needed to confirm this. 

4.5.2. Hardware and Deployment Considerations 

Image-level perturbations are computationally inexpensive because they are applied directly during rendering. Let 

n=W H denote the number of image pixels. Operations such as spacing adjustments, stripe overlays, opacity variation, and image noise require only a single pass over the image and therefore have time complexity O(n).In contrast, decision-based adversarial attacks require re-peated queries to the target OCR system. Let q denote the num-ber of queries. Methods such as RayS [11] and ADBA [9] there-fore scale as approximately O(qn ), since each query evaluates a perturbed image containing n pixels. In our experiments, adver-sarial attacks often required thousands of queries, making them substantially more expensive than image-level perturbations. Consequently, image-level and typographic perturbations provide the most practical and scalable approach for educational deployment. This distinction is particularly important for educa-tional settings, where instructors may need to generate protected materials frequently and with minimal computational overhead. For this use case, it is impractical. Because adversarial attacks require substantially greater computation and generation time [5, 9–11], they may be better suited for protecting selected high-value content rather than routine assignment generation. 

# 5. Conclusion 

We present AIRRI, a framework for generating machine-resistant educational content. AIRRI combines typographic perturbations, image-level perturbations, and decision-based adversarial attacks to reduce the effectiveness of automated text extraction systems while preserving access to the underlying 9instructional material. We evaluated AIRRI using a dataset of higher-education assignments. AIRRI substantially degraded the extraction performance for EasyOCR and Tesseract and produced more variable effects for the transformer-based GOT-OCR2 model. AIRRI also increased downstream assignment completion failure rates; thus, degraded extractions can help reduce the effectiveness of image-to-text-to-LLM workflows. Future work will evaluate AIRRI using formal human-readability studies [3, 35], in-the-wild studies with educators and designers [39, 40], larger educational datasets, datasets across domains like medical texts [2], and additional vision-language models to better understand the tradeoff between human usability and machine resistance.
