Abstract 

Simulated students let researchers test tutor-ing systems and teaching strategies before real learners are available, but only if the simulation reproduces where a particular learner succeeds and fails. The common approach asks a large language model to role-play a student, but a role does not model what this learner knows or will do next, so the model’s own competence fills the gap. Simulated students perform at a level set by the model, differ little from one an-other, and seldom make the real learners’ errors. We model the learner’s behavior and recast stu-dent simulation as third-person prediction of the learner’s next action . HACHIMI-II builds a student model from the learner’s history, in-fers the learner’s state for the current item or tutor turn, and compiles it into a behavior har-ness that constrains the next action. On three item-response datasets, the Wasserstein-1 dis-tance between simulated and real student ability is 2.4–8.4 × smaller than that of the strongest baseline. On two dialogue datasets, HACHIMI-II reproduces real student errors with the same knowledge type, locus, and mechanism 1.8– 5.1 × more often than the strongest baseline, and ablations trace the gains mainly to the ex-plicit learner state. Predicting the learner makes capable models usable as simulated students. 

1 Introduction 

Simulated students let researchers evaluate edu-cational content and compare teaching strategies before any real learner is involved, and they sup-port the development of tutoring systems when real interaction data is scarce (Käser and Alexandron, 2024; Scarlatos et al., 2026). To serve these uses, a simulated student has to behave like the learner it stands for. It should answer some items cor-rectly, miss others, and make the errors that the real learner makes. 

> *Corresponding author.

Large language models can write student an-swers and dialogue turns, and the usual way to ob-tain them is role prompting, which asks the model to act as a student of a given level (Benedetto et al., 2024; Scarlatos et al., 2026). However, a role prompt names a level without modeling what this learner knows or how the learner will respond to the current item or tutor turn. An aligned model is trained to be correct and helpful (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) and already knows the con-tent that the student is supposed to lack, so it fills the gap with its own knowledge and a generic pic-ture of a student. As Figure 1 illustrates, it then either solves the problem despite the assigned role or inserts an error at a step where the learner would not fail. This failure has been described as the com-petence paradox (Yuan et al., 2026) and as expert knowledge leakage (Song et al., 2026), and we call its observable effect competence leakage . Under leakage, the ability level is set by the model, dif-ferences among learners largely disappear, and real errors are rarely reproduced. Existing remedies add learner profiles (Lu and Wang, 2024), agent memory (Xu et al., 2024), or prompt-level constraints on what the student knows. They enrich the description of the student, but the model is still asked to act as the student and still has to suppress what it knows. Evaluations of prompting-based simulators report limited fidelity to real student behavior (Scarlatos et al., 2026). In this paper, we counter competence leakage by modeling the learner’s behavior, which changes the task framing from role-play to prediction. A request to act as a weak student asks the model to suppress knowledge it has. A request to pre-dict what a particular learner will do next asks the model to use that knowledge, because the model must know the correct solution to judge where this learner will depart from it. Following this fram-ing, we propose HACHIMI-II (Figure 1). HACHIMI-II takes its name from HACHIMI (Jiang et al., 2026), whose corpus of one million student personas provides its learner prior, and adds the state inference and harness stages that turn a persona into a predicted action. We evaluate HACHIMI-II against role-prompted baselines on five datasets in two formats. In item-response simulation on XES3G5M, Eedi, and ENEM, the Wasserstein-1 distance between the simulated and real distributions of per-student accu-racy is 2.4–8.4 × smaller than that of the strongest baseline, and HACHIMI-II recovers the ranking of individual students. In dialogue simulation on Eedi QATD2k and Khan/CoMTA, it reproduces real er-rors with the same knowledge type, locus, and mechanism 1.8–5.1 × more often than the strongest baseline and more than doubles the F1 of error-state prediction. It also attains the highest agreement with the real learners on all five datasets. This paper makes three contributions. First, we show that competence leakage in role-prompted simulation affects the ability level, the differences among learners, and their errors, and we recast the task as third-person prediction of the learner’s next action. Second, we propose HACHIMI-II, which models the learner’s behavior with a student model, a learner state inferred at each step, and a behavior harness compiled deterministically from that state. Third, on three item-response and two dialogue datasets, ablations show that the three components govern different parts of the simulated behavior, with the learner state as the main source of the gains. 

2 Related Work 

Learner modeling and student simulation. 

Real learner traces are costly to collect (Choi et al., 2020; Scarlatos et al., 2026). Early simulators used hand-specified cognitive models such as models of human memory (Reddy et al., 2017), and learner models estimate proficiency through probabilistic mastery (Corbett and Anderson, 1994), item re-sponse theory (Lord, 1980), or recurrent, memory-augmented, and reinforcement-learning architec-tures (Piech et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2023). These models output correctness or mastery. The open-ended answers and dialogue turns that tutoring research needs are generated with language models. 

LLM-based simulated students. Role prompt-ing asks a language model to answer as a student of a given level (Benedetto et al., 2024; Scarlatos et al., 2026), profile-conditioned methods add knowledge states, misconceptions, histories, or personas (Lu and Wang, 2024; Jiang et al., 2026), and agent-based simulators add memory, reflection, and ac-tion modules (Xu et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025). Marquez-Carpintero et al. (2026) review these ar-chitectures. Training-based simulators fine-tune a model on student data (Qi et al., 2026) or special-ize a pooled simulator to each student (Yang et al., 2026), and Song et al. (2026) unlearn knowledge so that the model answers like a novice. Simulated stu-dents, including fine-tuned ones, still show limited behavioral fidelity beyond answer accuracy (Scar-latos et al., 2026; Xu and Zhang, 2026). HACHIMI-II needs no training and keeps the model’s knowl-edge, which it uses to predict where the learner will fail. Three lines of work are closest to ours. Wu et al. (2025) build a cognitive prototype of each student from past learning records and refine a sim-ulated solution toward the predicted performance. Yuan et al. (2026) name the competence paradox and argue that valid simulation requires an explicit specification of the student’s epistemic state. Meng and Lin (2026) condition simulated students on coarse engagement states. HACHIMI-II infers a finer state at every item and tutor turn, covering the predicted error, commitment, and help-seeking, and compiles it into constraints on the next action. Beyond answer-level ability alignment (Srivatsa et al., 2025) and teacher judgments of realism (Mar-tynova et al., 2025), we also compare the errors themselves in knowledge type, locus, and mecha-nism. 

Constraints and harnesses for generation. In agent systems, a harness is an execution layer out-side the model that organizes context, tools, mem-ory, and verification (Li et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026; Zhong and Zhu, 2026). Constrained genera-tion and language-model programming shape out-puts through explicit constraints and control flow (Gârbacea and Mei, 2025; Beurer-Kellner et al., 2023). HACHIMI-II applies this idea to behav-ior simulation. Its constraints are derived from a predicted learner state, and they restrict both the content and the form of the next action. 

3 HACHIMI-II 

HACHIMI-II models the learner’s behavior by pre-dicting the next observable action in the four steps shown in Figure 1. The same design serves item-response and dialogue simulation, with state and harness fields specific to each format (Appendix B). 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

At step t the simulator observes Vt = {cLt , c Tt , h t},where cLt is the learner evidence available before the step, cTt is the current item or tutor turn, and 

ht is the visible history. The target action at is the learner’s response to one item in item-response simulation and the learner’s next turn in dialogue simulation. Generation uses only Vt. HACHIMI-II computes 

mt = MPH (cLt , h t), st = S(mt, c Tt , h t),bt = B(st), at = G(Vt, m t, s t, b t),

(1) where mt is the student model, PH is the HACHIMI-1M persona corpus (Jiang et al., 2026), 

st is the learner state, bt is the behavior harness, and at is the generated action. M, S, and G are prompted calls to the backbone model, and B is a deterministic mapping. 

3.2 Student Model 

The student model mt describes who the learner is. It combines an observed profile, summarized from the learner’s visible history, with a HACHIMI-1M persona that matches this profile (Appendix A). The observed profile is the main evidence about the learner’s ability and habits, and the persona adds a prior on longer-term background such as motivation, confidence, and interaction style. The student model is written in the third person, so the backbone reasons about the learner. 

3.3 Learner State Inference 

The learner state st predicts how the learner will handle the current item or tutor turn. The state has four variables, shown in Figure 1 and Table 1, and each follows an established line of educational re-search. Following evidence-centered design (Mis-levy et al., 2003; Mislevy and Riconscente, 2005), the reachable knowledge lists the prerequisites of the current item that the learner has mastered. Fol-lowing the zone of proximal development (Vygot-sky, 1978; Wood et al., 1976), it also records how far the learner’s reasoning can reach and how much of the tutor’s support the learner takes up. 3.4 Behavior Harness Compilation 

The compiler B turns the learner state into explicit constraints on the next action. It is a fixed mapping, so the same state always yields the same harness, and the generator receives concrete limits on what it may write. For example, an explanation depth of none compiles to a limit of one sentence with-out a full derivation, and a predicted error at the process-skills step compiles to a requirement that the answer contain one concrete error at that step. Table 1 summarizes the mapping, and Appendix B lists the harness fields for each format. 

3.5 Harnessed Generation 

The generator G receives the visible context, the student model, the state, and the harness, and writes the action. The generated action is checked against the harness and regenerated when it violates a con-straint (Appendices C and H). Table 2 follows one XES3G5M item through the four steps. The state predicts that the learner can compute the travel times but will average the two speeds, and the generated answer carries this er-ror. The real learner answered this item incorrectly, while all three baselines solved it.

6 Discussion 

Our results show that an explicit model of the learner’s behavior counters competence leakage. Under role prompting, each simulator portrays one generic student whose accuracy is set by the model and the prompt. This uniformity is the ability-level counterpart of the flattening and caricature observed when language models simulate opin-ions, personas, and identity groups (Santurkar et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). As in social simulation, where interactions scripted by one model look more successful than those among agents that hold only their own information (Zhou et al., 2024), the simulator draws on knowledge that the simulated party does not have. Predic-tion changes the role of the model’s knowledge. To predict that a learner will average two speeds, the model must know how average speed is com-puted and where this learner departs from it, so the competence that leaks under role-play becomes the source of faithful errors. Evaluations of simulated students should compare the simulated ability dis-tribution with the real one and score the error state and its structure together with functional agree-ment, which is dominated by turns without an error. A simulator that predicts how a particular learner responds to an item or a tutor move supports uses that depend on ability differences or on specific misconceptions, such as comparing teaching strate-gies across ability levels or testing a hint against a misconception. It is also a step toward a world model of the learner (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Zhou et al., 2025), against which tutoring policies can be planned and compared before they reach real students. 

7 Conclusion 

Role-prompted student simulators exhibit compe-tence leakage and portray a generic student whose ability is set by the model. HACHIMI-II mod-els the learner’s behavior through third-person pre-diction, inferring the learner’s state for the cur-rent item or tutor turn and compiling it into a behavior harness for the next action. Compared with every baseline, it brings the simulated ability distribution closer to the real one on three item-response datasets and reproduces real errors with their full structure more often on two dialogue datasets. Faithful student simulation thus rests on predicting the learner, which lets tutor development and evaluation use capable models as simulated stu-dents. 

Limitations 

Our evaluation covers the correctness of item re-sponses and the presence and structure of math-ematical errors in tutoring turns, on datasets in mathematics and natural sciences. A learner’s next action also depends on factors that no record cap-tures, such as momentary attention and the outcome of a guess, and HACHIMI-II predicts the part of be-havior that the available evidence determines. Each dialogue turn is predicted from the real preceding dialogue. A direct extension is multi-turn simu-lation in which the simulated student’s own turns form the history, evaluated with a protocol that compares generated and real dialogues over many turns. The state and harness are defined through knowledge, reasoning, errors, confidence, and inter-action, and the method uses the backbone through prompts and deterministic rules, so it can be ap-plied to further subjects and backbones without training. 

Ethical Considerations 

All datasets in this work are publicly available, and we use them under their original terms of use. The learner records in these datasets are de-identified, and HACHIMI-II uses only their responses, dia-logue turns, and grade or age. A matched persona comes from HACHIMI-1M, a corpus of generated student personas (Jiang et al., 2026), and is used only as a prior on learning behavior inside the sim-ulator. The simulated students are intended to sup-plement data from real learners when educational systems are developed and evaluated. Each output is a predicted response to one item or tutor turn and should not be used to assess or classify individual students.

HACHIMI-II retrieves one HACHIMI-1M persona for each learner in six steps. 1. Observed profile. The learner’s visible his-tory is summarized into a profile with grade or age, ability level, past performance, strengths and weaknesses, response style, uncertainty, and help-seeking. 2. Candidate retrieval. A candidate pool is drawn from HACHIMI-1M on coarse fields, namely grade or age range, academic level, subject strengths and weaknesses, and learning-style tags. 3. Rule-based ranking. Candidates are scored on grade compatibility, agreement of ability level, overlap of strong and weak subjects, and similarity of learning behavior. 4. Reranking. The backbone compares the ob-served profile with the full descriptions of the top-k candidates ( k = 8 ), using motivation, confidence, learning habits, self-efficacy, and interaction style. 5. Validity check. Near-duplicate candidates are removed, and the selected persona is checked for membership in the candidate pool and completeness of its fields. 6. Fusion. The selected persona is merged with the observed profile into the third-person stu-dent model. 

B State and Harness Fields 

Table 10 lists the fields used in each format. Both formats follow the design in Table 1, and the fields differ because the evidence and the target action differ. Prerequisite familiarity, reachability, and the uptake of the tutor’s support refine the reachable knowledge. The likely error path in item-response simulation and the misconception status and pre-dicted error in dialogue form the active error pat-tern. Commitment and self-monitoring form the confidence state, and help-seeking, readiness to at-tempt, the form of engagement, and response depth form the local interaction move. In item-response simulation the state describes the learner’s relation to the current item, and the harness constrains the answer and its explanation. In dialogue the state describes the learner’s response to the current tutor turn, and the harness constrains what the next turn may say and do. 

C Generation and Checking 

In dialogue simulation, each generated turn is rewritten in the learner’s register, and a consistency check confirms that its content is unchanged. A rule-based check and a review by the backbone then compare the turn with the harness. They look for a response more complete than the state allows, a transition faster than the predicted state allows, and a break in the predicted error path. A turn that violates the harness is regenerated once under tight-ened constraints. In item-response simulation, the output contains a final answer and a short student response, and it is checked and regenerated in the same way.
