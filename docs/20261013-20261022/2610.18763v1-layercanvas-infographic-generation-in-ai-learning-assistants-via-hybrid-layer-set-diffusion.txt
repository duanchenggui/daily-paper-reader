ABSTRACT
Online learning platforms are increasingly deploying AI assistants to provide per-
sonalized, on-demand tutoring. These can leverage infographics to communicate
complex information and improve the learning experience. However, automating ef-
fective infographic generation remains a challenging task, requiring a combination
of visual richness with precise typography, structured layouts, and continued ed-
itability. Existing representation methods satisfy these goals only in part: Scalable
Vector Graphics (SVG) representations preserve geometry, hierarchy, and text but
provide limited aesthetic flexibility, while raster diffusion models produce richer
appearances by flattening content and layout into a single image. Conditioning
a diffusion model on a rasterized SVG does not resolve this conflict because the
raster preserves approximate placement but discards element ownership, occlu-
sion relationships, and the structure needed to modify and recompose the result.
We introduce LayerCanvas, a hybrid approach that retains SVG as the semantic,
structural, and typographic program while using diffusion to aestheticize non-text
content. Rather than generating one flattened raster, LayerCanvas partitions SVG

INTRODUCTION
Visual explanations are a fundamental component of modern learning platforms, where instructional
content is increasingly presented through a combination of text, diagrams, illustrations, and info-
graphics. Pairing words with pictures improves comprehension and retention relative to text alone
(Mayer, 2009; Clark & Paivio, 1991), while infographics can make dense information more accessible
and memorable (Bateman et al., 2010; Bhat & Alyahya, 2024). As learning platforms increasingly
deliver and adapt instructional materials digitally, there is a growing need to automatically generate
visual explanations that are both pedagogically effective and easy to revise as content evolves. Such
explanations must communicate through precise labels and clear visual hierarchy while also providing
the visual richness expected from modern image generators (Rombach et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2024;
Chen et al., 2025). Meeting these requirements is challenging: precision, structure, and editability are
naturally supported by representations such as Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) (W3C SVG Working
Group, 2011), whereas visual richness is most readily produced by raster generative models.
This representational contrast has a fundamental tradeoff. Vector graphics make geometry, grouping,
text, and rendering order explicit, which makes direct manipulation possible (W3C SVG Working
Group, 2011), but automatically generated SVGs (Rodriguez et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023; Xing et al.,
2024; Wu et al., 2024) often appear visually rigid, and stylizing them by hand is costly and difficult
to scale. On the other hand, diffusion and flow models provide richer aesthetic priors (Rombach
et al., 2022; Saharia et al., 2022; Esser et al., 2024), but synthesizing the artifact as a single raster
makes exact typography and local intervention difficult; even state-of-the-art image models render
dense, legible text unreliably, motivating specialized text-rendering methods (Liu et al., 2023b; Chen
et al., 2023; Tuo et al., 2024). These limitations are especially consequential for learning platforms,
which must serve learners worldwide by localizing explanations into different languages and adapting
layouts across display formats, from desktop interfaces to mobile screens. Supporting these uses
requires text to remain disentangled from imagery and layout components to remain independently
adjustable, properties that are lost when the infographic is flattened into a raster. A natural way then
is to rasterize the SVG layout and use it as a conditioning signal for a diffusion model, letting the
vector structure guide where content goes while diffusion supplies appearance.
Simply rasterizing an SVG for conditioning does not resolve the mismatch: it preserves coarse
spatial placement but discards element ownership, occlusion relationships, and the structure needed to
recompose the graphic after an edit. This loss of structure cannot be addressed simply by strengthening
raster conditioning. A system designed for editable generation should preserve these compositional
units throughout synthesis instead of attempting to recover their ownership from pixels afterward.
Motivated by this principle, LayerCanvas combines the structure of SVGs with the visual capabilities
of diffusion models. The SVG defines the semantic layout, hierarchy, paint order, and typography.
Its non-text elements are partitioned into ordered, non-overlapping layers and generated through
communicating parallel RGB streams, which are then alpha-composited according to the SVG z-order.
Text nodes are kept outside the diffusion process entirely and rendered directly from their preserved
vector metadata (Figure 1). Outputs remain editable, so users can replace text, move or hide groups,
change the z-order, reuse illustrations, or specify animation trajectories. This makes the representation
well suited to visual authoring workflows where generated content needs to remain editable and
reusable, including educational content creation.
Training such a model requires examples that pair SVG with the corresponding rendered layers and
final image. To create this paired supervision, we design a custom multi-agent framework, following
recent work on agentic pipelines (Jain et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). The agents create concepts

and SVG layouts, add illustrations, produce text-free targets and masks, and check that each example
renders and reconstructs correctly.
Our contributions are:
• A hybrid formulation that separates deterministic SVG structure and typography from
diffusion-based aestheticization;
• A multi-agent pipeline that produces and validates paired SVGs, text-free targets, layers,
masks, and provenance metadata;
• A rendering-aligned layer representation that supports parallel generation, explicit occlusion
modeling, and deterministic z-order composition, yielding over 25% higher placement-exact
OCR character accuracy and approximately 27% higher aggregate aesthetic quality than the
strongest raster baseline
• Practical workflows for non-destructive editing, localization, asset reuse, and animated
visual explanations.

RELATED WORK
Visual documents and typography.
Automatic graphic design combines templates, constraints,
retrieval, and learned layouts; neural methods generate sets or sequences of labeled regions, including
alignment and overlap structure (Li et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2021; Inoue et al., 2023). Such
representations organize posters, slides, and documents, but do not by themselves resolve illustration
appearance, occlusion, or editable object ownership. Programmatic graphics preserve structure, yet
hand-authored styling and rigid templates scale poorly to open-ended concepts. Image generators
provide richer aesthetics, and text-aware methods introduce character encoders, rendered-glyph
conditions, OCR objectives, or localized controls (Chen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024b; Tuo et al.,
2024). Instead, we retain SVG as the source of truth for geometry and typography, remove text from
diffusion targets, and render editable vector text after aestheticization.
Controllable, object-centric, and layered generation.
ControlNet and T2I-Adapter condition
pretrained diffusion models on spatial signals (Zhang et al., 2023; Mou et al., 2024), while grounded,
reference-conditioned, and regional methods add box, entity, exemplar, or prompt-region controls
(Li et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023; Bar-Tal et al., 2023). These approaches improve spatial control
but typically produce a single entangled raster or latent field, limiting direct editing after sampling.
Object-centric, compositional, and layer-aware models separate entities, concepts, transparency, or
foreground/background content (Burgess et al., 2019; Locatello et al., 2020; Avrahami et al., 2023;
Zhang & Agrawala, 2024), with recent methods explicitly generating or recovering editable image
layers (Yang et al., 2024a; Yin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). In contrast, LayerCanvas uses SVG
layer identity as explicit conditioning, packing mutually non-overlapping elements into ordered RGB
layer sets. This known layer structure makes output decomposition and local intervention intrinsic to
the representation, rather than requiring layers to be recovered post hoc.
Agentic multimodal curation and differentiation.
Foundation models have increasingly been used
to generate instructions, propose examples, and curate synthetic multimodal supervision (Wang et al.,
2023; Liu et al., 2023a; Gadre et al., 2023). More recent agentic pipelines extend this paradigm by
coordinating specialized agents for scientific tasks, including translating NeRF papers into executable
code and automating the generation of editable scientific figures (Jain et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026).
These systems demonstrate the potential of agentic workflows for generating and iteratively refining
structured scientific artifacts. Our pipeline builds on this agentic paradigm by combining specialized
generative and validation agents with deterministic SVG parsing, rendering, OCR, overlap analysis,
and alpha-composition checks, producing mechanically validated training records.

PROBLEM SETUP AND METHOD
3.1
OVERVIEW
Infographics combine two capabilities that are usually separated. SVGs provide exact layout and
typography, whereas diffusion models provide rich raster appearance but collapse the scene into one
pixel grid (Podell et al., 2024; Rombach et al., 2022). Conditioning diffusion on a rasterized SVG
preserves coarse geometry but loses element boundaries and information about which elements cover
others. LayerCanvas instead keeps the SVG as the main scene description and generates its non-text
content as an ordered set of editable raster layers.
Figure 2 summarizes the approach. A renderer creates training layers from the SVG, elements that do
not overlap are packed into ordered groups, and all groups are denoised in parallel by a frozen image
backbone. A trainable adapter identifies each layer and exchanges messages based on SVG overlap
and parent-child links. The generated layers are then combined in SVG paint order and exact vector
text is added back.

3.4
GENERATING COORDINATED LAYERS
LayerCanvas generates all layers together, but separate layers can easily drift toward different colors,
lighting, or object identities. Following the continuous interpolation used in flow matching (Lipman
et al., 2023), we give them part of the same starting noise and use the same denoising timestep.
This encourages a common look while preserving private noise for layer-specific detail. Shared
noise cannot make one layer respond to another, however, so the frozen image backbone needs a
small trainable adapter. The adapter follows the efficient frozen-backbone conditioning strategy of
ControlNet and T2I-Adapter (Zhang et al., 2023; Mou et al., 2024), but operates across an ordered
layer set so that each layer is influenced by the other. It answers three questions: which layer is being
generated, which other layers matter to it, and whether their effect should be global or limited to a
shared boundary.
Identifying each layer.

adds learned identifiers for paint order and the main element type:

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
SETUP
Dataset -
We develop InfoLayers, a corpus of 23,960 paired infographic records on a 768 × 512
canvas with a multi-agent pipeline. Each sample pairs a structural SVG with a text-free and a
composited target, together with the per-layer supervision our model consumes: amodal and visible
masks, paint order, and a hierarchy/overlap graph. Construction and layout statistics are given in
Appendix A.
Baselines -
We compare four methods on shared held-out layouts. The LLM-authored SVG
reference recolors and completes the structural SVG and renders it, giving exact vector text by
construction but no learned aestheticization. The two prompt-only open-weight SOTA diffusion
baselines, Qwen-Image-2512 (Qwen Team, 2025a) and Ideogram-4 (Ideogram AI, 2026), are each
conditioned on the design-concept prompt derived from the input SVG layout; for Ideogram-4 this
prompt is constructed from the same SVG layout, with its structural elements serialized into the
structured prompt format the model expects. LayerCanvas is our grouped layer-set diffusion model
with deterministic exact-SVG text compositing with the MMDiT backbone as Ideogram AI (2026).
For evaluation, all methods are placed on a 768×512 canvas.
Metrics -
Text fidelity is scored two ways. Placement-exact fidelity uses ground-truth-region OCR
Tesseract OCR contributors (2026) at 768×512, and reports character accuracy and exact-match rate.
Placement-agnostic fidelity runs full-image OCR at the same resolution and measures word recall,
how much of the ground-truth text appears anywhere in the image, so a baseline that renders readable
text at the wrong position still gets credit. The two exact-text methods place text from the SVG by
construction, so their remaining gap from 1.0 is OCR error rather than real text mistakes. For visual
quality we use a VLM as a judge: Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic, 2026) scores each design from 0 to 5

4.3
REPRESENTATION ABLATION
We compare grouped LayerCanvas with a single-raster conditioning baseline to test whether explicit
layer separation improves editability. The baseline conditions the same MMDiT model (Ideogram
AI, 2026) directly on the rasterized SVG using T2I-Adapter (Mou et al., 2024). A single stream
must learn to render the whole graphic at once, which becomes harder as the layout grows denser.
In contrast, each LayerCanvas layer contains non-overlapping elements, making it simpler to learn
before the layers are composed. Under fixed noise, LayerCanvas better preserves the moved element
and surrounding layout, while the control redraws unrelated content (Figure 4). It also improves text
recall and all VLM scores, raising average quality from 3.55 to 3.77 (Table 2).

DOWNSTREAM APPLICATIONS
Because LayerCanvas preserves SVG structure alongside independently addressable raster layers, a
generated design can be adapted without regenerating the whole image. Vector text supports changes
to wording, typography, and language, while separate visual layers enable animation, asset reuse, and
local element edits such as removing a card or swapping an icon while preserving unaffected content.

Retained grouping and paint order further support layout retargeting, re-theming, and SVG-level
corrections while limiting changes to the intended components. Together, these capabilities turn
a generated infographic into a reusable authoring artifact. We highlight three below; the rest are
detailed in Appendix C.
5.1
TEXT EDITING AND LOCALIZATION
End-to-end raster generators must repaint text whenever wording, typography, or language changes.
LayerCanvas instead generates only the visual substrate and preserves typography as styled SVG
nodes, so copy, style, and locale changes require no diffusion pass and cannot alter the generated
artwork (Figure 13).
5.2
INDEPENDENT LAYER ANIMATION
We adapt the zero-shot animation principle of Text2Video-Zero (Khachatryan et al., 2023), in which
the motion and appearance priors of a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model produce a temporally
linked sequence without video-specific training. Whereas a flattened document would expose the
complete page to this update, LayerCanvas lets the temporal operation target one named image
layer while all other layers remain fixed: every unselected image layer and the full-resolution SVG
typography are copied identically into every frame.
5.3
SVG-SPACE REFINEMENT AND FAITHFUL DIFFUSION PROPAGATION
An upstream layout proposal may contain a misplaced, mislabeled, or incorrectly styled element.
In LayerCanvas, the SVG is a persistent control surface: the element can be corrected directly, and
the model propagates that correction to the raster without retraining or redesigning the rest of the
document.
6
CONCLUSION
We formulate infographic generation as structured aestheticization: a deterministic SVG supplies
geometry, hierarchy, typography, while parallel layer-set generation supplies visual appearance.
Aligning this representation with the renderer supports many downstream applications like editing
9


and artifact reuse. Enforcing generalization to more layouts and improving transformations under
changed occlusion remain important research directions.

DATA
Training LayerCanvas requires a form of supervision that, to our knowledge, no existing corpus pro-
vides: infographics paired at the level of individual, renderer-aligned layers. A usable training record
must expose, for a single graphic, (i) a structural program that fixes geometry, hierarchy, paint order,
and typography; (ii) an aestheticized, text-free rendering of that program; and (iii) a decomposition of
that rendering into ordered, mutually non-overlapping RGBA layers with amodal and visible masks.
Public SVG datasets supply programs but no rich appearance, while raster infographic collections
supply appearance but discard element ownership, occlusion, and text metadata. We therefore synthe-
size the corpus with a multi-agent generation pipeline that couples a large language model, which
designs and validates editable SVG programs, with a diffusion image editor, which aestheticizes only
the non-text visual content. The pipeline is designed so that every artifact LayerCanvas consumes at
training time, namely the colorless structural input, the text-free target, the per-layer amodal/visible
assets, and the graph of overlap and hierarchy relations, is produced deterministically from an SVG
the agent has already checked, rather than recovered from pixels after the fact.
This section describes how concepts are sourced from an educational course catalog (§A.1), how the
multi-agent pipeline turns each concept into a validated paired record (§A.2), how those records are
decomposed into layers, augmented, and validated (§A.3), the resulting scale, splits, and provenance
(§A.4), an analysis of the appearance statistics the pipeline induces (§A.5), and the implementation
and compute details needed to reproduce it (§A.6).

We seed generation with concepts drawn from popular online educational courses. Each concept is
a short, self-contained instructional idea (for example, “handling missing values in Python,” “the
tip-of-the-iceberg model of specialist agents,” or “the Power BI ↔Excel round-trip”), and it carries
the identifier and title of the course it was extracted from. Anchoring the corpus in real course material
keeps the distribution of topics, terminology, and visual conventions close to genuine educational

A.2
MULTI-AGENT GENERATION PIPELINE
The pipeline proceeds in four stages. A design stage authors a structured, editable SVG program
from a concept; an extraction stage renders the structural input and the text-free layout and serializes
structured conditioning; an aestheticization stage restyles each editable region with a diffusion image
editor; and a composition stage mattes and reinserts the styled illustrations in paint order. All
appearance in the final target originates in the SVG program or in the diffusion edits of its tagged
regions, never in a free-form raster of the whole scene.
Stage A: structured SVG design (LLM).
A design agent, built on a large language model, turns
a concept into a complete, standalone SVG on a fixed 1536 × 1024 canvas. Beyond producing an
aesthetic educational layout, the agent must mark at least one editable-visual group: a coarse vector
placeholder standing in for a tangible, photographable subject (a robot, a database cylinder, a brass
scale, a stack of coins). Each such group is emitted with a strict, machine-parseable signature,
<g id="editable-visual-k" data-role="editable-visual"
data-visual-desc="..." data-edit-prompt="...">...</g>,
14


where data-edit-prompt is the exact, self-contained instruction later handed to the diffusion
editor. To prevent the corpus from collapsing onto a single visual template, the agent is assigned one
of fourteen layout archetypes per concept (hero-left, hub-and-spoke, comparison-split, circular-cycle,
above-below, quadrant-grid, timeline, and others), rotated deterministically across a batch and treated
as an adaptable starting structure rather than a rigid template. The prompt enforces explicit layout
discipline (shared alignment gridlines, equal gaps for repeated elements, symmetry matched to the
number of editable visuals) and an aesthetic direction tuned for legibility (light airy backgrounds,
per-section color coding, a strong title/subtitle/takeaway hierarchy). A guard explicitly forbids
screens, dashboards, spreadsheets, and other flat, text-bearing objects from being marked editable,
because such subjects photorealize poorly and reintroduce garbled text; those elements remain crisp
vector content. The agent operates in a render-and-inspect loop: it rasterizes its SVG, examines the
image, and performs at least one revision pass to correct misalignment, uneven spacing, dead space,
or low-contrast text before emitting a manifest of its editable groups.
Stage B: structural extraction and conditioning.
From the validated SVG we deterministically
derive the model’s inputs. The structural input (Fig. 7, left) is produced by “rawifying” the SVG, that
is, stripping color, fonts, gradients, and textures, setting text in a neutral face, and rendering to a flat
raster, so that it encodes placement, grouping, and paint order without revealing target appearance.
We also render a text-free layout in which all recursive text nodes are removed. The record captures
the scene as typed metadata: every text run with its content, semantic role (title/heading/label),
normalized bounding box, font family, size, weight, and fill; every illustration with its identifier,
description, the data-edit-prompt, its bounding box, and its z-index; and a global style block
listing the design palette (hex swatches), font families, and mean corner radius. This record is the
interface between deterministic structure and diffusion aestheticization used throughout the method.
Stage C: illustration aestheticization (diffusion editor).
Each editable-visual crop is rendered in
isolation and passed to a diffusion image editor (Qwen-Image-Edit Qwen Team (2025b)) that repaints
only that region into a rich illustration on a plain white studio background. A style controller makes
the requested look dominant: it strips residual realism adjectives from the agent’s subject description,
prepends a leading style directive, and swaps a matching negative prompt, so a single subject can
be realized in any of six styles (photoreal, flat2d, soft3d, cartoon, watercolor, isometric) while its
identity and composition are preserved. The middle column of Fig. 7 shows the resulting text-free
targets, in which the coarse placeholders have become detailed objects but every label region remains
blank.
Stage D: matting and z-ordered composition.
The styled illustration is cut from its white back-
ground with an alpha matte (rembg/u2net (Gatis, 2025)), cleaned to its largest connected component
and feathered, fitted to the placeholder’s bounding box, and composited back into the scene at exactly
the illustration’s original z-index. Because the surrounding layout is rebuilt from the SVG with the
editable groups removed, the realistic render occupies the placeholder footprint without disturbing
any text or vector element, and soft contact shadows are added programmatically and kept off text
and table regions. The output is the final paired target (Fig. 7, right): the aestheticized, text-free
composite with the preserved vector text layer rendered crisply on top.
A.3
LAYER DECOMPOSITION, AUGMENTATION, AND VALIDATION
Ordered non-overlap layer decomposition.
For every sample we additionally emit the amodal
layer decomposition. Each top-level layer is rendered in isolation to recover its amodal silhouette
(its complete support as if nothing occluded it) and its visible mask (the pixels surviving occlusion
by all higher-z layers); their difference is exactly the occluded region. Editable layers take their
amodal pixels from the matted realistic render, reconstructed with the same parameters used in
composition, so that they agree with the final target. We also record, for every pair of layers whose
amodal silhouettes intersect, an overlap edge annotated with the back/front assignment and the
covered fraction. These masks and edges are what make the ordered non-overlap layer sets and the
graph-biased communication of the method well defined; they are computed deterministically from
the renderer, not discovered from pixels.

Figure 8: Representative dataset infographics. The corpus spans diverse layout archetypes (above-

Style augmentation via illustration reuse.
Because Stage A (LLM design) and Stage B (struc-

them: given an already-built sample with its per-layer amodal RGBA and its tagged editable-visual
illustration, we generate additional (input →target) pairs whose only difference is the visual style
of the illustration. The existing target layers and structured conditioning are reused verbatim; only
Stage C (re-style the illustration) and Stage D (re-matte, re-compose) are rerun on-GPU. This yields
multiple stylistic realizations of the same structural layout at a fraction of the cost, and a denser-
composition variant reuses previously generated illustrations inside new, higher-overlap layouts to
increase occlusion depth.
Validation and quality control.
Records are filtered at several points. The design agent’s own
inspect-and-revise loop rejects layouts with misalignment, empty regions, or unreadable text before a
sample is emitted. Structural extraction validates that text nodes are fully removed from visual targets
and that every illustration crop is isolated with clear margins. Composition validates alpha coverage
of the matte and geometric fit within the target footprint, and layer reconstruction is checked against
the composite so that within-group elements do not overwrite one another and every cross-group
overlap follows SVG paint order. Samples failing these checks are regenerated or dropped.

A.5
DATASET ANALYSIS: COLOR COMPOSITION IS COUPLED AND LEARNABLE
A natural question for a synthesized corpus is whether it contains learnable structure beyond each
element in isolation, in particular, whether the colors of different elements within a graphic are related,
as they are in human-designed infographics where palette choices are made jointly. We quantify this
on a 1,000-sample subset by comparing, within each graphic, the colors of the vector design and the
colors of the diffusion-generated illustrations, all in perceptual CIELAB space.
Two findings emerge (Fig. 9). First, the vector side is palette-coherent by construction: text fills are
drawn verbatim from the design palette (median ∆E = 0 to the nearest palette swatch), confirming
that the LLM designs a single coherent color scheme per graphic. Second, and less trivially, the
diffusion-generated illustrations are measurably coupled to that vector palette even though the
editor sees only a per-illustration subject prompt. Aggregate illustration color and palette color are
positively correlated per channel, most strongly on the blue-yellow (b∗, r = 0.35) and red-green (a∗,
r = 0.22) axes and weakest on lightness (L∗, r = 0.11); a permutation test rejects independence
(pperm < 10−3, observed-to-null coupling ratio 1.14×); and a canonical correlation analysis between
illustration and palette color reaches a first correlation of ρ1 = 0.41. The coupling is also learnable:
knowing the palette improves prediction of an illustration’s b∗by ∆R2 = +0.13 beyond the style
label alone, and the leading two principal components of the joint color representation explain 0.44
of the variance versus 0.24 under a shuffled null.
Mechanistically, this coupling is authorial rather than injected: the same language model writes
both the design palette and each illustration’s subject prompt, so color intent leaks through word
choice, and it survives the editor’s independent re-styling. This is a desirable property for our setting:
it means the corpus rewards a model that coordinates appearance across layers rather than styling
each layer in isolation, which is precisely the behavior the correlated, communicating streams of
LayerCanvas are designed to capture.

A.6
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The design agent is driven by a large language model (Claude Opus 4.8 Anthropic (2026)), accessed
through a standard chat-completion API, and invoked with a render-and-inspect tool loop that supplies
the rasterized SVG back to the model for revision. SVGs are rendered with rsvg-convert (La-
chowicz et al., 2014) at the fixed 1536 × 1024 canvas. Illustration aestheticization uses the Qwen-
Image-Edit Qwen Team (2025b) diffusion image editor in bfloat16, with classifier-free guidance
5.0, 40 denoising steps, and a fixed seed for reproducibility; crop sizes are snapped to multiples
of 32 within [256, 1024]. Standalone asset generation, where needed, uses the Qwen-Image Qwen
Team (2025a) text-to-image model. Background removal uses rembg Gatis (2025) with the u2net
matting model; a border-connected white-key matte is available as a fast alternative for saturated
subjects. Color analysis is performed in CIELAB (via scikit-image (van der Walt et al., 2014))
with k-means dominant-color extraction and permutation, canonical-correlation, and PCA baselines.
The stages have different resource needs, so we separate them. The language-model stage requires
only API access and runs on a CPU host, while the GPU stages (aestheticization, matting, composition,
and layer rendering) run on NVIDIA H100 and H200 GPUs. Large batches are executed on 8×H100
and 8×H200 nodes. The style-augmentation and dense-reuse passes require no language-model
access, since they reuse the previously validated structural layers and only rerun the GPU restyle-and-
compose steps.

B.1
ADDITIONAL METHOD DETAILS
Renderer-derived masks and hidden areas.
The offline renderer removes all SVG text nodes and
records a text-free target, per-element RGBA assets, element types, normalized boxes, parent-child
links, paint order, and masks. For element ei, the full (amodal) mask Ai covers its entire area before
later elements are painted, while Vi retains only the pixels visible in the final rendering. Their
difference Hi = (Ai −Vi)+ identifies content hidden by an element in front. Because per-element
assets are rendered before composition, Hi contains the element’s true appearance rather than a blank
region. This provides a direct training target for content revealed by deletion, translation, or z-order
edits. Illustration prompts and text regions are routed to their owning visual elements, although text
remains absent from image targets.
Properties of ordered grouping.
The rule in Eq. (4) never places an element in the same or an
earlier group than an already processed element that it overlaps. Thus every overlap keeps SVG
paint order, while Eq. (3) guarantees that elements sharing a generated layer cannot overwrite one
another. The groups are not based on meaning or element type: different element types may share a
generated layer whenever their full masks do not overlap. The layers are also not generated one at a
time; the group index determines composition order but all layers are sampled simultaneously. After
a geometric edit, grouping and graph information are recomputed without resampling unaffected
layers.

Style and boundary paths.
The communicated summary ehg is returned to the image features

because the style and boundary output layers start at zero.

Lbnd matches values and spatial gradients near ancestor boundaries. For every auxiliary term, we
first recover the one-step clean estimate bx1,g for each group from its noisy state and predicted velocity
using Eq. (11). This estimate remains in the packed model representation; training does not decode a
full RGB image through the VAE for these losses. Only the composition loss combines the estimated
groups: it applies their amodal masks, sorts them by SVG paint order, and compares the result with
the corresponding composition of the clean target groups. The remaining auxiliary losses operate
directly on individual clean group estimates.
The full objective is
L = λlayerLlayer + λcompLcomp + λhidLhid + λbndLbnd
(12)
Exact typography.
All SVG text nodes are removed from diffusion targets, and prompts request
blank title, label, and caption regions. We retain the Unicode string, role, bounding box, font family
and style, alignment, transforms, hierarchy, and owning visual element. During generation, the
text-free composite is placed beneath a transparent vector-rendered text layer Rtext(T ), as specified
in Eq. (2). Spelling, line breaks, alignment, and font edits therefore require only vector re-rendering,
not another diffusion pass.

C
DOWNSTREAM APPLICATIONS
This section demonstrates how the hybrid raster-SVG layer-set representation supports deterministic
editing and reuse after generation. Each application uses the trained grouped layer-set model or a
previously generated output from that model; operations performed only on the persistent structured
representation are identified explicitly.
C.1
TEXT EDITING AND LOCALIZATION
End-to-end raster generators must repaint text whenever wording, typography, or language changes.
Because their glyph generation is data-driven, they may also favor the visual conventions of a
dominant training language rather than faithfully rendering a requested, less represented script.
LayerCanvas instead generates only the visual substrate and preserves typography as styled SVG
nodes. Copy, style, and locale changes therefore require no diffusion pass and cannot alter the
generated artwork.
Implementation.
Each text node retains its Unicode content, position, font family, size, weight,
style, anchor, fill color, transforms, and parent ownership. Editing replaces only the requested
attributes before the text-only SVG is rasterized and alpha-composited over the unchanged model
output. Localization uses a script-appropriate local font and shaping direction; nonlinguistic values
such as numbers and code may remain unchanged. Figure 13 shows the same generated layers with
text rendered in four languages.
Deterministic behavior.
This operation is a property of the representation rather than a learned
prediction: the requested node attributes are written directly into SVG and rendered by the vector-text
runtime. Thus the rendered language follows the provided Unicode content and selected font rather
than a language distribution learned by the image model. The only external requirements are an
available font with the requested glyphs and enough space in the retained text box for the new copy.
If a translation changes line length substantially, standard SVG layout operations can resize or reflow
the text box without regenerating any image layer.
25


C.2
INDEPENDENT LAYER ANIMATION
We adapt the zero-shot animation principle of Text2Video-Zero Khachatryan et al. (2023), which
shows that the motion and appearance priors of a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model can
produce a temporally linked sequence without video-specific training. A flattened document would
still expose the complete page to this generative update, allowing unrelated illustrations, layout, and
typography to drift. LayerCanvas instead lets the temporal operation target one named image layer
while all other layers remain fixed.
Implementation.
Following Text2Video-Zero, we derive a short motion trajectory from an image
diffusion prior rather than training a video model. The user selects an image layer, optionally together
with a bounded subregion, and specifies endpoint states or a motion transform. Intermediate states
are produced through latent interpolation or motion-guided warping, decoded only for the selected
content, and alpha-composited over the unchanged layer set at each timestep. This final step is
specific to our representation: every unselected image layer and the full-resolution SVG typography
are copied identically into every frame. In Figure 5, the selected subregion is an eye band covering
6.6% of the canvas.
Discussion.
The generative component supplies motion only; spatial isolation comes from the
LayerCanvas representation. Pixels outside the selected region and the complete vector-text layer are
not predicted at subsequent timesteps, so they remain bit-identical by construction. This converts
a global zero-shot image animation method into a local document operation and avoids spending
temporal model capacity on content that should not move.
C.3
ASSET REUSE AND RECOMPOSITION
A flattened image binds every illustration, diagram, and decorative element to its original page.
Reusing one of them requires segmentation followed by inpainting or regeneration of the surrounding
content. LayerCanvas instead keeps generated visual groups as addressable image layers with their
own alpha and paint order, so an element can become a reusable asset after generation.
Implementation.
A selected layer is copied with its image content and alpha mask. Standard layer
operations then translate or scale it, adjust its appearance within the mask, and assign a new position
in the target paint order. Multiple selected layers may be placed onto a new background and combined
with newly authored SVG text. These operations act on the saved representation and do not require
segmentation, inpainting, or another diffusion pass. Figure 14 shows three layers reused in a new
composition.
Discussion.
Reuse preserves the generated appearance of an asset while decoupling it from the
document in which it first appeared. A collection of generated layers can therefore serve as a project-
specific asset library: recurring illustrations or diagram components may be placed across related
documents while layout and text remain independently editable. The resulting composition is still
structured, so the reused layer can be moved, hidden, or replaced again later.
C.4
LAYOUT RETARGETING AND RESPONSIVE RESIZE
A design may need to appear as a presentation slide, poster, and mobile graphic. Resizing a flattened
page either distorts its elements or removes content through cropping. LayerCanvas retains the
document as addressable groups, allowing the same generated content to be arranged for a different
canvas.
Implementation.
Each illustration, diagram, card, and text block carries a bounding box and group
assignment. A simple reflow solver packs these groups in reading order, using more columns on
wide canvases and a vertical stack on narrow ones. Generated image groups are scaled uniformly,
while SVG text can be re-rendered for the new box. The operation changes only layout metadata and
recomposition; the image layers are not generated again. Figure 15 shows the same content adapted
to slide, poster, and mobile layouts.

Retargeting becomes a document-layout problem rather than an image-generation prob-

C.5
COMMAND-DRIVEN INTERACTIVE EDITING
Interactive authoring is naturally expressed through commands such as “remove this card,” “emphasize
this panel,” or “swap this icon.” Prompt-based regeneration may redraw the entire canvas. Named
layer groups instead allow the command to be grounded to one element and executed as a local,
reversible edit.
Implementation.
A lightweight interpreter maps a command to {ACTION, TARGET} using ac-
tion phrases and aliases for the document’s semantic groups. We support removing, emphasizing,
swapping, and adding elements. The executor applies the operation to the targeted SVG group or

Discussion.
The language interface does not edit pixels directly. It selects an operation and
resolves a semantic target, after which the persistent layer representation defines the exact region
and compositing behavior. Commands can therefore be previewed, reversed, or chained without
resampling the full document. A learned language parser could replace the lightweight interpreter
without changing the underlying editing operations or their locality.
C.6
SVG-SPACE REFINEMENT AND FAITHFUL DIFFUSION PROPAGATION
An upstream layout proposal may contain a misplaced, mislabeled, or incorrectly styled element. In a
raster-only pipeline this often requires a new prompt or manual pixel editing. In LayerCanvas, the
SVG is a persistent control surface: the element can be corrected directly, and the model propagates
that correction to the raster without retraining or redesigning the rest of the document.
Implementation.
We deliberately perturb one SVG element by translating, resizing, recoloring,
relabeling, or replacing it. The incorrect and corrected conditions use the same sampling noise so that
differences are attributable to the structural edit. Composition uses each group’s full-resolution vector
alpha mask rather than an upsampled latent-grid mask. Figure 6 presents the incorrect condition, the
SVG edit, and the corrected render as a matched triptych.
Discussion.
The structured intermediate remains editable after an initial generation rather than
becoming a disposable prompt. A user or upstream layout system can inspect the result, correct
the responsible SVG group, and regenerate the affected visual content under the revised condition.
Fixed noise helps preserve the remaining appearance, while recomputing masks and overlap relations
allows changes in position, scale, or occlusion to propagate consistently.

D
LIMITATIONS, ETHICS, AND BROADER IMPACT.
Despite these benefits, LayerCanvas requires a valid source SVG and inherits errors and biases in
its content, layout, and training data. Deterministic typography preserves supplied text but does not
ensure factuality, accessibility, or culturally appropriate localization, while edits that change occlusion
or z-order may affect unintended regions. Responsible deployment therefore requires documented
data provenance, license and bias checks, generated-content disclosure, and human review.
