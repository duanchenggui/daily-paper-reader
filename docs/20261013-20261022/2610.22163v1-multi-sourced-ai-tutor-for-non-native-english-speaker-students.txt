Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being adopted as teaching tools in computing education, particularly for developing AI tutors capable of answering student questions. With the rise in enrollment of international students in computing programs, researchers have begun to explore the difficulties that non-native English speaker (NNES) students face in computing courses due to language barriers. To aid such students, we developed a general-purpose AI tutor system that can process student questions in foreign languages (currently focused on Mandarin) and provide bilingual responses. Our system also incorporates the functionality of generating a glossary of technical terms and their explanations to enhance comprehension. Our approach utilizes a specialized Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline with a novel question extraction mechanism, enabling us to effectively utilize a diverse array of course materials for answering student queries. We evaluated our AI tutor using 91 Piazza posts, demonstrating that it could answer 83.52% of the posts correctly and in a helpful manner. The tutor also provided high-quality translations for 76.92% of the questions and generated technical term glossaries for 64.47% of them. Our findings underscore the potential of multi-source RAG pipeline in enhancing AI tutor systems, offering support for non-native speakers that are challenging to provide with traditional supporting staff of a computing course. 

Keywords: large language model, non-native English speaker students, computing education 

# 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have profoundly transformed com-puting education, showcasing remarkable capabilities in solving computing assignments [18, 20]. LLMs are also being used for facilitating instructional activities, such as generating assignment questions [19], providing automated grading [22], and notably, de-veloping AI tutor systems that address student queries. Furthermore, as classrooms become increasingly multicultural, the need for inclu-sivity in educational materials and technologies grows. Non-native English speaker (NNES) students, in particular, face language barri-ers and a lack of confidence, which often causes hesitation to seek help from instructors or TAs. Our AI tutor system seeks to assist NNES students’ learning by accepting questions in the students’ native language (specifically Mandarin), delivering responses bilin-gually, and generating a technical term glossary to ensure equitable access to educational materials. Most AI tutors [3, 10, 11] utilize Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [9], which enhances question-answering by dynamically re-trieving and synthesizing relevant information from a data repository, ensuring accuracy and contextual relevance. Our RAG-based GPT-4 tutor differs from systems that rely on a single source, such as lecture captions or textbooks. Instead, it integrates a comprehensive range of course materials, including lecture notes, project descriptions, quizzes, past exams, and the course website, enabling holistic data retrieval. Two specialized mechanisms have been developed to enhance our AI tutor. Firstly, our system incorporates a translation stage that converts Mandarin questions into English before applying RAG, enabling full leverage of RAG’s capabilities. Secondly, our system employs a question extraction mechanism that addresses instances where a student’s query references only the question number instead of providing the question details, by precisely identifying and retrieving details of the referred question. We evaluated our system using 91 Piazza posts, demonstrating that it can accurately and helpfully answer student questions and generate technical term glossary in Mandarin. Our work focuses on answering the following research questions: 

• RQ1 . What is the quality of the translation stage and to what extent does it improve RAG’s performance in processing non-English questions? 

• RQ2 . What is the accuracy of the question extraction stage and to what extent does it enhance the system’s ability to manage diverse course materials and student questions? 

• RQ3 . What is the relevance and usefulness of RAG’s results concerning the questions? 

• RQ4 . What is the quality of the AI tutor’s responses in terms of correctness, helpfulness, solution revelation, translation, and technical term glossary generation? 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 AI Tutors 

Since the debut of Large Language Models (LLMs), researchers have consistently explored their potential applications in computing education. Beyond aiding student learning by providing thorough code explanations [12], step-wise worked examples [5], and prompt problems [1], as well as facilitating various instructional activities, LLMs have proven effective as the basis for educational question-answering (QA) systems, or in other words, AI tutors. Hicke et al. [3] investigated the effectiveness of various optimiza-tion techniques in LLMs for answering questions collected from a CS1 course Piazza. They enhanced Llama-2 using Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) on textbooks and lab manuals, supervised finetun-ing with selected Piazza QA pairs, and reinforcement learning with human feedback; and applied RAG to GPT-4. They show that these techniques resulted in substantial improvements: Llama-2’s answer accuracy (factual correctness & groundedness) increased from 0.30 to 0.58, and GPT-4’s from 0.40 to 0.73, while usefulness (practical values to human instructors) increased from 0.18 to 0.50 for Llama-2 and from 0.28 to 0.65 for GPT-4. Liu and M’hiri [10] developed a virtual teaching assistant (TA) using GPT-3.5 and RAG on assignment documents. The virtual TA arXiv:2610.22163v1 [cs.AI] 15 Oct 2026 first performs embedding searches to compare students’ questions with assignment documents, preventing the revelation of answers to the current assignments to students who directly ask for them. Subsequently, the TA generates responses tailored to each question category and conducts quality checks to ensure response accuracy and avoid solution leak. Furthermore, Liu et al. [11] and Kazemitabaar et al. [6] developed their own QA chatbots and deployed them in real course settings. The two chatbots have distinct features: the prior utilized GPT-4 with RAG on lecture captioning and structured prompts to generate responses, while the latter used the few-shot learning framework to prevent code revelation. Both chatbots maintained a high correctness rate in resolving student inquires throughout the semester. Moreover, students expressed satisfaction with these tools in the feedback survey, stating they appreciated having such a "24/7 personal tutor" and the opportunity to "work alongside AI." Most of the AI tutor systems above use RAG based on a single source format, such as textbooks, assignment documents, or lecture captions. Our AI tutor aims to incorporate all documents in a course, including lecture materials and readings, lab and project description documents, quizzes and past exams, as well as the course website, by developing appropriate data pipelines and prompt techniques for each type of course material. 

2.2 Non-Native English Speakers in Computing Educa-tion 

The enrollment of international students in computing programs has increased by 14% to 298,523 during the 2022-23 academic year. Notably, 23% of them pursued mathematics and computer science, and 19% studied engineering [4]. These students often face language barriers and confidence issues, which negatively impact their learning experience in computing courses. In response, researchers are exploring tailored supportive strategies for this demographic. Polishchuk [15] surveyed 124 first-year non-native English speaker (NNES) engineering and agriculture students, identifying several key communication barriers, including vocabulary and grammar difficulties and challenges with accents and slang. Notably, 85.5% of students reported translating utterances to their native language, which hindered their fluency, while 61.25% were afraid of making mistakes, and 47.5% felt nervous speaking in front of the class. Polishchuk recommended enhancing collaborative work and com-munication practice outside the classroom. Guo [2] noted how confidence issues affect NNES’s communica-tion with instructors and TAs. Via a survey of 840 respondents from 86 countries on a programming education website, this study found students reluctant to participate in discussions or ask questions due to fear of errors and being misunderstood. They preferred instructional materials in simplified English, supplemented with visuals and inline dictionaries. Guo suggested peer mentoring and language support workshops to improve participation and confidence. Soosai Raj et al. [16, 17] conducted a four-week experiment with second-year engineering students in India, comparing those taught only using English to those receiving bilingual instruction in English and Tamil. The study found that bilingual teaching reduced cognitive load, improved comprehension of programming concepts, and boosted student engagement and confidence. Lau and Yuen [8] noted that K-12 students in Hong Kong understood programming better with bilingual instruction, highlighting the value of native language incorporation for NNES students. To address the challenges faced by NNES students, we aim to develop an AI tutor that accepts questions in the student’s native language and provides bilingual answers to facilitate understanding of complex computing concepts. This approach will also help build confidence among students who may hesitate to approach their instructors and TAs. Since our authors are mainly native Mandarin speakers, we implemented our prototypical system for Mandarin. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Students’ Questions 

We collected 620 questions from a semester’s Piazza posts for an intermediate-level Data Science (DS) programming course of 400 students 1. We excluded 238 posts that included images for two reasons: (1) they are difficult to transcribe, either because they are entire desktop screenshots with only a small portion containing relevant information or they involve complex plots and graphs; (2) the GPT-4 API vision service was prohibitively expensive prior to the release of GPT-4o. The analysis focused on the remaining 382 text-based posts, which we cleaned by removing duplicates, identifiable information, unnecessary HTML formatting, and enclosing code snippets and error messages in backticks. For Piazza posts, students must tag their posts with instructor-defined folders: exams (exam 1, exam 2, final), quizzes, programming projects (P1, P2, ..., P7), labs, logistics, and others. One author used these student-tagged folders and the post content to sort the posts into four categories: 90 logistics, 54 conceptual, 41 environment setup, and 197 programming posts. Logistic questions dealt with syl-labus specifics and demands for clarifications on acceptable coding practices and plotting requirements. Conceptual questions typically pertained to quiz and exam content. Environment setup questions covered the setup and maintenance of virtual machines and Jupyter-Lab, package installations, and git-related issues. Programming questions primarily revolved around project development challenges, addressing issues such as implementation of specific functionalities, debugging, and optimization for code efficiency. We then generated the Mandarin dataset by translating the Piazza posts into Mandarin using the Google Translate API 2. To ensure precision, all translations were manually reviewed and edited by a native Mandarin-speaking author. Sections previously marked with backticks were excluded from the translation. 

3.2 AI Tutor Data Pipeline 

A flowchart of our pipeline is shown in Figure 1. It includes 4 stages: translation, question extraction, RAG, and response generation 3.

3.2.1 Translation 

Our pipeline processes two types of inputs: the original English Piazza questions and their human-verified Mandarin translation mentioned in Section 3.1. When a question is received in Mandarin, the model translates it into English using the Microsoft Azure AI Translate API 4. We opted for translation over a Mandarin-dedicated LLM to leverage the capabilities of GPT-4, which remains state-of-the-art, especially in programming context. 

3.2.2 Question Extraction (QE) 

A significant portion (about 35%) of the questions collected from Piazza relate to specific projects, quizzes, and prior exam questions, often referencing only the question number (e.g., "P2 Q10" and "FA22 Exam 2 Q10") in the subject line, without including the question’s content in the post. Unlike typical computer science projects that require students to implement well-known data structures or algorithms, DS projects involve sequential (numbered) analysis tasks on given datasets, making it important for the model to receive the original question’s details. Furthermore, this step is irreplaceable by RAG as both embedding-based and keyword-based retrieval methods face challenges in extracting the exact question from the course materials only using the question number. Embedding-based methods struggle due to the short and numeric nature of these references, while keyword-based systems encounter difficulties due to variability of question number abbreviations (e.g., "Project 2 Question 10" and "Fall 2022 Midterm 2 Question 10"). To address these issues, we used the Llama 3 8B model to generate standardized question numbers from student posts. This model is hosted using Ollama on a machine with a Nvidia GeForce RTX 4090. This link, https://bit.ly/3Lv3Eiw , provides the few-shot prompt we used for the QE task. The prompt is structured as follows: the task description, the input format accompanied by a brief description of the course materials, the expected output format, and several examples of input and output. The generated standardized question number enables us to accurately fetch the question content from a question bank using a rule-based algorithm. The question bank is created as text files by parsing past exams and solutions (PDFs) and Canvas quizzes using fitz and Canvas API, respectively. QE is a crucial step that ensures the subsequent RAG process retrieves the most relevant content related to the specific questions in the student’s posts, thereby improving the accuracy and relevancy of the generated responses. 

3.2.3 RAG 

We structured our RAG using LangChain [7] with a comprehensive vector store of all course materials. This includes lecture notes (Python scripts and Jupyter Notebooks), PowerPoint slides, work-sheets (PDFs), complementary readings (Python scripts and Jupyter Notebooks), project and lab repositories (Python scripts, Jupyter Notebooks, and Markdown), and the course website pages (HTML). Various parsers and splitters were employed to manage these di-verse data types. For parsing, we converted Jupyter Notebooks to Python scripts using nbconvert, and PowerPoint slides and PDFs to Markdown using pptx2md and fitz. Markdown and HTML content are initially split using corresponding Langchain Markdown and HTML Header Text splitters, and further split by RecursiveChar-acterTextSplitter in Python for excessively long chunks. Jupyter Notebooks, which are already formatted using Markdown headers, are first split using a modified version of LangChain Markdown-HeaderTextSplitter for Jupyter Notebooks and then further split by the RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter . We also included the source name in the split chunks to allow reference to course materials in the generated response. The vector store is then created using FAISS [13] with OpenAI’s text-embedding-ada-002 embedding. In similarity search phase, we input both the student post and any extracted questions. We exclude past exams and Canvas quizzes from our search to avoid skewing results towards similar exam or quiz questions, which could prevent the retrieval of lecture materials. We retrieve the top 10 most relevant results. 

3.2.4 Response Generation 

Responses are generated using the Microsoft Azure OpenAI GPT-4 API. This link, https://bit.ly/3SeXvLg , provides the system and user prompts for the generation task. In the system prompt, we direct the model to function as an AI tutor for non-native English speaker students, stipulating that it should not directly reveal solutions but rather provide hints, explanations, or suggestions. We instruct the model to avoid writing code snippets. We also ask the model to cite RAG results accordingly using the source path. The response is required to be in both English and Mandarin. Any technical vocabulary or error messages that might challenge non-native English speaker students is asked to be compiled into a glossary at the end of the response to aid understanding. In the user prompt, we include the original student post, any extracted question numbers and content, and the results from the RAG.

4.2.1 Translation 

The translation stage showed high quality in translating Mandarin posts to English, answering the first part of RQ1. The Microsoft Azure AI Translation API failed to translate only 2 of the 382 Mandarin posts; these remained untranslated. The translations of all other posts retained their original meanings and all essential information. 

4.2.2 Question Extraction 

The question number extractor can accurately fetch question numbers for all categories of questions, answering the first part of RQ2. The extractor was deemed correct if it outputs the correct question number when the original post (folders, subject, and content) contained sufficient information to identify a specific question. If no specific question could be determined, no question number was output. As indicated in the first row of Table 1, the extractor achieved accuracy rates of 97.78%, 90.74%, 100%, and 97.46% for logistics, conceptual, environment setup, and programming questions, respectively. The extractor also performs comparably well on Mandarin and translated posts compared to the original English posts. The second and third rows of Table 1 show the percentages of overlap between the QE results for English and Mandarin/translated posts. The percent-ages of overlaps between English and Mandarin posts are 98.89%, 72.22%, 100%, and 97.97% for the four categories, respectively. The percentages of overlaps between English and translated posts are 95.56%, 74.07%, 100%, and 97.46%. We suspect the high similarity between QE results for the Mandarin and translated posts is due to the fact that question numbers often appear in abbreviations like "P2 Q10", which remain unchanged in both Mandarin and translated posts. Most failed cases occurred in the conceptual category when the original posts did not contained a question number, yet the QE results for the Mandarin and translated posts still attempted to find one. 

4.2.3 RAG 

To assess the RAG and Response Generation stages, we evaluated the top 15% most viewed question posts and randomly selected 10% from the remaining posts from each category. This test set include 21 logistics, 14 conceptual, 9 environment setup, and 47 programming questions, totaling 91 posts. This approach ensured coverage of both the questions of highest interest to students and less common queries that might present unique challenges. We computed two metrics from human evaluation to assess the RAG’s performance: relevance and helpfulness. Relevance measured whether the retrieved chunk was pertinent to the question post, while helpfulness determined if the chunk provided an answer or significant contextual information to the question. RAG cannot replace QE. Firstly, it cannot differentiate between posts that do and do not contain question numbers. Secondly, it does not perform as well as our question number extractor. Among the 91 evaluated posts, 6 logistics, 6 conceptual, and 24 programming posts contain a question number. When RAG searched all course materials including quizzes and exams, it only correctly retrieved 3 (50%), 4 (66.67%), and 20 (83.33%) of the questions within the top 10 searches, respectively. Furthermore, of the 20 correctly retrieved programming questions, only 14 (70%) are the top 10 RAG results. This confirms that QE is crucial in our pipeline to accommodate the special case of student queries that refer questions by numbers but not content, answering the second part of RQ2. Lastly, including quizzes and exams in RAG searches tends to overwhelm the results for conceptual questions with similar quiz and exam questions, averaging 7.5 out of 10, which obscures the retrieval of relevant lecture materials. Therefore, we exclude quizzes and exams in our RAG search, reserving it only at the QE stage. With QE, the RAG results are highly relevant and helpful, which answers RQ3. Among the 10 RAG retrievals, the average number of them being relevant are 6.1, 7.7, 7.3, and 6.6 for logistics, conceptual, environment setup, and programming questions, respectively (first row of Table 2). Additionally, 80.95%, 71.43%, 88.89%, and 89.36% of the four categories of question posts contained at least one RAG retrieval that was helpful to students’ questions. RAG retrievals also performed well with translated posts but less so with Mandarin posts. As shown in the third and fourth rows of Table 2, out of the 10 retrieved documents for all 382 posts, the average numbers of overlaps between RAG results for English and Mandarin/translated posts are 7.0, 5.7, 7.5, 6.8 and 8.5, 7.0, 8.9, 8.2 for the four categories, respectively. 4.2.4 Response Generation 

To answer RQ4, we evaluate the generated responses using the following task-specific metrics: 

• Correctness : Whether the generated output is factually correct and does not contain any misinformation. This metric helps in down voting LLM-generated hallucinations. 

• Helpfulness : Whether the response effectively addresses the student’s question. An answer can be factually correct, but not necessarily situated to the student’s question. 

• Solution Presents in Response : Whether the response directly provides solutions, particularly code snippets, for projects. Our system provides students with hints and directions, not solutions, which we try to achieve by tuning our prompts. 

• Translation Quality : Whether the translation of the generated output from English to Mandarin is free of errors that could impede understanding. Incomplete translations and missing translations are also considered as errors. 

• Generates Technical Term Glossary : Whether the generated output includes a glossary of the technical terms related to the question and the response. The prompt sent to the LLM includes instructions for generating such a glossary. Table 3 presents the evaluation scores for the generated responses, where each metric is binary, scored as 0 or 1. The last column of the table presents the inter-rater reliability for the five generation metrics. These reliability scores measured the initial round of evaluations conducted by two authors and excluding the final adjustments made by the third author in cases of conflict. We utilized Gwet’s AC1 to compute the reliability because it is more stable than Cohen’s Kappa, particularly when a specific binary rating predominates [21]. The Gwet’s AC1 scores are 94%, 85%, 83%, 67%, and 87%, reflecting the consistent evaluation. In terms of correctness, our pipeline demonstrated a high level of accuracy, achieving perfect scores for both conceptual and environ-ment setup questions (100% each), and a high score for programming questions (97.83%). However, the correctness score for logistics questions was lower at 80.95%. This reduction often occurred in situations where students inquired about specific requirements, such as the necessary formatting for a plot or the use of a particular function. In the absence of clear guidance from the RAG results, the model made assumptions rather than acknowledging the lack of available information. The helpfulness of the responses, as shown in the second row of Table 3, varied slightly across categories but generally remained ro-bust. Responses adequately addressed student questions in 61.90% of logistics posts, 93.33% of conceptual posts, 88.89% of environment setup posts, and 89.13% of programming posts. The responses generally avoided disclosing direct answers. As indicated in the third row, none of the logistics, conceptual, and environment setup questions included direct solutions. However, programming questions experienced a modestly higher rate of direct solution revelation at 13.04%, primarily because these responses sometimes included parts of code snippets. Translation quality is crucial for the comprehension of non-native English speaker students. The pipeline provided Mandarin responses without significant errors in most instances, achieving success rates of 71.43%, 86.67%, 77.78% , and 76.09% for logistics, conceptual, environment setup, and programming questions, respectively. Fail-ures typically occurred when responses were not fully translated or lacked Mandarin entirely. When translations were complete, they maintained high quality consistently. The inclusion rate of a technical term glossary is reflected in the final row of Table 3. The results demonstrate that 95.24%, 86.67%, 77.78%, and 86.96% of the queries in the four categories contain a technical term glossary. When the glossary is provided, the terms generated are essential to the questions, uncommonly encountered in everyday English, and their explanations are accurate. 

# 5 Discussion and Future Work 

5.1 Our Contributions 

Our multi-source RAG-based AI tutor system, which utilizes diverse course materials and a question extraction mechanism, distinguishes itself from single-sourced models prevalent in prior work. It can generate precise, contextually informed responses, achieving an average helpfulness score of 83.52% across the evaluated Piazza questions. Additionally, 82.89% of these responses benefit from the use of question extraction or RAG in generating helpful content. Over 76.92% of the responses provide quality responses in Man-darin, and 87.91% of them contain an effective technical term glossary at the end. Non-native English speaker (NNES) students may hesitate to approach instructors/TAs due to language challenges and confidence issues. Our system has the potential to mitigate such challenges by offering a non-judgmental, accessible interface, fostering engagement and inclusivity in computing education. 

5.2 Limitations and Future Work 

For future work, we aim to deploy our AI tutor system to NNES students in a real classroom setting after making the following enhancements. We will work on improving accuracy of question extraction, particularly for translated posts, to ensure all target questions are retrieved. Additionally, we will implement safeguards to prevent the system from inadvertently revealing answers, especially in programming questions where unintended code disclosure can occur. To create a more engaging learning experience, the AI tutor will be designed to interact in a conversational manner rather than providing just a single response to posts. After deployment, we plan to assess the questioning behavior and needs of NNES students and tailor our system based on the feedback received. The release of GPT-4o [14], notable for its advancements in vision and non-English language processing, has reduced the cost of vision services. This development enables us to expand our system to accept questions accompanied by images. We will also assess whether our system’s performance is enhanced with GPT-4o and consider adapting our system for additional languages to test its validity and extend its applicability. Given the time-consuming and resource-intensive nature of human evaluation across our system’s multiple stages, we will explore automated methods of evaluation to ensure that each component of our pipeline functions efficiently and effectively. Moreover, we aim to ensure that our AI tutor evolves alongside the course by continuously updating the RAG vector store throughout the semester to incorporate new course materials. Additionally, we will integrate mechanisms to detect when students ask about currently active quiz or exam questions. Lastly, we intend to develop an interface that allows other instruc-tors to upload their course materials and customize their own AI tutor, further broadening the reach and utility of our pipeline. 

# 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we developed an AI tutor system that processes student questions in Mandarin and delivers bilingual responses that are correct, helpful, and well-translated, and complete with a technical terms glossary, without directly revealing the answers. Our system utilizes Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) with a special question extraction mechanism to answer queries from a diverse array of course materials, including Jupyter Notebooks, Python scripts, PowerPoint slides, and PDFs. Another main contribution of our AI tutor system is the design of the prompts that instruct the GPT-4 model to apporpriately use resources from question extraction and RAG to provide the appropriate level of guidance in responses. Our evaluation results for our AI tutor on data science questions underscore the potential of AI tutors to enhance educational accessibility for non-native English speaking students in computing courses. Future efforts will focus on refining the system through deployment in real classroom settings and expanding language support to further improve student learning experiences.
