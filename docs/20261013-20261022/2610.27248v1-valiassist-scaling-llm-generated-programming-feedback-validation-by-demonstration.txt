Instructors may struggle to fully specify validation criteria upfront, as these often emerge through inspecting concrete examples and revising misaligned feedback. 1 Introduction 

Providing timely, individualized feedback to students is valuable but difficult to sustain at scale. LLMs offer a way to generate programming feedback across many student submissions [29, 45, 46], but such feedback may be incorrect, conflict with course policies or learning objectives, or provide guidance that instructors consider pedagogically in-appropriate [27, 29]. Because these failures can be subtle and consequential, instructors must validate the feedback generated before it reaches students. However, this validation work can be tedious and repetitive [51] across large student submissions where instructors repeatedly encounter recurring error patterns and pedagogical situations that call for similar judgments, but with important contextual differences. Prior work has explored several ways to make human validation of AI-generated content reusable at scale. Some systems ask users to explicitly specify evaluation criteria or policies[17, 31, 34, 56], while others infer criteria or model behavior from users’ judgments on generated outputs[49, 50]. These approaches reduce repeated review, but they also expose a tradeoff between specification effort and situated judgment: criteria written upfront ask users to anticipate relevant cases, whereas judgments made on concrete outputs are easier to provide but may not produce an explicit specification that users can inspect and reuse.[49, 50] To understand how this tradeoff manifests in programming education, we first conducted an observational study of instructors validating AI-generated feedback without criterion-authoring support. Across 178 validation episodes, instructors frequently (69.66%) validated feedback in relation to the corresponding student submission and revised it based on that context. Yet those decisions remained local to individual cases, requiring similar judgments to be reconstructed when related situations appeared elsewhere. This structure led us to recast scalable feedback validation as an end-user programming problem, where the goal is to capture and reuse the logic behind instructors’ situated judgments. Specifically, when instructors validate feedback, they are implicitly expressing conditional logic: when a particular situation occurs in the student submission and generated feedback, the feedback should change in a particular way. Scaling that judgment therefore requires activities similar to programming, including specifying conditions and actions, applying them to new cases, inspecting their behavior, and revising them when they fail. The challenge is to give instructors this programmatic leverage without requiring them to stop their pedagogical work and write a complete program from scratch. Programming-by-Demonstration (PbD) offers one way to support this kind of end-user programming where users demonstrate desired behavior on concrete examples, and the system infers reusable behavior from those demonstrations[12]. Feedback validation, however, poses a particular challenge. The demonstrated action itself, such

The system must infer when the same pedagogical judgment should apply, while instructors must be able to inspect and refine where that abstraction generalizes. Therefore, we ask: How can we scale instructors’ validation of AI-generated feedback by turning their pedagogical judgments into reusable logic that they can understand and refine across diverse student submissions? 

In this paper, we introduce ValiAssist, a feedback validation system that turns instructors’ validation behaviors into reusable, inspectable programs. We call this approach validation by demonstration (VbD). ValiAssist treats an instructor’s revision of AI-generated feedback as a demonstration of an underlying validation criterion, infers reusable pedagogical logic from that demonstration, and allows instructors to examine how the inferred logic behaves across heterogeneous student submissions. We iteratively designed ValiAssist through a comparative formative study to better align this representation with instructors’ validation workflows while keeping the resulting criteria easy to understand and refine. We evaluated ValiAssist in a within-subjects study with 16 instructors experienced in programming and teaching. Compared with a natural-language baseline, participants using ValiAssist created criteria covering more distinct pedagogical intents ( 𝜇 = 2.56 vs. 2 .00, 𝑝 < .05); the resulting feedback satisfied a greater proportion of instructors’ own criteria (91% vs. 57%, 𝑝 < .01) and more than doubled their accuracy in correcting problematic criteria (77% vs. 32%, 

𝑝 < .0001). System interaction logs further showed that instructors did not simply specify criteria once and apply them at scale; instead, they repeatedly applied, inspected, and refined criteria across new submissions. These findings suggest that ValiAssist can help instructors not only turn their judgments into explicit, reusable criteria, but also progressively clarify those criteria by seeing where they did and did not apply across other submissions. This work points to a form of human-AI augmentation in which systems help experts make partially articulated knowledge visible, testable, and reusable. Our research makes the following contributions: 

• Design implications, drawing insights from a formative study, for supporting users in validating large-scale LLM-generated programming feedback by demonstration. 

• A new interaction technique for scalable LLM-generated programming feedback validation, validation-by-demonstration ,that infers users’ validation intentions and represents them in a new DSL for users to flexibly review, adjust, and apply to all other feedback. 

• An interactive system, ValiAssist, that implements our validation-by-demonstration approach with a mixed-initiative interface for effective human-AI co-validation in validating programming feedback at scale. 

2 Related Work 2.1 LLM-assisted Evaluation 

Though presenting generated documents with similarity-based visual augmentation can support users’ sensemaking process [18], validating large-scale generated texts requires complex attention shifts that can induce decision fatigue and lead to an infeasible workload. As LLM and LLM-as-a-judge [56] techniques advance, a growing body of AI-assisted interactive evaluation tools has focused on different aspects of the evaluation process, such as facilitating prompt iteration or refining evaluation criteria [4, 6]. These systems highlight LLM’s power in supporting users to handle large-scale data, despite the substantial needs for users to efficiently specify their intentions, identify, and repair human-LLM misalignments. For example, EvalLM [31] explored methods to help users compose criteria, and EvalGen [50] used users’ ratings of generation results to synthesize validation criteria. 2.2 Pedagogical LLM-Generated Feedback Validation 

Pedagogical feedback is among the most influential factors in learning outcomes [22]. LLMs can generate individualized programming feedback [16, 45, 46], with some approaches incorporating teacher supervision [53]. LLM-generated guidance is already reaching students in programming courses [39, 40]. However, its reliability remains uneven [13], and educators’ concerns extend beyond incorrect outputs [21]. Existing systems offer different ways for instructors to control the guidance students receive. Some constrain generation before outputs are produced: CS50 Duck [40] uses written instructions, TeachTune [26] represents instructions as a node graph, and CodeHelp [39] and Hedderich et al.’s Co-Pilot [24] use structured configuration. Others ask instructors or teaching assistants to rate generated outputs. SPHERE [51] and Edison [44] collect judgments without reusing them. In contrast, CS50’s human-feedback loop [41] and a teacher-in-the-loop DPO pipeline [53] incorporate ratings into the model. GradeHITL [11] instead revises a written rubric based on instructors’ answers to its questions. A third group asks instructors to revise outputs directly, whether to study teachers’ revisions [7], steer LLM assessment of the remaining reports [10], or calibrate an LLM grader [5]. Table 1 organizes these systems by the act the instructor performs and what that act leaves behind to guide outputs the instructor does not inspect. Each region of this space carries a cost. Stating a criterion upfront asks instructors to articulate in advance the cases that only outputs would reveal [39, 40]. Folding judgments into opaque model updates generalizes with almost no added burden [41, 53], but leaves no artifact the instructor can read, contest, or reuse. Revising in place needs no articulation beyond the revision, but its effect stops at the item touched [7]. The space therefore trades articulation burden against retained control. Rating leaves that trade-off unresolved because its signal is scalar: knowing that an output is unsatisfactory does not say which condition produced it. A readable criterion then needs a second channel

2.3 AI-assisted Educational Systems 

Recent work has increasingly applied large language models (LLMs) to educational contexts, supporting both student learning and instructor practices [9, 25]. Here, we focus on the latter: systems that assist instructors with tasks about and beyond providing feedback. For instance, LLMs have been employed in course planning [14], assignment and project design [48]. Other systems use LLMs for cross-disciplinary idea generation [15] or to simulate virtual peers and teaching assistants that enrich classroom interactions and collaborative learning [29, 42]. LLMs have also been integrated into learning analytics pipelines and formative feedback systems to deliver personalized support grounded in students’ behavior and performance [55]. Collectively, these studies underscore the expanding role of LLM-based tools in instructional design, classroom facilitation, and teaching support. At the same time, emerging findings caution that such systems may risk diminishing instructor engagement and cognitive involvement over time [32, 36], further highlighting the importance of keeping instructors in the loop when designing intelligent educational systems. 

2.4 Programming by Demonstration 

Programming by demonstration (PbD) and programming by example (PbE) infer a program from a user’s actions or examples on concrete cases [12, 38]. Because a few demonstrations are consistent with many programs, each system needs a way to choose one that generalizes as intended. FlashFill [19] and SMARTedit [35] represent the programs consistent with the examples and rank them, Wrangler [28] and SUGILITE [37] return the ambiguity to the user through previews and confirmations, and Blue-Pencil [43] recovers repeated edits from a noisy, partial edit stream. In programming education, FixPropagator [23] learns a code transformation from a teacher’s fix to one submission and propagates the teacher’s written feedback to every submission that transformation fixes. ValiAssist follows this line of work in inferring a rule from an action on one case, and, as in PbE, one example under-determines the rule. The difference is what must be inferred: unlike a code fix, a feedback revision shows what should change but not when it should recur, and that condition, over the untouched submission and in semantic labels an LLM judges at execution, cannot be checked symbolically, so the instructor inspects where the rule holds (§5.1.3).

3.2 Design Probe 

To explore whether such a decision can be captured and carried forward, we developed a system probe based on insights drawn from the preliminary observation and prior work. The probe let instructors revise generated feedback, inferred a structured WHEN -THEN criterion from that revision, and applied the criterion across the remaining submissions for review. The probe serves as a pilot artifact used to surface breakdowns in criterion understanding and verification. Two observations from the preliminary study (§3.1) shaped the probe’s criteria format. In the code-first and interleaved workflows instructors reached their decision against the submission, so a criterion has to be able to refer to the submission as well as the message. What instructors then did about a misaligned message was revise it rather than score it, so a criterion also has to carry the edit and not only the judgment. Since instructors moved between the two surfaces while deciding, the generated feedback is displayed side-by-side with the submission (Fig.2A) so that they can cross-check without navigating away. The validation criterion is therefore presented in a two-phase structured format (Fig.2C), where the WHEN statement represents the conditions to be matched over the submission and the feedback, and the THEN statement shows the revision to be applied (probe’s DSL in Appendix §A). After a demonstration edit, the probe system generates validation criteria (Fig.2.E) for the instructor to review and refine. After creating validation criteria, the instructor can apply them to all submissions and review the results (Fig.2) to decide which feedback to keep, so that a decision made on one message reaches the submissions they have not opened. To generate validation criteria from user’s demonstration, we employ an off-the-shelf LLM (i.e., GPT-4o), to process the relevant demonstration context (specifically: programming problem , code submission , user edits log , feedback before revision , and feedback after revision ) with our criteria format.

3.3 Methodology 

We recruited 12 participants (7 male, 5 female) who have both programming ( 𝜇 = 3.75 years, 𝜎 = 1.86) and teaching experiences ( 𝜇 = 2.50 years, 𝜎 = 1.24) to participate in our within-subjects study where they used the probe and a baseline system to validate generated feedback. Participants completed a survey after each condition and a semi-structured interview at the end of the study. We used data from two authentic coding exercises in a local university’s introductory-level Python class. The baseline condition replicated the common practice of LLM-as-a-judge , where users create validation criteria in pure natural language. To explore the influence of criteria formats, the baseline condition also allowed users to create criteria from their demonstration. In each condition, participants use the system to validate LLM-generated feedback for incorrect student submissions, aligning it with their own teaching style and strategy.

4 Design Goals 

Our formative study suggested that demonstration helped instructors express validation intent in context without specifying abstract criteria upfront. However, once criteria were inferred, instructors needed to understand how they were derived, how they would generalize, and how they would affect feedback in execution, since they remained responsible for judging pedagogical appropriateness. Based on these insights and prior work, we derived the following design goals: 

• DG1: Support instructor understanding of how criteria reflect their demonstrations. Natural-language criteria are flexible, but they can be underspecified for reliable execution and difficult to revise systematically. Our formative study suggested that structured criteria can better support expressing and managing validation logic, but only when instructors can understand where each generated component comes from. Therefore, the system should link criterion conditions and actions back to demonstration traces, such as edited spans, referenced code or feedback evidence, and intermediate prompts. 

• DG2: Support instructors’ criterion evolution and maintenance. Validation criteria drifts during validation, and accumulate criteria that may overlap or conflict [50, 52]. Our formative study also suggested that instructors valued being able to adjust structured criteria more directly than natural-language criteria. Therefore, the system should support efficient criterion editing and management, allowing instructors to revise criteria and identify potential redundancy or contradiction with minimal overhead. 

• DG3: Support instructor understanding of criterion generalizability A major challenge we observed was uncertainty about what a criterion would do when applied beyond the demonstrated case. Since AI-assisted validation should keep instructors in the decision-making role [21], the system should make criterion execution inspectable by showing where a criterion matches and what edits it would apply. This allows instructors to verify generalization, diagnose failures, and refine criteria continuously.

5.1.5 Detecting Criterion Drift and Redundancy. 5.2 Technical Pipeline 

5.2.1 Validation Criteria from Demonstration. Algorithm 1 summarizes our criteria inference pipeline. Based on our think-aloud study, we found that a single user revision may contain multiple revision intents, which can often be distinguished by the locations and temporal intervals of edits. Accordingly, the system first aggregates keystroke-level events into edit sessions and decomposes them into atomic revision intents. For each segment, it reconstructs a scoped revision to isolate only the changes relevant to that intent, generates multiple candidate rules, normalizes them into a valid DSL structure, and filters out contradictory candidates whose conditions make their actions impossible or redundant. The remaining candidates are then described and passed to an interaction-aware maximal marginal relevance (MMR) style selection step, where candidate relevance is computed as rel (𝑐 ) = 𝛼 Rel heur (𝑐, 𝑠 ) + ( 1 − 𝛼 ) Rel LLM (𝑐, 𝑠 ). 𝑐 denotes a candidate rule, 𝑠 denotes the current revision segment, Rel heur is a symbolic alignment score based on entity, predicate, and action match, Rel LLM is an LLM-estimated relevance score. Candidates are then greedily selected by maximizing 𝜆 rel (𝑐 ) − ( 1 − 𝜆 ) max 𝑐 ′ ∈𝑆 sim (𝑐, 𝑐 ′), where 𝑆 is the set of already selected candidates, sim is the similarity between candidate description embeddings.

5.2.2 Criteria Execution. In the automatic validation process, as described in sec 5.1.3, for each feedback message, ValiAssist will first evaluate it with the conditions in the criteria and execute the user-defined actions on the feedback if the evaluation results of corresponding conditions are matched (i.e., TRUE ) with respect to the code-feedback pair. During execution, exact-text and length predicates are evaluated programmatically, while semantic and stylistic predicates are evaluated with the LLM. However, due to the nondeterministic nature of LLM, the actions may not be conducted as described in the actions. To amend this issue, after the feedback has been revised by LLM accordingly, ValiAssist will again evaluate the revised feedback message with the conditions, and run actions of those violated rules again, repeating the above until either all conditions are evaluated to be FALSE , or the number of iterations has exceeded the threshold. 

5.3 Implementation 

ValiAssist was implemented as a web-based tool using React.js (frontend) and FastAPI(backend). We used OpenAI’s GPT-4o [1] for core LLM functionalities, text-embedding-3-small [2] for text-embeddings, and AgglomerativeClustering for embeddings clustering. We also adopted well-known prompting engineering techniques, such as few-shot prompts [8], AI chains [54], and persona settings [47]. Hallucination risks in automatic validation are reduced by the design of structural, DSL-based validation criteria, which mixes both code-based and LLM-based predicates and follows a strict structure and logic.

6 Technical Evaluation 

We compare our validation-by-demonstration pipeline with an existing approach that validates text documents with pure natural-language criteria in a programming feedback validation context: 

• Ours: We used the approach from our validation-by-demonstration pipeline for structured criteria inference from demonstration, and criteria execution. 

• NL: We adopted the approach and prompts from prior work for LLM-assisted evaluation [31], and refined it to fit our context. For both approaches, we used GPT-4o with a temperature of 0. 

6.1 Dataset 

Two authors who are experienced in both Python programming and programming education annotated two authentic coding exercise datasets to create the technical evaluation dataset. The annotation tool’s UI can be found in the Appendix B. We used the feedback generation approach in the probe system (Sec.3.2) to create feedback for each error submission and stored it for static reuse. Two annotators first independently review the LLM-generated feedback and revise the feedback with different methods (i.e., direct edit, prompt, and comment). Annotators were instructed to produce revisions that reflect diverse teaching strategies to lower the risk of biased data collection. To get authentic latent pedagogical intent of their revision, annotators were asked to clearly describe their intent in natural language after they finished a revision. This natural language description was then logged as the ground truth intent for the corresponding revision. Each intent description was cross-checked to ensure clarity. Each annotator records 10 revisions for each coding exercise dataset, producing 40 revision-intent pairs. After that, we sampled 6 code-feedback pairs for each revision-intent pair as a testing subset, producing 240 test cases in total. Each test case was then annotated to produce the ground truth for criteria matching (i.e., whether the corresponding intent could trigger a revision in the sampled code-feedback pair), by the original creator of this pedagogical intent for authenticity. To mitigate the influence of unbalanced ground truth for criteria matching, annotators manually made sure that there are both positive and negative (i.e., TRUE and FALSE ) test cases in each testing subsets.

In ValiAssist, before executing criteria to scale the validation process, the system has to suggest criteria based on users’ demonstration. As annotators’ intent description already serves as NL approach’s criteria, we only ran Ours to generate criteria by inputting context information and user edits from the dataset. For generated criterion, two annotators individually rated the criterion in regards of its clarity and correctness in a Likert-scale format (i.e., 1-5; higher is better). We conducted a within-subjects user study to evaluate ValiAssist’s effectiveness and efficiency in supporting instructors’ validation process of LLM-generated programming feedback in regards of criteria creation, application, and correction. The study specifically investigated the following sub-research questions: 

• SRQ1: Can validation-by-demonstration improve the effectiveness, efficiency, and user control during the validation process? 

• SRQ2: How useful is ValiAssist’s structured criteria design in helping user understand and improve their validation criteria?

9 Discussion 9.1 Validation by Demonstration as Progressive Specification 

Our findings suggest that validation by demonstration should not be understood as a one-shot translation from an instructor edit into a reusable rule. Instructors repeatedly applied inferred criteria, inspected their behavior on new submissions, and revised them before applying them again. In this sense, a demonstration served as a starting point for specification rather than a complete specification itself. This extends a conventional programming-by-demonstration framing in an important way. When the desired behavior depends on heterogeneous contextual evidence, the central challenge is not only inferring a program from an example, but helping users discover the boundaries of that program through its execution. For AI-assisted end-user programming systems, this suggests coupling local demonstrations with corpus-level inspection and repair: systems should expose where an inferred abstraction applies, surface counterexamples or unexpected matches, and allow users to progressively refine its scope. 

9.2 Guardrails in LLM-assisted Validation 

Our findings suggest that the key risk in LLM-assisted validation is not just inefficiency, but miscalibrated trust . In the baseline condition, some participants treated natural-language criteria and LLM judgments as reliable enough after a first pass, even when the validated feedback still violated their own standards: One participant (P2) continued validating in a largely “vibing” manner despite substantial misalignment, while another (P9) achieved very low validation accuracy yet still expressed strong faith in the approach, noting that “it would be very powerful if it could work.” This gap between actual performance and perceived promise is especially concerning in large-scale educational settings, where over-trust can allow low-quality or pedagogically misaligned feedback to propagate broadly. Our results suggest that ValiAssist offers two complementary guardrails against this risk: structured criteria serve as an explicit guardrail by making validation expectations inspectable and revisable, while validation-by-demonstration serves as an implicit guardrail by grounding criterion creation in instructors’ concrete revision work rather than abstract prompt authoring. Together, these mechanisms help keep instructors engaged in the validation process and support calibrated trust , so that LLM assistance scaffolds judgment rather than quietly becoming unchecked delegation.

9.3 Adaptive Criteria Creation Support Based on Context and Mental Model 

Although our lab study showed that ValiAssist significantly improved criteria creation, correction, and maintenance, 5 participants (P1, P5, P9, P11, P14) still preferred using pure natural-language descriptions for criteria creation in sub-task 1 as it gave them greater expressive freedom. P9 described their ideal workflow as “description along with demonstration.” This preference also echoes our formative study: participants with more experience giving programming feedback were less likely to start with demonstration, as they could often articulate a concrete criterion directly. Together, these findings suggest that criteria creation support should adapt to both task context and instructors’ mental models, rather than enforcing a single authoring strategy. Demonstration appears most useful when instructors are reacting to concrete failure cases or refining still-emerging intent, whereas natural-language description better supports cases where instructors already have a clear rule in mind. This suggests a broader design direction for mixed-initiative authoring: future systems should therefore support smooth transitions between description and demonstration as complementary entry points into criterion authoring. 

9.4 Limitations 

Our lab study design has several limitations. First, though we used authentic student programming data, our evaluation was conducted in a lab setting rather than in a genuine working and grading environment. The pressures regarding misleading real students’ learning progress were reduced in our setting, which could impact the attention instructors paid during feedback validation. Second, participants tested each condition of ValiAssist for a limited time (i.e., about 30 minutes). Though this study could reflect instructors’ behavior in short-term interactions, ValiAssist’s long-term impact (e.g., reusable validation criteria across assignments) could not be examined during our lab study. Lastly, although we demonstrated ValiAssist’s criteria-mediated scaling within several corpora (i.e., technical evaluation and user study), we did not run a deployment across a full term or a class of hundreds of students. Whether the same leverage holds when the criteria set grows over weeks, and when submissions arrive with error patterns the demonstrations did not cover, remains to be examined in an in-class deployment. 

10 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced ValiAssist, a validation-by-demonstration system for scaling instructors’ validation of LLM-generated programming feedback. Our study with 16 instructors showed that ValiAssist supported feedback better aligned with instructors’ own criteria and more accurate correction of problematic criteria than a natural-language baseline. Beyond these improvements, instructors’ repeated cycles of application, inspection, and refinement suggest that demonstrations served as starting points for progressive specification. By making inferred criteria and their execution inspectable, ValiAssist helped instructors clarify not only how feedback should change, but also when those changes should apply across student submissions. Together, these findings illustrate an approach to scaling expert judgment in which reusable logic extends the reach of individual decisions while remaining open to experts’ inspection and refinement.
