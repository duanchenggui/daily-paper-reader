# MARS: A Momentum-Aware Adaptive Rating System for Personalized Assessment 

Vikrant Sahu, Khethavath Sunil Naik, and Gagan Raj Gupta 

Department of Computer Science and Engineering , Indian Institute of Technology Bhilai , Chhattisgarh, India 

{vikrantsahu, khethavathn, gagan }@iitbhilai.ac.in 

Abstract —Adaptive assessment systems personalize learning by estimating learner proficiency and recommending suitable questions. However, many existing approaches rely on fixed diffi-culty labels, static rating updates, or black-box prediction models that provide limited control over rating stability, interpretability, and time-constrained routing. This paper proposes MARS, a Momentum-Aware Adaptive Rating System for interpretable adaptive assessment. MARS represents the question bank as a directed weighted knowledge graph and updates learner pro-ficiency using dynamic rating sensitivity, response quality, and momentum-aware learner velocity. We also formulate a time-budgeted mark-maximization objective in which the policy selects questions within a fixed test duration under negative marking. Experiments on ASSISTments 2009 and MARS-SimExam evalu-ate MARS for prediction, rating stability, and adaptive routing. Results show that MARS provides smoother rating updates than Fixed Elo and achieves the highest score under time-budgeted negative-marking routing, improving the total score by 8.09 over the strongest baseline and reducing overtime violations from 50% to 5.6%. These results suggest that MARS is suitable for real-time, interpretable, and deployment-friendly adaptive assessment. 

Index Terms —Adaptive assessment, educational data mining, Elo rating, momentum, knowledge tracing, graph-based recom-mendation, time-budgeted assessment, learner modeling. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# ADAPTIVE assessment is an important component of intelligent learning systems because it enables question difficulty, topic coverage, and feedback to be adjusted ac-cording to learner performance. In competitive examination preparation and online learning environments, learners differ in proficiency, response speed, consistency, topic-wise weakness, and recovery after mistakes. Therefore, an effective adaptive system should not only select the next question using recent accuracy or fixed difficulty labels, but also estimate evolving learner ability, support remediation, and provide interpretable recommendation logic. Traditional computerized adaptive testing methods, such as item response theory, provide a strong psychometric foun-dation but often depend on calibrated item banks and offer limited flexibility when question banks evolve over time [1]– [3]. Rating-based models provide a lightweight alternative by modeling learner–question interactions as a match between learner ability and item difficulty [4]–[6]. However, fixed rating updates can be unstable, particularly for new learners, and may overlook useful response-quality signals such as response time, hint usage, streak behavior, and topic structure. Knowledge tracing models, including Bayesian, deep, and graph-based variants, have shown strong performance in cor-rectness prediction, but they often provide limited transparency for adaptive routing and remediation decisions [7], [13], [22]. To address these limitations, this paper proposes MARS 

(Momentum-Aware Adaptive Rating System ), a graph-based adaptive assessment framework that models the question bank as a directed weighted knowledge graph with same-topic, prerequisite, and cross-topic relationships. MARS updates learner proficiency using dynamic rating sensitivity, response quality, and momentum-based learner velocity, enabling stable and responsive assessment. The framework supports adaptive question routing, weak-topic recovery, and interpretable re-mediation by selecting questions based on difficulty, learner momentum, graph structure, topic weakness, diversity, and centrality, while guiding learners through related easier con-cepts before revisiting unresolved topics. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:  

> •

Momentum-aware adaptive rating. We propose MARS, an interpretable online rating framework that extends Elo-style learner modeling with dynamic update sensitivity, response-quality scaling, and momentum-driven learner velocity to stabilize noisy learner-state updates.  

> •

Graph-guided remediation and routing. We design a question-graph-based routing policy that selects items using target difficulty, learner momentum, weak-topic recovery, diversity, and graph structure, enabling adaptive progression and contextual remediation.  

> •

Score-aware time-budgeted assessment. We formulate adaptive question selection as a mark-maximization prob-lem under fixed test duration, and evaluate MARS against rating-based and knowledge-tracing baselines for predic-tion, stability, and deployment-oriented routing. II. R ELATED WORK 

Computerized adaptive testing is commonly grounded in item response theory (IRT), where learner ability and item parameters are used to estimate the probability of a correct re-sponse [1]–[3]. Although IRT provides a strong psychometric foundation, it usually requires calibrated item banks and suffi-cient historical responses, which can be limiting when question banks evolve. Rating-based models provide a lightweight on-line alternative by treating learner–item interaction as a match between learner ability and item difficulty [4], [6]. However, fixed Elo-style updates may be unstable for new learners and arXiv:2610.21685v1 [cs.CY] 15 Oct 2026 may not account for response quality, topic weakness, reme-diation, or graph structure. Glicko-style extensions introduce uncertainty and volatility into rating estimation [5], but they are not designed for educational routing with response-time, hint, and graph-based signals. Knowledge tracing models estimate learner knowledge from sequential interaction data. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing mod-els mastery as a latent skill state updated using learning, guessing, and slipping parameters [7], [8], while classical factor-based, matrix-factorization, and logistic learner models use prior success and failure counts to estimate skill acquisi-tion [9], [10], [12], [42]. Deep Knowledge Tracing introduced recurrent neural networks for learner-response sequences [13], and later analyses and extensions examined deeper recurrent models, regularized sequence models, memory networks, at-tention, transformers, and graph neural networks to improve prediction [15]–[22], [26], [36], [43]. Survey work also shows that prediction gains alone do not solve interpretability and deployment constraints in adaptive assessment [27], [28]. These models can achieve strong next-response prediction, but their internal states are often less transparent for real-time routing, remediation, and score-aware item selection. Graph-based knowledge tracing and educational recom-mendation methods use relationships among skills, concepts, or questions to improve learner modeling and recommenda-tion [22]–[25]. Recent work further combines graph structure, semantic item representations, and attention mechanisms for knowledge tracing [29]–[35]. Response time and hint usage are also important indicators of response quality and can help distinguish mastery, guessing, and effortful failure [11], [14]. MARS combines these directions by maintaining explicit learner-state variables, including rating, velocity, streak, weak topics, answered items, served items, and remediation status. Unlike prediction-only knowledge tracing models, MARS uses graph structure, response quality, and momentum-aware rating updates not only for learner-state estimation but also for inter-pretable adaptive routing under time and scoring constraints. This section presents the proposed MARS framework. MARS extends rating-based adaptive assessment by integrat-ing dynamic learner uncertainty, multi-signal response qual-ity, graph-aware item calibration, momentum-inspired learner progression, rapid-response anomaly control, and contextual remediation. 

A. System Overview 

At each learner interaction, MARS receives the current learner state si,p , the attempted question qi,p , and the observed response ri,p . The system first computes the expected cor-rectness probability using the learner rating and the effective item rating. It then computes a response quality score using correctness, response time, hint usage, and streak quality. This response quality is compared with the expected probability to produce a raw rating update. The raw update is passed through the momentum velocity layer, which smooths noisy individual responses and updates the learner rating. Finally, the next question is selected from either a standard graph neighbourhood or a remediation neighbourhood. Figure 1 illustrates the MARS adaptive assessment pipeline. Learner responses, question-bank metadata, and knowledge-graph relations are used to update learner state through ex-pected success estimation, response-quality scoring, dynamic rating updates, and momentum-aware smoothing. The updated state then guides candidate generation and scoring using graph proximity, difficulty alignment, weak-topic recovery, diversity, and risk-aware utility. The selected question is delivered with an interpretable explanation, and the resulting response closes the feedback loop for subsequent routing and remediation.

This section describes the evaluation protocol used to assess MARS.The experiments are conducted on two datasets: AS-SISTments 2009 for online correctness prediction and rating stability, and MARS-S IM EXAM , a persona-based synthetic assessment dataset for time-budgeted routing. 

A. Datasets 

ASSISTments 2009 is used as the public benchmark for online correctness prediction and rating stability [39], [40], [44]. It contains learner–question interactions with correctness labels, skill tags, timestamps, hint information, and response-time information. Since public educational logs are obser-vational, they are suitable for prediction benchmarking but limited for evaluating counterfactual adaptive routing [41]. To evaluate adaptive question selection under realistic exam-ination constraints, we additionally use a synthetic assessment dataset generated using a Fixed-Elo-based persona simulator. B. Question Graph Construction 

For each dataset, a directed question graph is constructed using available skill and interaction information. Same-skill edges connect questions from the same knowledge component, while transition edges are created from consecutive learner attempts. Bridge edges connect related skill regions based on cross-topic transitions. Item difficulty is estimated from training interactions using smoothed correctness statistics or provided question Elo values where available. The graph supports centrality estimation and adaptive neighbourhood selection during routing.

For rating stability, we report mean absolute update (MAU) and update standard deviation (SDU), where lower values indicate smoother learner-rating trajecto-ries. A. Correctness Prediction Results 

Table II reports results on ASSISTments 2009. GKT achieves the strongest prediction performance, followed by DKT. This is expected because neural knowledge tracing models are optimized for sequential correctness prediction. MARS does not outperform neural models on this public observational benchmark, but it remains an interpretable online rating model with stable learner-state updates. Table III reports results on the synthetic dataset. In this setting, MARS achieves the highest AUC, while Fixed Elo gives slightly better accuracy, log loss, and Brier score. This shows that MARS is more competitive when the environment contains adaptive routing, learner momentum, and difficulty-aware interaction dynamics. These two results indicate that MARS should not be inter-preted as a replacement for neural knowledge tracing models in passive prediction benchmarks. Instead, MARS is better positioned as an online adaptive rating and routing framework for environments where interpretable learner-state updates, rating stability, and adaptive intervention are required. For rating stability, MARS reduced the mean absolute rating update from 9.47 to 4.95 and the update standard deviation from 13 .84 to 7.68 compared with Fixed Elo, corresponding to a 44 .5% reduction in update volatility. Fixed Elo is reported here as a reference rather than a competing baseline.

These gains show that MARS is useful not only for stable online rating, but also for practical adaptive assessment settings where the system must balance expected marks, risk, and test-duration constraints. When negative marking is in play, the gap between total marks and total score is what actually decides performance. Although DKT obtains slightly higher raw marks, its advan-tage does not translate into higher final score because it incurs substantially more overtime violations. Raw correctness, on its own, tells an incomplete story; any adaptive policy worth deploying in a competitive exam setting has to weigh risk and time discipline just as heavily. 

C. Adaptability and Deployment Suitability 

For application-oriented deployment, MARS is designed to support low-latency online updates, small per-learner memory, and interpretable routing decisions. Table V summarizes the deployment characteristics of the compared models. MARS updates learner state using only the current rating, velocity, recent accuracy, update variance, and item metadata. Therefore, it avoids full-sequence recomputation during infer-ence and is suitable for web-based practice platforms, LMS-integrated quizzes, and mobile adaptive assessment systems. 

D. Overall Discussion 

The results show that MARS is not intended to replace high-capacity knowledge tracing models for passive next-response prediction: on ASSISTments 2009, DKT and GKT achieve stronger predictive performance. Instead, MARS is better positioned as an online adaptive rating and routing framework. In the time-budgeted negative-marking setting, MARS achieves the highest total score and score efficiency, while DKT obtains slightly higher raw marks but much higher overtime violations. This indicates that fixed-duration adaptive assessment requires more than maximizing correctness or raw marks; the policy must jointly consider expected correctness, mark value, penalty risk, and available time. Overall, MARS is best suited for real-time, interpretable adaptive assessment where learner states must be updated online and routing decisions must remain auditable. VII. L IMITATIONS AND RESPONSIBLE USE 

MARS depends on the quality of the question graph, skill labels, and item-difficulty estimates; weak graph edges may degrade routing quality. ASSISTments 2009 is observational and cannot fully evaluate counterfactual routing, while the syn-thetic persona-based dataset cannot fully represent real learner behaviour. Since the synthetic dataset is generated using a Fixed-Elo-based simulator, the score-efficiency gains should be validated on real adaptive deployment logs. In preliminary routing experiments, a reward-only policy without negative marking tended to over-select high-mark, high-difficulty items, revealing an objective-misalignment risk; therefore, the final routing objective includes negative marking and time penalties to discourage risk-seeking item selection. The present time-budgeted comparison focuses on BKT, DKT, GKT, and MARS under a common routing objec-tive. Future work will compare MARS with uncertainty-aware Glicko-style knowledge tracing [5], Transformer-based knowledge tracing [21], and recent graph-aware knowledge-tracing models [30], [32], [33]. Response-time features may also be affected by device, network, reading-speed, or ac-cessibility differences. MARS should therefore be used as a decision-support tool with transparent recommendations, privacy-preserving data handling, bias auditing, and human oversight before high-stakes deployment [37], [38]. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper introduces MARS (Momentum-Aware Adap-tive Rating System), a personalized adaptive assessment framework that integrates learner-rating updates, response-aware proficiency estimation, graph-based question selection, momentum-driven learner-state stabilization, and interpretable recommendations. MARS is designed to provide transparent, adaptive, and real-time question selection for personalized learning environments. In a time-budgeted negative-marking setting, MARS achieves higher total score and score efficiency than the rout-ing baselines while substantially reducing overtime violations. These results position MARS as an interpretable, deployment-friendly adaptive routing framework rather than a replacement for neural knowledge tracing models in passive prediction benchmarks. To demonstrate the suitability of the proposed MARS framework for real-time deployment in large-scale adaptive assessment environments, we analyze the online time complex-ity of a single learner-item interaction step. The overall time complexity is determined by three primary operations: rating state updates, candidate generation, and candidate scoring. 

A. Learner State and Rating Update 

Upon receiving a learner’s response, MARS computes the effective item rating, expected correctness, response quality score, dynamic update factor, and momentum velocity. As defined in Equations (11) through (31), these operations con-sist strictly of scalar arithmetic, conditional checks, and basic mathematical functions (e.g., exponentials). Consequently, the time complexity for updating the learner state is constant: 

Oupdate = O(1) (46) 

B. Candidate Generation 

For standard progression, MARS generates a candidate set 

Cstd i,t by performing a breadth-first search (BFS) on the directed knowledge graph G = ( V, E, w ) starting from the current item qi,t , bounded by a maximum hop limit kh. Assuming an average node out-degree of d, the maximum number of items explored is bounded by dkh . The contextual remediation candidate generation follows a similar graph traversal logic. Thus, the time complexity for candidate generation is: 

Ogen = O(dkh ) (47) 

C. Candidate Scoring and Selection 

For each candidate q′ ∈ C i,t , MARS computes a composite utility score comprising heuristic fit, graph-structural impor-tance, and momentum alignment (Equation 35). Because struc-tural metrics such as in-degree, out-degree, and node centrality are precomputed offline, scoring each candidate requires O(1) 

operations. Scoring the entire generated candidate set scales linearly with the size of the set: 

Oscore = O(|C i,t |) ⊆ O (dkh ) (48) 

D. Overall Online Complexity 

The total time complexity per interaction step is the sum of the individual operations: 

Ototal = O(1) + O(dkh ) + O(dkh ) = O(dkh ) (49) Because the maximum hop limit kh is a small constant (e.g., 

kh ∈ { 1, 2, 3}), the online inference time scales independently of the total item bank size |V |. This sub-linear scaling confirms that MARS satisfies the stringent low-latency requirements of web-based and real-time adaptive learning platforms. E. Rating Stability 

On the ASSISTments dataset, MARS yielded substantially more stable learner-rating trajectories than Fixed Elo. The mean absolute rating update decreased from 9.47 to 4.95, and the update standard deviation decreased from 13.84 to 7.68, representing a 44.5% reduction in rating volatility. These results suggest that MARS provides smoother learner-state es-timation while preserving the interpretability and online update characteristics of rating-based adaptive assessment methods. 

F. Ablation Results 

Table VII presents the component-level ablation results. MARS-Final achieves a balanced trade-off between predic-tive performance and rating stability. Removing momentum slightly reduces AUC and accuracy while increasing update variability. Replacing dynamic K with fixed K produces the strongest AUC among MARS variants, but it also substantially increases update magnitude and volatility, making the learner trajectory less stable. The ablation results show three important findings. First, fixed K improves prediction but sacrifices stability, confirming that dynamic update sensitivity is useful when stable learner trajectories are desired. Second, the momentum term provides a modest but consistent improvement over the non-momentum variant. Third, the streak multiplier substantially reduces AUC and increases log loss, indicating that streak should not directly multiply the rating update. METRIC INTERPRETATION 

AUC measures how well a model ranks correct responses above incorrect responses and therefore reflects discrimination independent of a fixed threshold. Accuracy measures the proportion of correct binary predictions. Log loss penalizes confident but incorrect predictions more heavily and is useful for evaluating the quality of predicted probabilities. Brier score measures the average squared difference between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, with lower values indi-cating better calibration. For rating stability, MAU represents the average size of rating updates after each interaction, whereas SDU measures the variability of those updates; lower values indicate smoother learner-state trajectories and lower sensitivity to noisy responses. In the time-budgeted routing setting, Score represents the final reward after penalties, Marks represents the raw marks obtained before penalties, Score/min measures time efficiency, and Overtime indicates how often a policy exceeds the allotted test duration from the dataset. It is accounted identically for every policy: item durations come from one median response-time model fitted on the training split, and an item is admitted only while elapsed time is below 

T , so a session can overshoot only on its final item. Overtime differences therefore reflect item selection, not accounting. HYPERPARAMETER SELECTION 

Sessions were split by test into disjoint train, validation and final sets in a 70 /15 /15 ratio. The routing weights in Table VI were tuned on the validation split only, using 50 

trials of Tree-structured Parzen Estimator search that maximise Score + 0 .5 Score/min − 5 Overtime − 0.01 tover , where tover is mean overtime in seconds. The selected weights were then frozen and evaluated once on the final split, and the same weights are used for every routing policy. CODE AVAILABILITY
